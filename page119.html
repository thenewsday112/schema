Your next job doesn’t have a title yet: Emerging role skills Q&A with Cory Hymel of Andela

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

While the whispers say that everyone is becoming a generalist, that's not where headcount is going.

There’s been plenty of chatter about AI’s effect on jobs. Will everyone get laid off to train AI? Will every developer become a super generalist? Are there new roles rising from the ashes? But Cory Hymel, head of research at Andela, wanted numbers behind that chatter. He and his team looked at nearly 50,000 job postings to find the skills and roles that are emerging before the industry has properly given them titles. I spoke with him to find out what that research says about the new developer skillsets that land permanent roles.

Q: You did this research on how skills shift and change in job titles. Could you give an intro to this to the project?

A: At Andela, our general approach and methodology to the market is that job titles and resumes are the best worst thing that we have out in the world right now. We're obsessed with understanding very fine-grained skills of what people are able to do.

That's premise number one. Premise number two was that we hear very commonly "Oh, AI is changing jobs. Oh, AI's gonna get rid of jobs," or, "AI is, gonna create new jobs." But it's all very anecdotal and not very data-driven. We looked at it and said, "Is there a way that we can actually go in and put some numbers and some rigor to actually understand what's going on behind the curtains with all of these jobs?" We've got a internal skill taxonomy and ontology that we use that grounds all of this and is the base of the research and data structure that goes in.

But for us, it's really important because the other big side of our business is around learning and upskilling. So how can we provide our customers information around where the industry is heading. Here's some skills that people should be learning that we're seeing become more common. These are the roles. Some of these might fall in between two.

While you might say, "Front-end engineers need to go learn this," actually you probably should pull in product managers as well.I think job titles have always lagged the work, but especially now it's getting worse and worse.

Q: So it's not necessarily about job titles changing, but more about new skills being applied to existing job titles and mid-range roles appearing. I think you talked about DevSecOps as an example of a bleed role.

A: Exactly. What I'm seeing in the news pretty frequently is that this pervasive narrative that AI's gonna make everyone generalist, and it's just turning these jobs into gray jobs where skills can bleed all over.

And what the data shows is that isn't true. There are very niche skill bundles that are either forming as these net new roles that no one's talked about yet or the underlying skills within historical roles are starting to change.

From the labor market or the hiring side, it's, one, happening so quick that people can't keep up with it. Or two enterprises don't really know what skills people should be learning, so they just give up this generalist title of AI engineer. It's like, what what does that mean? There's 100 different flavors of what that could be. But for them it's just easier to say "Oh, we need someone that does AI."

Q: At re:Invent last year, Amazon's CTO, Werner Vogels, talked about the T-shaped developer, where you have a deep focus but a broad base of understanding. Does your data reflect that? Or are you seeing a more specific concentration of these skill bundles?

A: I've gotten that question a few times, and transparently, I've not followed up with it as much as I probably should have. Because you have the T shape and you have the I shape, and I think there's a star shape. I've gotten questions about a few different ones, but from what we're seeing, I think that probably the T shape does typically follow the pattern in the data. But I don't have a definitive answer on that yet.

Q: That's something I'd love to know because, anecdotally, I hear a lot of people talking about this broadening of what a fullstack developer is that's covering product design to SRE. I wonder if people are actually hiring roles for that or if that's just something that happens within organizations.

A: I don't know. We didn't see in the data this idea of a net generalist. But what we are seeing is that if you look at the typical flow of the software development life cycle, if you put roles down in some linear fashion of product manager and then front-end engineer, back end engineer, DevOps, and then QA, the bleed in between those roles are where we're starting to see the most common mix in skills.

So for instance: the front end product engineer. This might be like a like an F-shaped engineer. Meaning that like they lean to one side—they might have a background in front-end engineering, but now they're being asked to stretch into a product role to talk to customers to get feedback, to prioritize and backlog features, and so on.

We're seeing the same thing on the back-end and the DevOps side of back-end engineers now needing to know about deploying these models into production and think about scalability and stuff—that F goes in the other direction.

I need to think about my shapes a little more for this.

Q:That shape is interesting, though. The F shape one— with AI, now that you're able to touch the things that are closest to you, you can branch out, right? Back end engineers can start touching the database and stuff like that.

A: Even to some degree like merging over into prod, even into the front end side too.

Q:What are some of the the skill bundles that you saw in the research?

A: One of the ones our customers often describe but never label was this idea of an LLM application engineer. This is someone that's very versed in building products on top LLMs, whether it's OpenAI APIs or open weight models or Claude code. They know how to plug into the APIs, but they can also take the step beyond that, meaning that they know how to construct the harnesses.

They know and are familiar with things like evals, which when we talk to customers, they think about it as just a backend engineer. That's a very specific skill set. Your classical back-end engineer will not necessarily know about building agent eval harnesses or governance and guardrails. That one was super interesting and also showed up pretty prevalent in the data.

Another one that I've heard it a few times now, is that 2026 and 2027 is gonna be the year of the CFO. How to deal with consumption spending. Now you have new products that are all on these consumption models, which pass-through products. Not only that, but your developers are consuming these new kind of OpEx spends. A FinOps reliability engineer, someone that's able to think about project development and factoring the cost—not just of typical end-user consumption, like cloud consumption—but also developer tooling. When we're building our AI-native SDLC, people talk about financially responsible ways to do this. We hear a lot about how do we optimize our token spend and how do we reduce our token spend. An engineering role that's very keen to understand costs, like in these cases, we use cheaper or faster models with simpler processes, or we design these to have where you might need Fable or something else because it is highly complex,

The last one was this docs-as-code engineer.

We hear a lot about spec-driven development. It’s treated like something that should be pushed down directly on top of developers, when in reality, there is this new role where people that were doing classical technical writing or classical documentation are some of the best spec-driven engineers. They're the ones that construct the context better than developers can to feed into these new models, as well as set the context for long-term agentic development on these bigger code bases.

Q: The AI application engineer is an interesting one—I hear a lot from people that everybody should be building their own software factory. It seems like this is the thing you're expected to do as an individual engineer, no matter what, because everybody's going through an agent, everybody's doing evals on their own code. It's interesting to see that these skills are specialized in the data.

A: I think that's the big I think insight with all of this. It challenges the narrative and the status quo of, either one, everyone's just gonna be the expanded fullstack developer, or two, everyone's just a generalist and you can just do everything now. In reality, we are seeing statistically significant trends of specific skill groupings that are coming out in hiring and job data.

Q: Did you notice anything about job titles? Do job titles matter for these skill groupings?

A: In my opinion, job titles are important, especially in this context, because it gives enterprises a way to better describe the work that needs to get done. When you look at an enterprise and how a job gets created, specifically when it comes down to the job title, you've got the individual that's closest to the work. A lot of times they might be someone within the team.

But there's layers on top of it, right? It's gotta go to HR, and then to finance, and then procurement, and then eventually a recruiter. You get this chain of custody, and the work gets more ambiguous as it goes down that chain of custody, not only in the description, but the understanding of what the role should be. What I've seen is that the fallback to a generalist role becomes easier.

I hope that with these more specific job titles, from the person that's closest to the work all the way to the edge recruiter, they're able to accurately describe exactly what needs to get done. A better title for that is really important. Because one, it allows the hiring firm to be more specific, it reduces their time, they get better candidates. It puts them in a better place to succeed.

From the candidate side, imagine I've been a back-end engineer for the past 20 years and I now see a back-end engineering role. I'm just hitting apply because I just need to get a job, right? The odds of me ending up in a position where I won’t be successful is relatively high because the underlying skills and requirements and understanding could be vastly different.

I do think in this case it is it is important for these new titles to come along.

Q: In the research post you talked about talent debt. What is talent debt?

A: Talent debt accrues the same way that technical debt does. But the difference with talent debt is that the skill half-life is shrinking substantially. Back in the day, you could go and get a job as a Java engineer and work for 20 years doing Java programming and be okay. But now what we're seeing is that the pace of change is going so fast that the half-life of how long those skills stay relevant is shrinking substantially.

What you get is this skill compression, which then forms into talent debt. A lot of companies are experiencing it now where all of a sudden this race into AI, they look at what they have on the bench and say, "Oh my goodness, we've got nothing even close to what we have to do here."

They have two options then. One is either try to reskill who you have already. Upsides are that you get to keep the strong domain knowledge of the individuals within your team, understand strong system knowledge, and it's good to invest in your people.

The flip side is to try to hire it out. It's faster, but you don't have the domain knowledge, you have ramp time, and you have the the risk of putting the wrong butt in the wrong seat. All of that compounds into this concept of talent debt, which is very similar to technical debt.

Q: Developers have always had to keep learning on the job, right? Even if you're a Java engineer for 20 years, you're learning new frameworks, new features come out, new libraries. Is this acceleration a different sort of thing, where it's not just learning new things for Java, but learning a whole new thing every six months?

A: I think so. It's one thing to pick up a new framework, right? It's one thing to pick up a new programming language. I think a lot of developers are fine with that. The big difference now is that it's a fundamental bedrock shift of the way that you actually work.

That is the big, hard transition. Changing from procedural to object-oriented programming makes you really think structurally about how these things get built, what are ways to optimize and go faster. For a lot of people, that's a stiff change. If you've ever tried to switch between the two, doing C or something procedural into an object-oriented framework, you conceptually think about things much differently.

It's thinking conceptually differently, but at a much bigger scale of how you work as a person. For an engineer, it's going beyond and having to relearn syntax or language or frameworks or methodologies, but into a structural re-understanding how you put all of these new tools to work. How do I actually step back from the code, and how do I adjust in my mind what accountability is and give that to a virtual synthetic knowledge worker.

The engineers that are able to move in between it are ones that have substantial advantage. There’s an engineer working with me from our network making some updates to one of our platforms. He gave me a quote on the time because it was adding some leaderboards in, and I said, "We just need to add a leaderboard. You go give it to Claude Code. This should be an hour."

He was looking at it over four days, so I asked him, "Why does it take so long? Like coding tools are you using?" He uses Claude Code to understand it, but still hand codes everything to a large degree. He doesn't practice agentic development, mostly because he feels accountable for the code that he ships. “If something that I ship is wrong, then that's on me."

I have another developer that just gives it to Claude. If it works, if it passes the unit test and the acceptance test and the smoke test, then ship it to prod. Who cares.

It's interesting to see these different dynamics, but the other underlying one was that the developer that was very hesitant about using it has been using the tools for a while, but has a very small token budget. I think that there's like this fluency gap between those that can, developers that can afford to like fully embrace this new world and have the budget to burn the tokens to get the comfortability with it versus a large group of people that don't. That might be another area of study.

Q: You’re talking about scaling up and becoming comfortable with the tools, but I've heard a lot about AI affecting developer skills, de-skilling, growing lazier with cognitive offloading, people knowing less about the code., Like you said with the the one that's in YOLO mode—if it works, who cares how it works? Do you think AI will make the rescaling and the scaling up harder or easier?

A: I believe there's a lot of different answers to it. On one degree, AI will make upskilling a lot easier, right? You have a personalized tutor. It can explain things to you. I've seen good engineers ask Claude to quiz them on the code so that they get a firm understanding.

Is there gonna be skill degradation? For sure. I think that's one of the premises of any new technology that comes out. Gartner had this skill atrophy onion where you have your core skills that make you who you are. You've got skills that are unique to your domain that are not usually replaceable. Then you have this middle onion skill, which are these skills that are okay to atrophy. For a developer, that might be things like understanding the syntax. I don't necessarily need to know Go to write a Go program anymore. Is that an okay thing to let go? There are some skills in there, maybe general architectural setup that still trigger the sniff test of this doesn't seem just right.

Engineers that have been around for a while that are okay with letting some of these skills go and gaining these new ones are gonna be in extremely good position to accelerate forward. The people that I have concern about are individuals coming into the market now that haven't had time to develop that innate sniff test of when things are going awry and just blindly YOLO stuff. Upskilling and reskilling and skill atrophy or degradation is gonna be a problem in five, ten years when that gap forms.

Or maybe who cares? We weren't allowed to use calculators in school. Now everyone's got calculators, and we turned out fine.

Q: Nobody's coding in Assembly anymore, right? Doesn't matter.

Q: For folks who are a little antsy about the future of their jobs and their skills, does this data validate those fears?

A: I think there is a lot of noise about AI making everyone general. There is a lot of AI noise around AI changing jobs. But what we found is that there are new specific skill sets coming out becoming extremely important. People should understand where they fit. We've got another tool where you can put in your current role and we can show you the new role that it's morphing into.

Understanding where the trends are going and where, as an engineer, you can start to invest effort to prepare yourself will be really helpful for everyone.

The United Kingdom has issued economic sanctions against Cryptomus, a chameleonic cryptocurrency platform that experts say serves as a major conduit for payments related to ransomware, narcotics, darknet markets, fraud and cybercrime services that are helping Russia circumvent existing financial restrictions.

Heleket’s support agent on Telegram claims, despite all evidence to the contrary, that it is not affiliated with Cryptomus. Image: Richard Sanders.

On October 8, the U.K. issued sanctions (PDF) against Cryptomus — previously known as Certa Payments and Xeltox Enterprises — for processing payments that benefit the Russian government and its financial institutions. The sanctions freeze any Cryptomus assets at U.K. banks, and bar British companies from offering it any business, virtual or otherwise. Also targeted in these sanctions were the virtual payment platforms VexPay, TokenSpot, and the Tsunami Payments service.

As explained in our December 2024 investigation, “How Cryptocurrency Turns to Cash in Russian Banks,” Cryptomus has been a key payment processor for dozens of Russian cryptocurrency exchanges and websites hawking cybercrime services. The company has changed its name several times since emerging in 2022, most recently to “Heleket” last year after Canada levied a record CAD $176 million fine against the platform for anti-money laundering violations.

The blockchain data-crunching firm Chainalysis said the U.K. sanctions target the broader infrastructure supporting Russia’s wartime economy.

“Cryptomus and Heleket have emerged as major payment hubs for illicit activity,” Chainalysis wrote in an October 8 blog post. “Together, these services received more illicit funds across tracked categories than the mixers in our dataset and the number of illicit counterparties surged to more than 900 in a single month in late 2025.”

The Cryptomus support bot on Telegram, responding on Oct. 8 to an inquiry by saying the platform was suddenly unable to process transactions.

TRM noted that Cryptomus’s transaction volume cratered after the platform instituted new “know your customer” or KYC rules, and that the cryptocurrency platform responded to the Canadian fines by launching Heleket as a parallel service that continued to offer no-KYC payment processing.

Richard Sanders is a blockchain analyst and investigator who spent months mapping out Cryptomus’s payment infrastructure and transacting with its customers. Sanders said the U.K. sanctions on Cryptomus are important because Canada’s fine against the platform was ultimately unenforceable and uncollectible.

“A fine doesn’t stop a cryptocurrency payment processor from processing payments,” Sanders wrote on LinkedIn. “Sanctions kill the operation. The distinction matters.”

As our 2024 investigation noted, Cryptomus shared a Vancouver address with dozens of other foreign currency dealers and money transfer businesses — none of which appeared to be physically located there. Following the CAD $176 million fine for alleged money laundering violations, Cryptomus appealed the penalty, launched Heleket as a parallel service, and kept on processing large volumes of sketchy transactions, Sanders said.

Sanders said the sanctions also are notable because they are the first known instance of a “Five Eyes” country (referring to the Australia, Canada, New Zealand, the U.K. and the U.S.) sanctioning a cryptocurrency operation incorporated in another Five Eyes jurisdiction.

Each country carries thousands of pages of specifications and roughly 1,000 scenarios that must be met before anything reaches production.

Some of those terminals have to keep working offline for up to 40 days, by regulation, because in the country they operate in, a payment terminal is critical infrastructure on the same level as a gas station or a pharmacy. That’s the environment the type system has to hold up in. Not a demo. Production, worldwide, for decades, not a year.

If a specification lists every case explicitly and never says “otherwise,” then an else in the code is inventing a case the spec doesn’t describe. His team had handled only full refunds. When partial refunds became a real scenario, the old else branch would have absorbed it silently: compiles fine, tests pass, and it’s wrong in production. The business drifts constantly quarterly and monthly in payment in one country, and for the whole EMEA region – it becomes daily at Yuri’s scale. An exhaustive when turns every drift into a list of exactly what needs to change, rather than a bug report from a customer who was charged the wrong amount.

Yuri’s Black Friday example: authorization, risk management, and fraud checks used to run as three independent calls, each in its own try/catch. If fraud management ran slowly under load, the transaction could still be approved while the fraud check silently failed, and stolen cards could start getting approved. Kotlin gives two structured concurrency scopes that look similar and behave completely differently, and the spec, not convenience, should decide which one you use.

Here, coroutineScope fails fast: if the fraud check fails, authorization and risk are canceled as well, and nothing is silently approved. A supervisorScope would let siblings keep running independently, which is the right call for something like fan-out notifications, where one failure shouldn’t cancel the rest. The question isn’t which scope is more idiomatic. It’s what the spec says should happen when one branch fails.

A gateway call that times out isn’t necessarily a failure. The acquirer may have already processed it; the client just didn’t hear back in time. Catching that as a generic exception and moving on erases the difference between “nothing happened” and “something happened, we’re just not sure what”. Yuri’s fix models a Timeout as a first-class outcome in the domain.

TimedOut isn’t the absence of an answer. It’s a distinct state that routes to a reconciliation query rather than a retry, because a naive retry after a timeout that actually succeeded upstream is exactly how a customer ends up charged twice.

Yuri’s least expected pattern targets certification tests, not application code. A typical certification suite runs a thousand tests at roughly 200 lines each, and, in his words, not even an architect can read that. The team rewrote the tests as Kotlin DSL. Written that way, a certification specialist who has never written Kotlin can read the test, follow the logic against the specification, and sign off on it directly. The test stops being an artifact only test engineers can verify and becomes a specification the business and certification specialists can review.

Yuri is specific about the outcomes: development ran 2.7 times faster, and certification passed with only one defect and on the first attempt, unusual enough that regulators asked what had changed. The resulting codebase was about 30% smaller. Code review shifted from hunting for missed state checks to comparing code against the specification sentence by sentence, because the compiler now catches the class of bug reviewers used to look for by hand. Onboarding a new engineer used to take two or three weeks before they understood enough of a 5,000-page specification and a legacy codebase to ship anything. Afterward, new hires opened a production pull request within days. Engineers could also move between device teams and backend teams, something a split codebase in two different languages had made difficult. The last effect Yuri called out was that engineers started speaking the business’s language well enough to talk directly with product managers, sales, and regulators, occasionally proposing changes to the regulations themselves.

Yuri is direct about the mistake most teams make: treating migration as one project, a rewrite in Kotlin. His systems run 25-year-old Java and C++ code processing live transactions every second, with no acceptable downtime, so a rewrite was never on the table. Framed as a refactor-vs-rewrite decision, the answer for a live payments platform is almost always refactor. His alternative is four tracks that run in parallel, not in sequence.

Running all four at once, according to Yuri, is what makes a payments modernization effort safe and cost-effective in a regulated environment. Running them in sequence is what makes migrations stall.

Getting the migration approved is not the same as its adoption. Without an operating model for how the migration actually runs day-to-day, Yuri was blunt: there’s no happy ending waiting at the end of it.

He pointed to a resource for that exact gap: a 22-page playbook on the Kotlin site, published two days before this talk, covering, in his words, “the part that everybody skips”.

To avoid paying this setup cost on every request, HTTP clients and servers try to reuse existing connections across multiple requests when possible. The mechanics of this differ based on the HTTP version:

In HTTP/1 (both HTTP/1.0 and HTTP/1.1), multiple requests and responses on the same connection are sent sequentially on the wire. If two GET requests are sent to index.golang.org over the same TCP connection, the responses arrive as a single stream of bytes:

As shown in the diagram, net/http.Get reads and parses the status line and headers from the byte stream to populate net/http.Response, then returns immediately. The response body remains unread on the connection and is consumed only as the caller reads from net/http.Response.Body.

Because responses are sent back-to-back on the same stream, net/http can only find the start of the next response after the current response body has been read to the very end (as determined by the Content-Length or Transfer-Encoding header).

Through Go 1.26, when resp.Body.Close is called, net/http sees that there is still an unread response body. Realizing that it cannot reuse the connection, it abandons the existing connection and creates new connections for its next requests, causing it to repeatedly incur the cost of establishing new connections.

(HTTP/2 does not have this problem. Because it multiplexes streams of frames over a single connection rather than sending responses strictly back-to-back, an unread response body on one stream does not block other requests from sharing the connection.)

This subtlety of HTTP/1 connection reuse can cause silent performance degradation in seemingly innocuous code.

As in our opening example, a client might only care about an API response’s status code. This will unfortunately cause connection reuse to fail.

The connection cannot be reused for a new request until that newline is consumed. Thus, a healthy connection might end up being abandoned, even though it was only a single byte away from being reusable.

Why Your Sandbox Never Quite Matches Production (And Why That's Fine)

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sandbox testing works best when you stop chasing production parity. Learn what environment drift actually costs and which parts are worth fixing.

The sandbox has never matched production. Not yours, not anyone's. A configuration variable set differently. A database seed that skips the legacy record formats real users actually have. An upstream service that deployed a new response schema last Thursday and nobody updated the simulation. These are not edge cases - they show up in every environment, on every team, at every scale. The gap is structural. It comes with the territory. What changes when you accept that is where you put the maintenance work.

Drift accumulates because production and the sandbox change on different schedules, with different triggers, and nobody coordinates the two deliberately enough.

Production evolves constantly. Application deployments, infrastructure updates, upstream service API changes, database schema migrations, third-party integration updates - each of these moves production forward in some dimension. The sandbox catches some of these changes when someone explicitly updates it. It misses the ones that happen without triggering an update.

Teams that blame the environment for drift are usually blaming the wrong thing. The environment drifted because nobody updated it. A sandbox does not track production automatically - someone has to make it track production, deliberately, on a schedule tied to something real. Without that, drift is the default outcome.

The better question is not "how do we eliminate the gap" but "which parts of the gap actually cause test failures to miss real production failures." That answer is narrower than most teams assume.

Not all drift is equal. The sandbox can diverge from production in ways that never cause a test to miss a real failure, and in ways that guarantee it will.

Infrastructure divergence - different instance types, different memory limits, different networking rules - looks significant on paper. Smaller machines, different subnets, scaled-down replicas. In practice, almost none of this changes what an integration test catches. The test is validating how the application handles a payment service response. Whether that test runs on a t3.medium or a production-grade instance does not change whether the response handling is correct.

Behavioral contract drift is the category that actually breaks things. When the order service's simulated payment dependency returns a response structure that no longer matches what the real payment service returns, the tests validate the application against an assumption that production will contradict. The test passes. The integration fails. The sandbox did not fail at its job - the simulation was wrong, and nobody noticed it had become wrong.

Infrastructure parity is satisfying to pursue because progress is measurable. You can diff the configurations, close the gaps, and declare the environments equivalent. Behavioral contract accuracy is harder because it requires ongoing knowledge of what the real upstream service returns right now - not what it returned when the simulation was authored, not what the documentation says it returns, but what the deployed version actually produces today.

When a team first builds a sandbox, the simulated dependencies are accurate. Someone studied what the upstream service actually returns, built a simulation that reflected those observations, and committed it. At that moment, the simulation is correct.

Then the upstream service keeps deploying.

Nobody tells you when the payment service ships a schema change. No notification arrives when a previously optional field becomes required, or when an error format gets restructured, or when a new field appears in the response that your application now needs to handle. The simulation you built against the old version keeps running. The tests keep passing. You deploy. Production encounters the new format on the exact code paths that depend on the fields that changed.

The suite ran clean. Green across the board. And it was wrong - not flaky-wrong, not intermittently-wrong, but systematically wrong in a way that looked identical to correct. That is the more expensive failure mode. A suite that reliably validates against stale assumptions does not look broken. It looks trustworthy right up until production contradicts it.

The teams that manage this best are not the ones that build more accurate simulations initially. They are the ones that connect simulation updates to upstream deployment events rather than to test failures. The trigger determines everything. "Update simulations when tests fail" means the sandbox is wrong from the moment of each upstream deployment until the next failure surfaces the drift - often in production. "Update simulations when upstream services deploy" means the drift window is measured in hours rather than weeks.

Observation-based approaches to generating simulations help here. Tools that capture what upstream services actually return during interactions with staging environments - rather than requiring developers to author what they believe the service returns - start from observed reality rather than from documentation that may have drifted. Keploy, for instance, captures real HTTP traffic at the kernel level through eBPF, which means the simulation reflects what the service actually returned during the recording session rather than what a developer thought it returned. The diff between a previous simulation and an updated one surfaces exactly which fields changed in the upstream response, which is the information the downstream team needs to assess whether application code requires updating alongside the simulation refresh.

The mechanism matters less than the trigger. Simulations updated on upstream deployment events stay accurate. Simulations updated after test failures stay stale.

Full production parity is not the goal. Targeted accuracy for the specific behaviors that integration tests are designed to validate is the goal.

The sandbox does not need to replicate production's data volume. It does not need to replicate production's traffic patterns. It does not need to run at production's infrastructure scale. These forms of parity are expensive to maintain and rarely determine whether a test catches a real failure.

What the sandbox needs to do is accurately represent the behavioral contracts between the services being tested - the request and response structures, the error conditions, the state transitions - with enough fidelity that a test failure in the sandbox indicates a real failure that would have occurred in production, and a passing test reflects an integration that will hold.

For sandbox testing to deliver on this, the team needs to be explicit about two things. Which behavioral contracts the sandbox is representing. And how those contracts stay current as upstream services keep evolving.

The first is a design question answered at setup time. The second is a maintenance question that does not have a setup-time answer.

Spent time on this long enough to have a clear opinion: the teams that get burned are not running bad sandboxes. They are running sandboxes that were accurate once and nobody updated them when upstream services changed.

Get the simulations current. Make sure the test data includes the record shapes that actually exist in production, not just the clean ones from initial setup. That combination catches the integration failures worth catching -- the ones that do not require production to surface them.

It does not catch every failure class. It catches the one that matters most in distributed systems: the assumption about how a dependency behaves that the dependency has since stopped honoring.

The rest of what production reveals - emergent behavior under real load, edge cases in actual user data, failure modes that require realistic traffic patterns to surface - those require production observation to understand. The goal of sandbox testing is not to eliminate that class of learning. It is to eliminate the failures that do not require production to reveal them, cheaply and early.

That is the frame that makes the gap between sandbox and production legible. Not as a problem to solve. As a boundary to understand. The sandbox covers everything inside it. Production reveals what the sandbox structurally cannot cover. Both are doing their jobs. The maintenance work is keeping the sandbox's coverage accurate within its boundary, not expanding the boundary to match production exactly.

Environment drift is not the enemy. Undetected drift in the behavioral contracts that integration tests depend on - going unnoticed until it produces a production failure - is the specific failure mode worth preventing. The distinction is worth making before investing in sandbox infrastructure that aims for parity it can never achieve.

Declarative Schemas 2.0 with pg-delta

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

On October 2 at Supabase Select we shipped Declarative Schemas 2.0. The Supabase CLI now generates migrations with pg-delta, a Postgres diff tool we wrote and open sourced, in place of migra, the technology behind our original implementation. Generated migrations now include RLS policy changes, grants, view settings, partitions, domains, and objects that extensions manage.

Declarative schemas first launched in April 2025. You keep your schema in SQL files under supabase/schemas/, edit them, and the CLI writes the migration. In 2.0 that workflow is the same: edit a file, generate the migration, review it, and apply it. New CLI projects get 2.0 by default. Existing projects opt in with one config block.

In this post we cover why we built pg-delta, how to turn it on, a working example, and what it does not do yet.

migra is an open source Python tool that diffs two Postgres databases and emits the SQL between them. It has powered supabase db diff since the first CLI prototype in 2021.

migra didn't understand domains, publications, or comments. Changes to them never appeared in the diff. You read every generated migration line by line to find what was missing. The limitations list in our docs kept growing and the answer for each entry was the same, write a versioned migration by hand.

Coverage was only half the problem. Supabase manages part of every project's schema. The auth schema belongs to the Auth service and should never appear in your diff. A trigger on auth.users that calls a function in public to create a profile row is yours and has to appear. The same split runs through roles, grants, and extension-owned objects. migra could only filter at the schema level so it had no way to keep auth out while keeping that trigger in. We needed an engine that decides per object what belongs to you and what belongs to Supabase.

We felt these gaps ourselves. We use migra on our own middleware database, and our repository carries dozens of migrations with hand-written patches for the grants and privileges it didn't track. Extending it further meant fighting its design. So we wrote our own engine from scratch and open sourced it.

The CLI starts two throwaway Postgres databases. It loads your schema files into one and replays your migration history into the other. pg-delta then compares the two by reading the system catalog, and orders the output using a dependency graph built from that catalog. You no longer hand-order files in schema_paths.

Everything migra's docs listed as unsupported is now in the diff. It also covers partitions, publications, subscriptions, and foreign tables.

Objects that an extension owns are changed through the extension's own API. pg-delta never emits create table or drop table for pg_partman partitions or pgmq queue tables. It emits calls like select pgmq.drop_queue('q');, and the CLI flags them as destructive.

Unsupported objects produce a warning. Pass --strict-coverage to turn those warnings into errors so CI fails.

The output is a plain SQL file in supabase/migrations/. Hand-written migrations keep working. Once pg-delta is on, db diff and db pull use it too.

The first engine powered branch merge diffs in the dashboard from February 2026 and the CLI from March, went out as a public alpha in April, and stayed in production until August. It worked. A few months of production traffic showed us where its design would stop scaling. Its knowledge of Postgres lived in several places at once. A parser inferred types from SQL text while a separate layer read the catalog. Whenever the two disagreed, the diff was wrong. Every new thing we taught the engine was another chance for them to disagree.

In June we started over with one rule: Postgres is the only thing that understands Postgres. The new engine never parses SQL. Because it only reads what a real Postgres built, the CLI loads your files into a shadow database rather than parsing them itself. Within a month of shipping in August as alpha.34 of the same @supabase/pg-delta package, the rewrite had replaced the first engine everywhere we use it. Dashboard branching computes merge diffs with it. Realtime exports its tenant schema with it and uses that snapshot to detect drift in production.

pg-delta generates schema changes rather than data changes. It creates tables, policies, and functions. It never writes an insert, update, or delete for the rows in your tables.

There is one exception. Some extensions store their configuration as rows. A pg_cron job, for example, is a row in cron.job. pg-delta treats those rows as part of your schema. A queue is a pair of tables that pgmq.create builds for you, plus a row in pgmq.meta. pg-delta never emits create table or drop table for those tables. It emits pgmq.create and pgmq.drop_queue instead and the CLI flags the drop as destructive.

Storage buckets are not included today. A bucket is a row in storage.buckets. pg-delta does not diff its rows. A bucket you create in the dashboard will not show up in your schema files. Until that changes, create buckets in a seed file or a hand-written migration instead. The RLS policies on storage.objects are a different story. They are schema and are diffed.

Today, we’re announcing AWS Lambda Web Functions, a new form factor that lets you run standard HTTP web applications on Lambda without rewriting your code into an event handler. You can take a standard port-listening server built with Express, Hono, Fastify, Next.js, or any Node.js framework, deploy it with a single command, and Lambda automatically handles scaling, multi-region distribution, and active CPU billing. No adapters, no infrastructure to manage, and no framework lock-in.

With this launch, AWS Lambda now offers three distinct form factors, each built on the same Firecracker isolation technology and designed for a different programming model.

Lambda Event Functions are what you already know as Lambda functions. They use the event-driven, single-concurrent handler model with wall-clock billing. We’re introducing the “Event Functions” name to highlight that programming model: your function receives an event, processes it, and returns a response. The event-driven programming model is ideal for event processing, queue consumers, and invocations triggered by other AWS services.

Lambda Web Functions use an HTTP-native programming model with multi-concurrent execution and active CPU billing. Your code listens on a port and handles standard HTTP requests. That programming model is ideal for web applications, API backends, and interactive HTTP workloads where multiple concurrent requests and I/O-heavy processing are the norm.

Lambda MicroVMs provide isolated, stateful sandbox environments with customer-managed lifecycles for running untrusted or user-generated code.

The three complement each other. An application might use Event Functions for asynchronous backend processing, Web Functions for the user-facing API and server-side rendering layer, and MicroVMs for interactive code execution features, all within the same AWS account and sharing the same operational model.

Why Web FunctionsModern web applications need to serve global audiences with fast, consistent response times while giving developers the freedom to use standard frameworks and iterate quickly. Building this today typically requires assembling multiple infrastructure components, configuring multi-region deployments, setting up latency-based routing, and tuning billing models to avoid paying for I/O wait. Developers often find themselves adapting their application code to fit a specific compute model rather than deploying the HTTP server they already have.

Lambda Web Functions removes this complexity. Each execution environment handles multiple requests simultaneously (up to 128 concurrent requests per environment), which means a burst of incoming traffic can share warm environments instead of mapping each request to a new one. Web Functions meter the CPU time your code uses, so time spent waiting on a database query or an API call consumes little CPU. Memory is metered for as long as the execution environment exists. For I/O-heavy web workloads like server-side rendering, API backends, and AI inference endpoints streaming token responses, this can reduce compute costs.

Deno is joining Cloudflare

📅 Oct 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For years, we’ve been working to make building server software simpler. We questioned how modules could be distributed, what security guarantees a JavaScript runtime could provide, what belonged in a complete toolchain, and how easily an application could be distributed as a standalone executable. Compatibility with Node.js became an important part of that work too: our users wanted the Deno improvements while continuing to plug into the existing JS ecosystem. The team and community built a runtime that brought these capabilities together and challenged assumptions about what JS development could look like.

Today we’re announcing that the entire Deno team is joining Cloudflare to take that work further.

Our ambition has always extended beyond the runtime. I wrote about this in my JavaScript Containers blog post: compute, storage, and communication working together, without every application assembling its own infrastructure.

With Deno Deploy, we took another step towards that goal. We wanted to make running applications as straightforward as possible. But building and operating Deploy also showed us how much complexity remained underneath that developer experience. I wanted to simplify that layer, too.

This led to celld. Building on the Cloudflare Workers programming model, celld lets developers build distributed applications from the start while making the system simple to operate. What excites me greatly is that the scaling is built into the programming model, rather than infrastructure each app has to assemble itself.

The progression from Deno to Deno Deploy to celld explains why this move makes sense to us. At Cloudflare we’ll combine our work with that of the Workers and Durable Objects teams. We want to make this programming model the default way to build servers, whether you run on Cloudflare’s network or your own infrastructure.

Joining also means choosing where to focus our effort. We’ve decided to put our future development work into this shared platform rather than continuing to develop a separate runtime and hosting service. We know this is a consequential change for people who have built on Deno.

The need for better abstractions is especially acute with AI. Durable Objects bring together capabilities that are particularly useful for agent harnesses: inexpensive, serverless execution, persistent state, WebSockets, and a high-level JavaScript interface. This is what I’m most excited to explore and why celld focuses on Durable Objects.
