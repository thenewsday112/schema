What’s the Best Kindle of 2026 (So Far)?

📅 Mar 20, 2019  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I’ve owned all kinds of Kindles, including the newest 2026 Kindles all the way down to a seasoned hand-me-down Kindle that predated the front light era. There are more options than ever now that the latest generation of Kindles have arrived, but you don't necessarily need to upgrade. Here's how to choose the best Kindle for you and your needs.

While you're at it, check out my other buying guides for readers, including the Best Kindle Accessories, Best E-Readers That Aren't a Kindle, Best Digital Notebooks, and Best Gifts for Book Lovers.

Updated October 2026: This guide has been updated with the new 2026 Kindle models as picks (unless the older models are on sale), plus notes on the new Kindle accessories.

The Kindle Paperwhite sports the best balance of features for most readers, including among the new generation of Kindles. For an extra $50 over the base Kindle, you'll get a warm-light option, a larger 7-inch screen, and 12 weeks of battery life. The warm light is a huge bonus if you're a nighttime reader like I am.

The 2026 version of the Paperwhite is not hugely different from the 2024 edition, but you will get a slightly lighter weight and a fractionally smaller body. A couple of AI features are added—Story So Far, which you can access in your menus as “Recap” to get a spoiler-free recap of your book based on how far you've read, and a feature called Ask This Book that's still to come—but I didn't notice any major performance changes like I did between the 2024 and 2026 base Kindle.

The biggest differences come with the Signature Edition, which adds an aluminum frame and two beautiful colors to choose from, plus pogo pins in the back of the device to make it compatible with the new Page Turn Cover ($80) that can add page-turn buttons onto the side of your device. It's a boon if that's a feature you're looking for, but a bummer it's only available with the more expensive model. (Both the regular 2026 Paperwhite and the 2024 Paperwhite are compatible with the cheaper Kindle Click [$35] Bluetooth page-turn remote if you want a more affordable page-turning accessory.) I love the feel of the aluminum body, but it does make it a little heavier if that's an issue for you. You'll also get an auto-adjusting front light, twice the storage, and no lock screen ads without having to pay extra.

★ Older, But Still Good: If you want a plastic model that comes in a color, shop the 2024 Kindle Paperwhite ($200) instead. It costs the same as the new model after the Amazon price hikes in August, so you might as well get the newest model if you're paying full price, but this model does have a raspberry pink version if you want a color. I also expect this model will be on sale for Black Friday, while the newer model may not be.

If you're looking to spend the least amount, there are two Kindle versions to consider: the older 2024 Kindle ($150) and the newer 2026 Kindle ($150). While the newer one has some improvements, the older model is more likely to be on sale this holiday season. It should drop to at least $125 during sale events, though it dipped down to $104 during the most recent Prime Day. The best time to buy a Kindle is during a sale event, and if you want to stick to a budget, wait for one of these models to go on sale.

But when they're not on sale and are listed at the same price, I'd get the newest model over the old. Both now cost $150 following Amazon's price hikes (the 2024 model used to cost $110), so you might as well go for the latest and greatest. Compared to the 2024 version, the 2026 Kindle has faster book-opening speeds and no lag when I exit a book. The newer model also offers a slightly warmer front light, though neither has a truly warm light like the Paperwhite or Colorsoft. I tested the more expensive aluminum-backed Kindle Aluminum, but the plastic version (which comes in the purple Ube color) is cheaper.

Many features remain the same, like the 6-inch screen, 300-pixel-per-inch display, an adjustable front light, 16 GB of storage (though you can upgrade to 32 GB if you get the aluminum version), and up to six weeks of battery life. The body of the updated Kindle is a little shorter without shrinking the screen, and it weighs a bit less, going from 5.56 ounces to 4.9 ounces. The aluminum, however, is slightly heavier at 5.7 ounces. I loved the feel of it even with the extra weight, though, and the color most of all (I have the Alpine Sky blue).

Both models work with the new Kindle Click ($35) Bluetooth page-turner remote, but neither is compatible with the Kindle Page Turn Cover ($80), which is only compatible with 7-inch models. Ultimately, you'll save more money if you wait for a sale, and the two devices are similar enough that you won't be missing out if you choose the older model over the new. But again, if there's no sale happening, get the newest model.

★ An Aluminum Version: While the other Kindles have a Signature Edition that comes with an aluminum body and extra features, the Kindle has the Kindle Aluminum ($190), which has the aluminum frame but not as many features. You will get 32 GB of storage (something the base Kindle has never had before) and the AI-powered Story So Far and eventually Ask This Book, but no other bonuses like an auto-adjusting front light or wireless charging. Still, it's a gorgeous, compact device; I just wish it didn't cost so much for so little.

If you already own a Kindle, a reason to consider upgrading is to get a color screen. It'll breathe life into your bookshelf with colorful covers, show your graphic novels in their true form, and let you color-coordinate your highlight system.

Is it a necessity? No, especially not for adults, whose books typically don't contain color pages or pictures. You'll also get a better price on a color e-reader from Kobo, which has the Kobo Libra Colour ($260) available for cheaper and comes with page-turner buttons built in. The color isn't super vibrant on the Kindle Colorsoft (or any color e-reader) since it's an E Ink screen, and the color features only have a resolution of 150 ppi.

Similar to the new Paperwhite, you'll get a 7-inch screen, 16 GB of storage, and a warm front light option, and there's a Signature Edition with an aluminum back and color frames, more storage, an auto-adjusting front light, and compatibility with the Kindle Page Turn Cover ($80). There's also compatibility with both 2026 and 2024 models for the Kindle Click ($35) page-turner remote, so you don't need to upgrade to the new Signature Edition to get a page-turning accessory. The biggest upside is that every version of the Colorsoft is also ad-free, but getting a lock-screen-ad-free Paperwhite is still cheaper than the cheapest Kindle Colorsoft. The color-body versions are available in the more expensive Signature Edition. Both models have the AI features we've seen on other Kindles, but only have eight weeks of battery life compared to the Paperwhite's 12 weeks.

★ The Older Version: You won't save any money going with the older 2024 Kindle Colorsoft ($290), at least not right now. But there's a chance it'll be on sale when this new version isn't, so keep an eye out if you want to find a Colorsoft on sale.

Students who want to take notes digitally but who also love to read will love a Kindle Scribe. There are now three new Kindle Scribe models, which I detail below, and my favorite is still the second-generation option from 2024, even with new generations out.

It has the same 10.2-inch (300 ppi) paperlike display as the first iteration, but its white border provides a more notebook-style look. It also comes with the Premium Pen that has a soft-tipped eraser at the end. It’s easily my most-used Kindle and digital notebook because of the long battery life (up to 12 weeks) and the convenience of grabbing it for both my reading and note-taking needs. While it doesn't have as many options and layouts as a true digital notebook, the Kindle Scribe nonetheless provides enough assistance to help me organize most of the ideas jumbling around in my brain.

The second-gen Scribe's software capabilities include Active Canvas, which lets you write directly in a book you’re reading, though it won’t work like true annotation. Instead, the notes appear in a small square on the screen like a Post-It note. That means it’ll always be there, and you can resize the screen to keep it less in the way. Even so, it didn’t feel ideally natural to use. It also offers Expandable Margins that let you write notes in the side panel of books, which feels a little more intuitive than Active Canvas, but still isn’t the full annotation experience you might want.

There are also a couple of generative artificial intelligence features: The Refined Writing tool transforms your notes into a script font, while Summarization quickly summarizes pages of notes into bullets with a script font that you can then share from the notebook tab. Both features work well, but I never find myself reaching for them. You can download these software features onto first-generation Kindle Scribes. Notably, no device in the Scribe line was subject to the recent price increase of other Kindles.

I’m a parent of a young kid, and the books in our house are very, very colorful. Pulling up a book on the colorful Colosoft Kids device is a much more fun experience than reading a black-and-white–only option. The color just makes everything more engaging: The bookshelf of your books! The titles available on Kids+! The pages in the illustrated books themselves! Having colorful ebook pages is something a lot of readers may enjoy, but I think kids of all ages are more likely to get the most use from the feature. They can enjoy picture books and graphic novels alike with full-page color; they can colorfully highlight books they want to annotate or study. It’s not quite as vibrant as a digital screen or color on paper since it’s an E Ink screen, but it’s still a fun feature that adds to the reading experience.

You can technically turn on Kids mode on any Kindle, so you don’t necessarily need a Kids-branded model—whether it’s the Colorsoft or a black-and-white model—in order for your kiddos to be able to read books separate from adult options. (Kindle also makes both a basic Kids [$180] and a Paperwhite Kids [$230] model.)

The real benefit is that Kids models come with free limited-time access to Kids+, Amazon’s subscription service that features tons of age-appropriate books. The Colorsoft Kids gets the longest free access, a full year, while the other models will only receive six months. That extra six months, which starts at $8 per month, plus colorful pages, makes a kid-specific model a viable option. The Colorsoft Kids is also waterproof and comes with a fun cover (you get to choose between two designs).

The Colorsoft Kids recently went up in price, as did the regular Colorsoft models and the rest of the Kindle Kids line. This one went up $30 to hit $300, while the Kindle Kids is now $180 (up $50) and the Kids Paperwhite is now $230 (also up $50). At some point, Amazon is also releasing new Kids versions to match all of the new models that the company announced earlier this month.

There's no bad Kindle, in my opinion, but I don't recommend buying all of them. Here's the rest of the current Kindle lineup and my thoughts.

Kindle Aluminum (2026) for $190: The 6-inch Kindle got its first 32 GB version this year with the 2026 Kindle Aluminum, which has an aluminum body that comes in two beautiful colors. It also has the new AI features we've seen in newer models, Story So Far and Ask This Book (the latter is still to be released, though). While it's a beautiful device—everyone who's seen me reading on it has commented that the color is a huge temptation to invest in one—it's a bummer you're not getting much for the price. The lack of a warm option for a front light is a big downside for me as a nighttime reader (especially at this price point), but I love the size and feel of this aluminum e-reader otherwise.

Kindle Scribe (2025) for $500, Kindle Scribe Colorsoft (2026) for $680, and Kindle Scribe Without Front Light for $430: These are all essentially the same device with the same physical design and software experience, with some key differences: the Colorsoft version has a color screen and shorter battery life, and the Without Front Light has (obviously) no front light, so you can't use it in a dark room without an external light. All three models of the latest Kindle Scribe have a new, taller design with an 11-inch screen, a slightly slimmer form factor, and an updated stylus. They also have a quad-core chip and a new homepage, and will support a variety of AI features, including AI search (along with some AI tools for summarizing books you’re reading without giving spoilers, though those won’t arrive until next year). Users can connect to Google Drive and Microsoft OneDrive to mark up documents, which the older model can’t do. Overall, I’d say they’re good devices, but as long as the cheaper second-generation Kindle Scribe is available, I think it’s the better buy.

Amazon launched new accessories along with its 2026 lineup of Kindles: the Kindle Click ($35) Bluetooth page-turner remote, the Kindle Page-Turn Cover ($80), and Amazon Wireless Charging Stand ($60), plus a variety of new covers from Amazon, including ones that will magnetically attach to the aluminum-backed versions of each Kindle. I've tested a few of these accessories so far, and while I've been happy with the experience, there's a clear winner for existing Kindle users. Here are all my thoughts on Amazon's range of new Kindle accessories so far.

This is the best new accessory for a Kindle. It's backward compatible with 2024 Kindles, so you don't need the latest model to use it. The Kindle Click connects via Bluetooth to your device and has buttons on the front to turn the pages forward and back. The two small buttons on the side adjust the screen brightness. For the price, it's a great buy, and you won't need an extra dongle to attach to your Kindle like many third-party page turners require.

This cover works great if you want page-turn buttons on your device, but it only pairs with the new Kindle Paperwhite Signature Edition or Kindle Colorsoft Signature Edition, so it'll cost you $330 for the cheapest combination. At that point, you might as well get the Kobo Libra Colour instead. It does come in pretty colors to match the colorful variants of these Signature Editions.

This is another option for the Signature Editions or the Kindle Aluminum. It attaches magnetically to the back of your Kindle's aluminum body and uses a plant-based leather to wrap around the cover's exterior. The inside has a microfiber interior that's safe to press against your e-reader's screen (and feels nice on your hand).

Looking for more accessories to use with your Kindle? Here’s a handful of my favorite cases, grips, stands, and more. Check out my full guide to the Best Kindle Accessories for more recommendations. Most of these were tested on the older 2024 Kindles unless otherwise noted.

This sturdy, clear case has reinforced corners, making it a smart buy for chronic droppers. The corners didn’t stop any accessories from being able to work with the device, but they did give the Kindle a thicker feel than the Strapsicle or PopSockets cases.

These cases from Bondi have an entirely magnetic back, so you can stick your grip of choice whenever you want on the device (rather than being locked into a specific position). So far, the brand only has cases for the older models.

This pillow stand holder can sit freely on your lap, bed, or any nearby surface while holding up your Kindle. There are two receptacles built into it, one of which is big enough for my larger mugs. I usually drop a snack or my phone into the other one.

This soft silicone strap hooks onto the ends of a Kindle (or other e-reader) without any adhesive needed. It was nice and secure when I slid my hand into it. It comes with two straps, but a single strap was plenty of security for me.

PopSockets came out with its own line of Kindle cases that have a built-in MagSafe adapter, so you can more easily add your PopSockets grip of choice. There are matching sets of cases and grips you can shop for, or you can mix and match as you please.

The PopSockets PopGrips are handy to add to Kindles so you can easily grip your device, but you will need to attach them with an adhesive, a MagSafe adapter, or a MagSafe Kindle case. There are a ton of fun designs, and you can even customize your own, like my custom WIRED PopGrip.

Amazon raised its prices across its product categories, including Kindle devices. The price increases hit several current Kindle models, leaving only Kindle Scribe models with their original price tags. Amazon declined to comment to WIRED, but a spokesperson told CNET the price hikes were due to rising costs in memory and storage components. Interestingly, none of the more powerful Scribe models needed a price update, but it’s also a relief since all three of those models are significantly more expensive than a regular Kindle.

If you have your eyes on one of these Kindles, I’d wait until Black Friday to shop, as they'll likely be on sale.

Amazon usually takes a few years between new Kindle models; we just saw a new lineup launch after two years, and it's likely we'll see the 2024 model availability start to dwindle soon. But the 2022 (and earlier) models are also still around if you want a better deal. I only suggest buying these if they're on sale for the right price, though.

Before making your purchase, cross-check the price against the latest model to make sure you’re paying a fair amount less for the older device.

Kindle (2022): This is the smallest of the lot at 6.2 inches tall and 4.3 inches wide, with a 6-inch screen. If you find tablets too cumbersome to hold with one hand, consider this one, with 16 GB of storage and a 300 pixels per inch (PPI) resolution. It’s tough to find, and I usually don’t see it for less than $75 these days.

Kindle Paperwhite (2021): This is the next step up from the basic model. It has a bright 6.8-inch screen with adjustable warm lighting, a fast page-turn rate, a USB-C port, and 16 gigs of storage. It’s waterproof, too. I like the Signature Edition, which also includes auto-adjust brightness and 32 GBs of storage. I don’t recommend spending more than $100 on this one.

Kindle Scribe (1st Generation): The original Scribe is a great option for note-takers. It has a giant 10.2-inch screen for taking notes, marking up PDFs, or drawing, and numerous paper formats you can choose, like college-ruled or dotted. It also comes with the Basic Pen, which feels natural to write with and attaches to the side of the Scribe magnetically. It’s only worth it for $230 or less, and I often see it for below $200 on eBay.

Kindle Kids (2022): This is an older kid-friendly model. It comes with a cover, a year’s subscription to Amazon Kids+, parental controls, and a two-year warranty, but the fact that it’s only available secondhand means you’ll likely miss out on the subscription window and may or may not still have a cover. Ideally, you won't spend more than $70 on this one.

Kindle Paperwhite Kids (2021): This older Paperwhite version is a step up from the previous basic Kids models. It has a high-resolution 300-ppi display with an adjustable warm screen and is also waterproof. You shouldn’t spend more than $110 on this one if you can find it at all. (I couldn't find any resellers for this older model at the time of writing.)

What’s the Best Way to Buy or Sell an Older Kindle?

Have an old Kindle lying around? Considering buying a used one? If you’re trying to figure out which model you're dealing with, this Amazon page includes photos, specs, and release years for the entire lineup. Once you know which one you have, you may be able to trade it in for credit toward a newer model.

Most old Kindles probably still work, just with fewer bells and whistles. It’s best to avoid Kindles with keyboards or any other physical buttons other than page-turn buttons. Some older models lost the ability to browse, purchase, or borrow new books directly from the device. However, you may still be able to do this via Amazon on your computer or phone and have it sent to your Kindle, which is a better experience anyway.

How Do You Get Free or Cheap Ebooks on a Kindle?

If you already pay for a Prime account, you’re eligible for some free ebooks through Prime Reading. It doesn't include as many options as Kindle+, and in a lot of cases, you’ll see both the Kindle+ and Prime options lumped together; you’ll have to look closely to find the free ebooks. It’s still something, though, and there are even a few magazines.

If you don’t want another monthly subscription and don’t want to continue giving your money to Amazon, take advantage of the ebooks available with your library card. Browsing a digital catalog is not as magical as spending an afternoon looking over every shelf in a library, but such is the ebook life (no one says you can’t read both!). We have a detailed how-to on this that you should check out, but here are some of the takeaways:

Kindles have other helpful features you may want to use. Long-press a word or phrase to see its definition, highlight it, or take notes. You can also search the entire book for that particular word or phrase or translate it to or from other languages.

Word Wise shows short definitions in small text within the lines. Unfortunately, it’s not available for all books, but when it is, you can turn it on or off and choose more and fewer hints. Clicking the short definition opens a longer one from Word Wise and the New Oxford American Dictionary or Oxford Dictionary of English (you can switch between the two), plus translations and a Wikipedia page, if applicable.

While reading, tap the top of the screen and click “Aa” to change font size or type. There’s a font called OpenDyslexic that helps make reading easier for people with dyslexia. You can control margin sizes and line spacing from that menu, too.

When a Kindle connects to a Bluetooth speaker or headphones, VoiceView Screen Reader (accessed through Settings > Accessibility) allows you to use gestures to navigate your device and reads aloud what you’ve pressed. It can also read a book aloud, though it doesn’t sound nearly as nice as an audiobook.

Amazon owns Goodreads, so it syncs seamlessly to Kindles (press the three-dot menu > Goodreads). If you have a Goodreads account, you can review the Kindle books you’ve read or browse your bookshelf and recommendations. Long-pressing a word or phrase, in addition to what is mentioned above, opens up an option to share quotes directly to Goodreads.

There’s also a web browser if you’re connected to the internet (three-dot menu > Web Browser). It’s not the best, so I’d reserve it for emergency searches.

When Amazon releases a new software update, it will download and install to your Kindle automatically when the device is connected to Wi-Fi. You can check if you’re on the latest software by going to Settings > Device Options > Advanced Options > Update Your Kindle. If it’s not on the latest software version, you’ll see an update available and the Kindle will then prompt you to download and install it.

What Do I Do If My Kindle Colorsoft Has a Yellow Bar?

Following customer complaints in 2024 of a yellow bar at the bottom of the Colorsoft Signature’s screen and discoloration along the edges, an Amazon spokesperson confirmed to WIRED that "customers who notice this can reach out to our customer service team for a replacement or refund.” The company also said it’s “making the appropriate adjustments to ensure that new devices will not experience this issue moving forward.” We haven't seen any recent complaints about this issue, and haven't had any issues with a yellow bar appearing on any Colorsoft device WIRED has tested, including the new second-generation model.

The best time to buy a Kindle is during a major sale event like Amazon Prime Day, Prime Big Deals Days, Black Friday, or Cyber Monday, when Amazon often offers steep discounts on its products.

Kindles are great for students, though the model that's best for each student depends on how they'll use it. If they want to pair it with a stylus for highlighting and be able to take notes, they should consider a Kindle Scribe model. These have long battery life, making them great for students to use for weeks on end without worrying about charging. Some textbooks are also available on Kindles. For students who love to read but don’t want to take a ton of notes, a regular Kindle or Kindle Paperwhite will work well.

February 2019 Security Releases

📅 Feb 28, 2019  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

(Update 28-February-2018) Security releases available

Updates are now available for all active Node.js release lines. In addition to fixes for security flaws in Node.js, they also include upgrades of Node.js 6 and 8 to OpenSSL 1.0.2r which contains a fix for a moderate severity security vulnerability. The original announcement is included below.

For these releases, we have decided to withhold the fix for the Misinterpretation of Input (CWE-115) flaw mentioned in the original announcement. This flaw is very low severity and we are not satisfied that we had a complete and stable fix ready for release. We will be seeking to address this flaw via alternate mechanisms in the near future. In addition, we have introduced an additional CVE for a change in Node.js 6 that we have decided to classify as a Denial of Service (CWE-400) flaw.

We recommend that all Node.js users upgrade to a version listed below as soon as possible.

Downloads are available for the following versions. Details of code changes can also be found on each release page.

Categorization: Uncontrolled Resource Consumption / Denial of Service (CWE-400)

All actively supported release lines are vulnerable and the severity is LOW. An attacker can cause a Denial of Service (DoS) by establishing an HTTP or HTTPS connection in keep-alive mode and by sending headers very slowly thereby keeping the connection and associated resources alive for a long period of time. Attack potential is mitigated by the use of a load balancer or other proxy layer.

This vulnerability is an extension of CVE-2018-12122, addressed in November, 2018. The 40 second timeout and its adjustment by server.headersTimeout apply to this fix as in CVE-2018-12122.

CVE-2018-12122 originally reported by Jan Maybach (liebdich.com), keep-alive variant reported by Marco Pracucci (Voxnest), fixed by Matteo Collina.

Categorization: Uncontrolled Resource Consumption / Denial of Service (CWE-400)

Keep-alive HTTP and HTTPS connections can remain open and inactive for up to 2 minutes in Node.js 6.16.0 and earlier. Node.js 8.0.0 introduced a dedicated server.keepAliveTimeout which defaults to 5 seconds. The behavior in Node.js 6.16.0 and earlier is a potential Denial of Service (DoS) attack vector. Node.js 6.17.0 introduces server.keepAliveTimeout and the 5-second default.

The original fix was submitted by Timur Shemsedinov (nodejs/node#2534) and backported by Matteo Collina.

OpenSSL 1.0.2r contains a fix for CVE-2019-1559 and is included in the releases for Node.js versions 6 and 8 only. Node.js 10 and 11 are not impacted by this vulnerability as they use newer versions of OpenSSL which do not contain the flaw.

Under certain circumstances, a TLS server can be forced to respond differently to a client if a zero-byte record is received with an invalid padding compared to a zero-byte record with an invalid MAC. This can be used as the basis of a padding oracle attack to decrypt data.

Only TLS connections using certain ciphersuites executing under certain conditions are exploitable. We are currently unable to determine whether the use of OpenSSL in Node.js exposes this vulnerability. We are taking a cautionary approach and recommend the same for users. For more information, see the advisory and a detailed write-up by the reporters of the vulnerability.

Shigeki Ohtsu and Sam Roberts for the OpenSSL upgrade.

Jan Maybach and Marco Pracucci for reporting vulnerabilities via the appropriate channels (see below).

Other members of the Node.js security team for reviews and discussion.

The Node.js project will release new versions of all supported release lines on, or shortly after, Wednesday, February 27th, 2019 UTC. These releases will incorporate at least two security fixes specific to Node.js, the highest severity of which is 'low'.

The OpenSSL project has announced releases for the 26th which may impact some release lines of Node.js and require inclusion in our security releases. The highest severity indicated by OpenSSL is 'moderate' and impacts OpenSSL 1.0.2 which is used by Node.js 6.x and 8.x. A bug-fix release for OpenSSL 1.1.1 will also be made available and we will assess the impact, if any, on Node.js 11.x which uses this version. Node.js 10.x will not be impacted by the OpenSSL releases.
