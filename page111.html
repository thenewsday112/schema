Will Open Firmware Become the Next Big Trend in Electronic Music Gear?

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For decades, most hardware synthesizers, samplers, and drum machines have operated within the limits of their manufacturers’ software. Users could customize presets and sequences, but adding fundamentally new capabilities usually depended on an official update—or purchasing a different instrument.

That model may be starting to change. The success of M-Vave’s inexpensive FM-1 synthesizer has helped demonstrate how a relatively affordable piece of hardware can become a platform for community-developed software. Independent developers have experimented with alternative synthesizers, sequencers, effects, and other applications, leading M-Vave to release an official development environment.

Now, larger music technology companies are exploring opening their platforms and supporting the alternate firmware trend:

These initiatives are not identical, and not every project provides complete access to every component of an instrument. But they raise an important question for the industry: Will open firmware be the next big trend in electronic music gear?

The M-Vave FM-1 is a compact, affordable FM synthesizer that has attracted attention beyond its original feature set. Its hardware has become a target for independent developers interested in replacing or extending the software, creating new musical applications for a device that costs considerably less than many established synthesizers.

M-Vave’s response has been to provide an official development platform. The FM1 Open GitHub repository includes source code, a software development kit, and a browser-based workbench for managing projects, samples, voices, and firmware.

The platform has continued to evolve, with documented features including multitrack sequencing, sample slots, effects demonstrations, MIDI functionality, and support for importing and exporting DX7 voices. That gives developers a practical foundation for experimentation rather than requiring them to start entirely from scratch.

There are limits to the openness, however. The application code is available under GPL terms, but the underlying FM-1 sound-engine library remains a binary component. Open firmware does not necessarily mean that every part of the instrument’s software has been published.

Nevertheless, the FM-1 demonstrates the potential of inexpensive hardware as a community development platform. Instead of treating a device’s factory feature set as its final form, musicians can explore what else the hardware might do.

Akai SampleKey: A Major Manufacturer Invites Community Development

Akai Professional has previewed SampleKey, a keyboard-equipped companion to its MPC Sample portable sampler. The teased instrument features a 25-key, two-octave keyboard, pitch and modulation wheels, a display, and sampling capabilities.

The more significant announcement concerns its software. Akai has shown community-created applications that transform the hardware into other types of instruments, including chord-generation tools and synthesizers. Demonstrations have included virtual analog, FM, and wavetable synthesis, and more.

A post shared by Akai Professional (@akai_pro)

Akai’s parent company, inMusic, has indicated that its approach will include community access to hardware, open-sourcing some software, and providing a software development kit. Akai has also shown similar modifications running on the existing MPC Sample, suggesting that the initiative could extend beyond SampleKey.

The exact scope of the source-code release and development tools are still to be announced, but the initiative promises to bring bring community-created applications into the plans of an established manufacturer with a large installed base of sampling hardware.

Behringer KICK: Open Development From the Beginning

The Behringer KICK is a standalone music production device built around sampling, sequencing, looping, and performance controls. Its proposed feature set includes a touchscreen, 16 RGB pads, multiple effect processors, MIDI connectivity, and CV/Gate connections.

What makes KICK especially relevant to this discussion is that Music Tribe, Behringer’s parent company, has published a substantial software repository before the product has reached retail. The KICK GitHub repository includes the application, audio engine, hardware communication code, and Linux build system, with the main project components published under open-source licenses.

The company has explicitly encouraged developers to use the code to create, modify, and reinvent the instrument’s capabilities. This places community development close to the center of the product’s identity, rather than leaving it as an unofficial activity that emerges after release.

KICK is still a developing product, so its eventual hardware specifications, software support, and retail availability should not be assumed to be final. But publishing the code at this stage suggests that Behringer is commited to the platform, and sees community-driven firmware as a potential selling point.

It also raises a competitive question: if musicians can build new applications for a device after buying it, the value of the hardware may depend as much on its development community as on the features included at launch.

Erica Synths LXR-02: Open Source From an Established Instrument Maker

Erica Synths has taken a more direct approach with its LXR-02 digital drum machine. On October 23, 2026, the company announced that the instrument’s firmware was now open source and available on its GitHub site.

The LXR-02 combines six digital drum voices with extensive sound-design parameters, sequencing, and effects. Publishing its firmware gives developers access to the code that controls the instrument, allowing them to investigate its architecture and explore potential changes or new features.

Unlike the unofficial firmware projects that have grown around some popular instruments, this is an explicit decision by the manufacturer to release the code. It also demonstrates that open firmware is not limited to low-cost products: the LXR-02 is an established instrument designed for hands-on drum synthesis and electronic music production.

Erica Synths has made the limits of its support clear. The company continues to support its original firmware, while modified versions are installed at the user’s own risk. Damage to the bootloader can require a paid repair.

The distinction is important. Publishing firmware source gives a community more freedom, but it does not automatically mean that a manufacturer will support every modification or assume responsibility for problems caused by unofficial firmware options.

Roland SP-404MKII: DOOM OS Shows the Power of Unofficial Firmware

Not every example of this trend originates with a manufacturer. DOOM OS, an independent project from Klangfeld Labs, modifies the software running on Roland’s SP-404MKII sampler to add a graphical sequencer and other workflow improvements.

The project includes a pattern grid, piano-roll editing, live recording tools, a track mixer, effect automation, custom shortcuts, and pattern-editing functions such as quantization, swing, reversal, and humanization. These additions give users alternative ways to program and edit sequences on hardware that was originally designed around a different workflow.

The project’s current published release is version 0.6.2-alpha, based on Roland firmware 5.52. It is not an official Roland release, and its developers warn that installing modified firmware carries risks. Users should back up their data and follow the project’s installation instructions before attempting a modification.

DOOM OS is relevant because it illustrates the demand that can develop around established hardware. Even a successful instrument can have unexplored possibilities, and independent developers may be willing to address workflow requests that the manufacturer has not prioritized.

Other Examples: The Trend Extends Beyond These Manufacturers

The FM-1, SampleKey, KICK, and LXR-02 are not the only relevant projects, although the level of manufacturer involvement varies considerably.

M-Vave’s broader product ecosystem is worth watching, but each product should be evaluated separately rather than assuming that the FM-1 development model applies to all of the company’s hardware. Official firmware and development resources can be checked through the M-Vave downloads page.

The trend seems to build on the ‘semi-open’ approach that Korg has taken with its ‘logue devices. Synths like the Minilogue XD and Prologue support user-created oscillators and effects, significantly expanding the capabilities of these devices, and allowing users to customize their gear to their needs. The initiative has been successful enough for them to add a drum machine, the Drumlogue, and other devices.

Community developers have also experimented with alternative software and firmware for instruments from Elektron and other established manufacturers. These projects show that there is demand for more flexible hardware, but they should not be confused with official open-source initiatives. In many cases, they remain independent efforts with different levels of access, documentation, and support.

The broader open-source music technology movement also provides useful context. Software projects such as Surge XT and VCV Rack demonstrate how community development can produce mature tools that evolve well beyond their initial releases. Hardware presents additional challenges, because developers must work within fixed processors, memory limits, bootloaders, and physical interfaces, but the underlying appeal is similar: users can contribute ideas and capabilities that a single company might never develop on its own.

And the entire trend is fueled by AI LLM tools and the rise of so-called ‘vibe-coding’. These tools are advanced enough that non-coders are now de-compiling firmware, creating virtual machines, and releasing alternate firmware.

The electronic music community is divided on its reception of ‘vibe-coded’ software. It’s led to a massive increase in the number of music software releases in the last few months, and some musicians see the explosion of new options as being of questionable quality, and just more ‘noise’ to wade through.

On the other hand, alternate firmware seems to be getting a warmer reception. This may be a result of the fact that there have always been small developers releasing music software, but the category of alternate firmware for mainstream hardware is novel and brings new options to electronic musicians.

What Does Open Firmware Mean for Musicians?

For musicians, the most obvious benefit is longevity. A device that supports new community-developed applications may remain useful long after its original feature set begins to feel limited. New sequencing approaches, synthesis engines, effects, and interfaces could make existing hardware more versatile without requiring a new purchase.

Open development can also give users more options and control. A performer might want a custom interface for live use, a producer might prefer a different sequencing workflow, and a sound designer might want to experiment with features that would be too niche for a general-purpose commercial release.

AI-assisted programming tools lower the barrier to trying these ideas. A musician who has never developed a full application may be able to prototype an interface or modify an existing project with help from AI coding assistants. But generated code still needs testing, and reliable audio software requires attention to timing, memory, compatibility, and safe installation.

There are trade-offs. Unofficial firmware can introduce bugs, compromise existing workflows, or create performance problems. Some manufacturers, like Elektron, have raised the spectre of third-party firmware ‘bricking’ gear, making it unusable if an update goes wrong. Manufacturers must also decide how much of their software to publish, which components to keep proprietary, and whether to support community-created modifications.

The term open firmware can therefore describe several different arrangements: complete source-code releases, development kits for creating applications, partially open platforms, or unofficial modifications of closed firmware. Those distinctions matter when evaluating what a company is actually offering.

The emerging opportunity is not simply to make every instrument open source. It is to give musicians more ways to extend the hardware they already own, while providing enough documentation and safeguards for those experiments to be practical.

Will Open Firmware Become the Next Big Trend in Electronic Music Gear?

The M-Vave FM-1 has helped demonstrate how much interest can gather around affordable, modifiable hardware. Akai is exploring a community-driven approach for its sampling products, Behringer is publishing code for KICK, Erica Synths has opened the LXR-02 firmware, and independent projects such as DOOM OS show what can happen when users take development into their own hands.

These examples do not mean that every manufacturer will embrace open firmware, or that open firmware and ‘vibe-coding’ will be permanent trends. They do, though, suggest that the relationship between instrument makers and their customers may be changing, with community-developed software becoming another factor in a product’s long-term appeal.

Bluesky Is Blocking Links to a Major Leak of DHS Employee Records

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Bluesky is blocking users from opening links to a newly leaked archive of records on more than 41,000 Department of Homeland Security employees and contractors, and labels the pages “malicious.”

The files include people’s email addresses and phone numbers, “shared without their authorization,” Bluesky tells WIRED, in violation of its community guidelines. The company says it chose to block the links, rather than remove individual posts, “to avoid limiting public discussion or restricting broader access to reporting and commentary.”

The DHS records, published Wednesday by the transparency nonprofit Distributed Denial of Secrets, list employees’ and contractors’ names, contact information, and membership in internal groups, tied in some cases to surveillance and undercover operations. A WIRED investigation found that the files lay out in detail how DHS is organized, from senior leadership to individual immigration agents, and in some cases appear to show who has access to certain technologies.

Users who try to open the archive through Bluesky’s website or app land on a page labeled “Blocked Link,” which says the URL has been identified as “malicious” and blocked for their safety. Bluesky did not respond to questions about whether the block was imposed automatically or whether any government agency had asked the company to restrict access to the data.

The DDoSecrets homepage loads normally from Bluesky. At least one third-party app, Klearsky, opens links to the DHS archive with no warning.

Bluesky promotes a decentralized approach to content moderation that lets users pick independent services that label or filter posts. Its current community guidelines bar sharing “home addresses, personal contact info, financial or medical data, or identification documents.” (A preview of the DHS leak analyzed by WIRED apparently contained work-related contact information one expert described as details that would be printed “on a business card.”) The same guidelines explicitly protect “factual reporting” on matters of public interest that are “related to government transparency and public officials.”

Bluesky started in 2019 as a Twitter research project, later became independent, and opened to the public in February 2024. By August of this year, it counted 45 million registered accounts, and an estimated 10.4 million people used its mobile app in June. Toni Schneider, the founding CEO of Automattic, has run the company since cofounder Jay Graber stepped down in March. Because the network runs on an open protocol, outside developers can build their own Bluesky apps and decide which of the company’s moderation labels to apply.

Emma Best, cofounder of DDoSecrets, says this is not the first time a social media platform has wrongly labeled the organization's website malicious or tried to restrict access to its publications. Twitter suspended DDoSecrets’ account in 2020, prior to the Elon Musk acquisition and X rebrand, and blocked links to its website after the group published BlueLeaks, a 269-gigabyte trove of internal records from hundreds of police agencies and fusion centers. Best says tech platforms often give in to government pressure but insists they cannot prevent the information from spreading. “They can’t stop the signal,” they say.

A group calling itself the Department of Peace gave the archive, dubbed “Icepick,” to DDoSecrets and journalist maia arson crimew. crimew also built a website for searching the records, and Bluesky blocks that site in its website and app as well.

At least one of Bluesky’s competitors has taken a different tack. On X, the Grok chatbot built by X’s parent company pointed curious users to the Icepick website, describing it as a “cleaned searchable web tool” and saying that the complete dataset can be downloaded via DDoSecrets’ website or by torrent. X did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

EMACS turns 50

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

How the MIT AI Lab’s text editor gave birth to the free software movement.

Programming used to be a very different experience from the one we know today. In fact, the first programmers didn’t write code at all; the six women who worked on the ENIAC, one of the world’s first computers, at the University of Pennsylvania in 1946 did it by physically configuring switches, cables, and plugboards.

A few years later, MIT engineers programmed the Whirlwind by punching code onto paper tape that was then loaded into the computer’s memory—making it among the first “stored-program” machines. Later, they and others moved on to punch cards, one for each line of code.

By the early 1960s, programmers with interactive computers could use a “line editor” to type their code on teletypes—large, noisy keyboard-and-printer machines. They’d type one line of code at a time, and their work would be saved on a spinning hard disk the size of a modern washing machine. But there was still no such thing as the now-familiar full-screen text editors that allow programmers to see and modify many lines at a time on a display.

One of the first such tools, EMACS, turns 50 this year, and it is still widely used.

EMACS first ran on the early video terminals at the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory in 1976, but its core concepts were so powerful that it was rewritten for many operating systems in the decades that followed. Apple even distributed it with Mac OS X. Beyond that, the software-­sharing community that started around EMACS helped give rise to the modern-day free software movement and the GNU/Linux operating system.

EMACS’s roots begin with the TECO text editor (originally the “Tape Editor and Corrector”) that Dan Murphy ’65 created in 1962 for the pioneering PDP-1 computer at MIT. The program’s commands were uppercase letters: A user would type “I” to insert text, “D” to delete a character, and so on. When tape gave way as a storage medium to files on a huge shared disk, the “T” in “TECO” came to stand for “Text.” And as years passed, TECO became a full language that programmers could use to create new commands, called macros, for editing their code.

In the fall of 1971, Carl Mikkelsen ’76 created Control-R, an add-on program for TECO that took advantage of “glass teletype” terminals with video displays. Control-R immediately displayed changes to the file being edited on screen; the programmer no longer needed to reprint each line to see the changes. The drawback: The display could show only a few lines of code at a time.

“Experienced and sophisticated users found EMACS to be eminently more usable than any of the previous forms of editing.”

Then, in 1976, Richard M. Stallman, a researcher at the MIT AI Lab, saw a full-screen editor—called E—on a visit to Stanford’s AI Lab. Inspired, he returned to MIT and set out to improve Control-R. The most important change to the program’s underlying structure: Every key ran its own macro when pressed, making Control-R far easier to customize. The lab’s hackers took to Stallman’s code with gusto and started changing and adding commands.

That created an issue for Guy Steele Jr., SM ’77, PhD ’80. A graduate student in the lab, Steele frequently needed to help other lab members debug their programs. “I noticed that it was much harder for me to help someone who was using one of these packages rather than straight TECO,” he recalls. “Eventually, it occurred to me to try [to] broker consensus on a standard set of Control-R commands that everyone could agree to use.”

David Moon ’75, another member of the lab, helped out. Stallman jumped in as well, and eventually he took over. Together, the group assigned a standard set of commands that made use of the Meta key found on many of the lab’s early keyboards: Meta-F would move the cursor forward a word, Meta-B would move it back a word, and so on. They called the result EMACS, short for “Editor Macros” or “Editing Macros.” “My goal was not to construct a great new text editor,” says Steele. “I was trying to address a social problem.”

Nevertheless, EMACS was a great new text editor. Whereas Control-R could be tricky, EMACS users with “no technical experience at all” could learn to edit letters, emails, and even programs “within minutes or an hour,” according to a history by Bernard S. Greenberg, SM ’74. Meanwhile, “experienced and sophisticated users found EMACS to be eminently more usable than any of the previous forms of editing.”

Stallman started distributing EMACS to anyone who sent him a magnetic tape and a postage-paid return envelope. “EMACS does not cost anything; instead, you are joining the EMACS software-sharing commune,” he wrote in the 1981 manual. “The conditions of membership are that you must send back any improvements you make to EMACS.”

Greenberg, meanwhile, decided to write his own version for Multics, an early operating system by that time owned by his employer, Honeywell. Greenberg’s EMACS won first prize at the International Honeywell Software Conference in 1980, and Honeywell started selling it. Honeywell wasn’t alone in marketing a version of EMACS: James Arthur Gosling, a Carnegie Mellon graduate who went on to lead the development of the Java programming language, made a version that he eventually sold as a commercial product.

This transition from free to paid software ultimately spurred Stallman to launch a project to create an operating system that could be freely shared and improved by its users. He modeled this effort, called the GNU Project, after the increasingly popular Unix operating system from Bell Labs. “GNU” was short for “GNU’s Not Unix,” alluding to the fact that, unlike Unix, it would be free.

The project’s first major application was GNU Emacs (now restyled in lowercase). The GNU/Linux operating system was still years away, so, despite the project’s aim to create an entirely free operating system, GNU Emacs initally ran on Unix. It would eventually run on a range of operating systems, including Linux but also other Unix-based systems. When Apple launched Mac OS X, the first release of its Unix-based operating system, in 2001, GNU Emacs was included.

Apple stopped shipping GNU Emacs in 2019, but the company still has a program called mg (“Micro GNU Emacs”). Though it supports most EMACS commands, it lacks an integrated programming language, making it what some call an “ersatz EMACS.”

Meanwhile, much of Apple’s OS still has hidden support for EMACS commands. The keyboard has no Meta key, but Control-Option-B will move back one word, and Control-Option-F will move one forward.

The 2,000-Nit OLED in Apple’s New MacBook Is a Big Deal

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Laptops with OLED screens aren’t particularly special. They’ve been around for a decade. You can currently buy a touchscreen OLED machine for as little as $700, and even the iPad Pro has had an OLED screen since 2024.

So, why did Apple wait over a decade to release its own touchscreen OLED MacBook Pro? The simple answer for the long wait is that the company wanted to do it “the Apple way.” But in this case, “the Apple way” involves somehow cranking up the display to 2,000 nits, solving both the brightness and glare problems that OLED laptops have always suffered from.

Having seen the thing with my own eyes at a media event today in Los Angeles, I can tell you that this screen is the real deal.

Apple is using a tandem OLED panel, the same layer-stacking technology used in the OLED iPad Pro. The real innovation here, however, is that the panel has been optimized to hit a new peak brightness of 2,000 nits, and that’s a result that matters.

That big number is meaningless on its own, but here’s the context. Nits are the standard measure of screen brightness used across the consumer tech industry. A cheap OLED laptop maxes out around 300 nits. A higher-end OLED laptop might get you up to 1,000 nits of brightness—but only in brighter and more colorful High Dynamic Range content like specific streaming apps, YouTube videos, and games. In other words, the vast majority of the time you’re using an OLED, it’ll feel like a dimmer screen than normal. The iPad Pro can get up to 1,600 nits for HDR content, and 1,000 nits for the less bright, less colorful Standard Dynamic Range content. That’s closer.

But this MacBook Pro’s screen is on an entirely different level. It’s a ground-up redesign of the tandem OLED layer arrangement in the iPad Pro, even down to the manufacturing process. And the result is that it can blast your eyes with 2,000 nits of brightness in both SDR and HDR. That means whether you’re looking at some generic wallpaper, a website, or an HDR-compatible movie, you’ll feel that extra brightness. That’s why, I assume, Apple gave it the name PROLED XDR. In a world where the words “OLED” and “HDR” don’t necessarily indicate how good the experience is, more weighty jargon is needed.

As with all tech, bigger numbers don’t always add up to a noticeable difference for the user. But when it comes to HDR performance, we’ve been waiting a long time for OLED to catch up.

HDR, or High Dynamic Range, serves as a measure of color depth, brightness, and contrast—and in the past, this has always involved a trade-off. An OLED screen can turn each individual pixel any color, and it can turn off individual pixels in the display. This allows OLED to have great color control and unbeatable contrast, since when you turn a pixel off, it renders absolutely black.

But OLEDs couldn’t get super bright, and other screen tech like mini-LED could get much brighter, which is why Apple stuck with mini-LED for years, using it on the MacBook Pro and Studio Display XDR. This technology allowed Apple to have the laptops with the best HDR performance out there.

With the 2026 MacBook Pro, that compromise no longer exists. This OLED screen has both the brightness and contrast in its pocket, and you can see it.

An OLED panel has lots of more benefits over conventional backlighting beyond just contrast. Mini-LED struggles with an effect called blooming, where bright parts of images bleed light to the surrounding area, which is where lots of detail is lost in the image. OLED completely fixes that, giving each pixel a much wider range of control (up to completely turning off). As part of the Apple event I attended, we were shown a selection of HDR images with lots of contrast, a number of which had fields of darkness at the edges that made it look like the image simply disappeared into the bezel. Very pretty. We also watched a darker scene in the show Widow’s Bay in HDR, which was an excellent showcase of the range of detail this screen can show.

The new screen helps this MacBook stay exceedingly light, as well. Much of the savings in weight comes from the OLED design, since the screen uses thinner and lighter materials than screens past. Despite the computer using the same aluminum chassis as the previous MacBook Pro, the new 14-inch MacBook Pro weighs just 3 pounds, which is 12 percent lighter than before.

To put the new screen into context, we were also shown a comparison between Apple’s OLED and the OLED of a high-end competitive Windows laptop. The difference was striking, especially in off-axis viewing. At its worst, there were visible rainbow effects on the Windows machine’s screen.

There’s a catch, though. While Apple’s screen can get up to 2,000 nits, it’s normally capped at 1,000 nits. When the laptop’s built-in adaptive brightness sensor thinks you need a boost, the screen’s brightens increases up to 2,000 nits. So the brightness only hits the high end when you’re outside or working under really harsh lighting conditions. The previous-gen MacBook Pros also did this, but to a lesser degree. With this machine, you’re getting 400 more nits of SDR brightness right out of the box, with the ability to crank it up to twice as much.

Glare is another issue with conventional, low-brightness OLED laptops that the MacBook Pro improves. When deeper blacks are rendered on the screen, it often results in harsher reflections. Sometimes it can get so bad, it feels like you’re looking into a mirror. The brightness alone on the MacBook Pro goes a long way toward overpowering glare and reflections, but Apple is also using what it claims is a “state-of-the-art” anti-reflective coating. Almost every laptop screen uses some kind anti-reflective coating, so one can hope that Apple’s “state-of-the-art” coating is at least on par with those, but I can attest that this is the very best I’ve seen.

The ultimate solution to cutting glare and reflections is still the option for a nano-texture display, where the anti-reflective pattern is actually etched into the screen itself rather than layered on top. That’s available as an upgrade on both the 14-inch and 16-inch models.

The other major innovation happening with the MacBook Pro’s screen is the introduction of touch. Like OLED itself, this is a feature we’ve seen on many other laptops for well over a decade. I came in hoping Apple would swing for the fences here, but its use of touch interaction feels much more modest. (Let us quickly forget the failure of the Touch Bar from 2016.)

The implementation itself is impressive. Interacting with elements on the screen feels every bit as fluid as it does on an iPad. The screen also supports many of the familiar trackpad gestures, such as pinch to zoom or a three-fingered swipe to switch Spaces.

The most natural feeling touch optimizations have been lifted directly from iPadOS. If you tap in the top left window controls, they zoom in to let you select more precisely. You can even grab a window and flick it to the side to quickly get into Split View. My favorite optimization is the ability to tap, hold, and slide down to select an item within the Menu Bar. Very clever. And yet, I was still hoping for a stronger commitment on the software side. You can’t use an Apple Pencil on this screen, and nothing in the hardware of the MacBook Pro has been adjusted to make touching the screen feel any more ergonomic.

The bigger issue is with third-party apps, though. While touch-to-click will work on all apps automatically, it’s up to developers to do any more than that. Even the third-party apps we were shown during demos had varying degrees of touch optimization.

The 3D modeling application ZBrush was a good example. While certain aspects were touch-enabled, none of the controls or menus had the useful optimizations that Apple’s first-party apps had. Hopefully this touch option will become more widely adopted, but I can foresee a future in which these fingers-first interactions are mostly forgotten outside of occasionally scrolling down a webpage with your thumb. And let me be clear: All of this is more than what Microsoft has ever done to optimize Windows 11 for touch. (And Microsoft even has Windows 2-in-1 tablets to support!)

With the 2026 MacBook Pro, Apple is doubling down on what’s working well for it: leading the way in high-end hardware. There’s plenty more testing to do on the MacBook Pro in the coming weeks, but this OLED screen already looks like the one the entire industry will be chasing for years to come.

Most Servers on the Planet Are Vulnerable to This Firmware Code-Execution Flaw

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Defenders across the world face the burden of responding to yet another botched code-execution vulnerability, this time in boot-time firmware used by most of the world’s server vendors and data centers.

The vulnerability, with a severity rating of 9.5 out of 10, resides in the TianoCore EDK II, an open source codebase that hardware makers and cloud providers use to create the UEFI firmware that runs during the boot-up sequence. Last year, a representative of the UEFI Forum called EDK2 the “preeminent solution / implementation of the UEFI specifications.” Virtually all UEFI suppliers (sometimes still called IBVs or independent BIOS vendors), including AMI, Insyde, and Phoenix, use the reference code extensively. Project Mu, Microsoft’s hardened EDK2 fork, used the code containing the vulnerable component as well.

The vulnerability, tracked as CVE-2026-97631, is a memory-corruption flaw in an ICMP component that attackers can exploit to execute malicious code that runs before the operating system and firmware can implement defenses like Secure Boot, which establishes a chain of trust for all code, including the Trusted Platform Module (TPM), loaded during boot. While final payloads delivered by CVE-2026-97631 have no persistence—meaning they run only once—they can give attackers a wide opening to install other payloads that also run each time early in the boot process.

The parties that discovered CVE-2026-97631—Alex Matrosov of Anthropic and Dan Holmes of Google—had arranged for it to stay under wraps until a coordinated disclosure that was scheduled for January. AMI, a maker of motherboards and other hardware, published this pull request last month that detailed the vulnerability and included a code fix. Once the post came to the attention of the discovering parties, TianoCore Project members published a public advisory last week.

With publicly available patch code, it will be trivial for advanced hackers to learn how the vulnerability is triggered and to build working exploits. Further compounding the urgency, patching UEFI code vulnerabilities in millions of end devices sold and used by thousands of hardware makers and cloud providers is cumbersome and time-consuming. Word of the vulnerability comes six days after another botched disclosure, this one involving a critical code-execution vulnerability in the PHP programming language.

“This vulnerability in the ICMP parser of the EDK2 network stack is interesting in terms of how it affects the chain of trust and the broader ecosystem,” said HD Moore, creator of Metasploit and chief research officer at security firm Dragos. “An attacker intent on compromising a nearby endpoint or server could use this bug to hijack program flow at a point where the boot process still has access to sensitive components, like the endpoint’s TPM. This could be used to break disk encryption for cases where an attacker has short-term network access (and it would be even easier in a theft scenario).”

CVE-2026-97631 is a 16-year-old buffer overflow in the PXE-Base Code driver (NetworkPkg/UefiPxeBcDxe), which is responsible for allowing servers, storage equipment, and other devices to boot using code downloaded from a local network. During this process—known as the pre-execution environment boot—the driver uses a network listener to monitor for ICMP errors. Because the driver doesn’t enforce a memory copy limit of 504 bytes, overwriting adjacent data, a malicious insider or a hacker who has gained low-level access to a local network can send an error message that appends code past this limit. The IUEFI module running on the CPU then becomes corrupted.

During network boot, the PXE Base Code driver (NetworkPkg/UefiPxeBcDxe) keeps a receive request open for ICMP error messages and copies each received message into the fixed 504-byte field Mode.IcmpError of its private context structure. The copy loop’s bounds handling is incorrect in both the IPv4 and IPv6 handlers (PxeBcIcmpErrorDpcHandle and PxeBcIcmp6ErrorDpcHandle): for a message longer than 504 bytes it still copies bytes into the field, and it advances the destination pointer by the cumulative rather than the per-fragment length, so an oversized message writes attacker-controlled bytes past the field. Nothing earlier on the receive path restricts the message length.

The malicious error can be delivered through a simple unicast sent from any network distance to a target device’s IP address, as long as it’s sent during the PXE boot sequence. An attacker can deliver a denial-of-service by causing the boot sequence to hang. When the attacker knows what the target firmware build is, it can plant a “counterfeit event” that executes the malicious code on the CPU in its UEFI firmware environment. The code runs in ring 0, a CPU’s most privileged execution level. Because exploits bypass attestation processes designed to cryptographically prove to remote servers that the hardware is secure, they present a key opportunity for elevating limited privileges.

CVE-2026-97631 joins a long list of previously discovered vulnerabilities in UEFI or code that works closely with it. The most severe of them, LogoFail, stemmed from logo images included in the UEFI of just about every hardware maker. Rendering bugs allowed attackers to subvert the chain of trust UEFI enforces and go on to install malicious components before the OS loaded or Secure Boot ran. PixieFail was a vulnerability similar to CVE-2026-97631. It, too, was the result of faulty PXE boot flaws in EDK2. Following discovery of the vulnerability, researchers embarked on multiple fuzzing projects designed to surface other critical flaws in the open source code library. It appears those efforts failed to find this latest flaw.

There are important caveats and limitations to exploiting this latest vulnerability. As noted, some level of control over a local network is a prerequisite. That likely limits exploitation to insiders or attackers, such as nation-states or ransomware operators, who manage to gain low-level access inside a network and are looking for a way to elevate system rights. There’s no evidence yet that CVE-2026-97631 provides an initial entry point into a network. There are other hurdles to clear.

“For exploiting the vulnerability itself, an attacker needs to guess specific addresses within the target system, so it’s not something that you can throw a single exploit at any target and get RCE across a diverse range of devices, but it needs to be tuned for each target model/version/configuration since the addresses will change based on those,” Jesse Michael, a principal researcher at firmware security firm Eclypsium, told Ars.

“Unlike some of the previous, high-profile vulnerabilities like BootHole, Black Lotus, and Baton Drop, this vulnerability can be fixed with a basic code fix, which will require deploying a BIOS/UEFI update, but they don’t need to deal with revocations, pk/kek/db/dbx key management updates, or making sure to update OS and provisioning media like you do with Secure Boot bypasses in the bootloader itself.”

CVE-2026-97631 doesn’t affect home and small office users, or any other users who boot their devices solely from disk drives. Instead, it applies only to organizations with machines that boot from local networks, or conceivably from Internet-connected resources. Even then, the targeted firmware must rely on EDK2, as opposed to a different reference implementation, and use the PXE functionality (which is turned on by default).

In an email, Intel said that none of its “commercial products currently under security support” are known to be affected. “Intel does not include the affected EDK2 functionality in its reference code, though it may be integrated by an OEM board vendor or system manufacturer,” the company added. It advised customers to check with their system manufacturer for specific guidance. Publicly available Intel reference code, however, makes reference to EDK2. Further, TianoCore has said that each Intel-supplied UEFI development kit “is a fully validated implementation … for a specific revision of the EDK II source.” Many other Intel documents reference EDK2 as well.

Arm, meanwhile, responded to a list of questions with: “CVE-2026-97631 is not directly applicable to Arm’s product portfolio.” Like Intel, firmware the company makes available also borrows heavily from EDK2. TianoCore has also documented Arm’s reliance on EDK2.

While it’s possible some platforms are free of EDK2 code, it’s prudent for users of all servers to investigate if CVE-2026-97631 affects them.

Questions sent to AMD, Phoenix Technologies, AMI, Microsoft, and Amazon—likely some of the other biggest organizations affected—went unanswered when this post went live. Without concrete answers to questions about the use of EDK2 by organizations like these, it’s impossible to say what or how devices are affected and what actions, if any, these organizations, or their users, should take. Representatives from the TianoCore Project also didn’t respond.

Some readers are likely to be unhappy at the lack of detail. Sometimes—particularly in cases involving botched disclosures that break embargoes months before they’re scheduled to lift—there’s no escaping the lack of actionable information in initial reporting.

Those affected should check with the maker of the firmware being used to boot their systems and ask for guidance. Beyond that, they should consider turning off network boot capabilities at least temporarily if possible. Firmware configurations should always enable security features such as TPM and Secure Boot whenever possible, although those defenses don’t protect against this particular threat. It’s also a good idea to monitor logs for signs of exploitation attempts.

A JSON schema Is not an AI governance layer

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The AI governance argument is happening one layer above the code that actually stops your agent.

In the space of two weeks this September, the argument about who should govern AI produced four different answers, all of them from the people who build the systems.

Demis Hassabis floated an industry-funded oversight body, modeled loosely on FINRA, to test frontier models before deployment. Dario Amodei called on labs to slow frontier development and give independent evaluators genuine access, and days later Anthropic and Accenture committed at least $2 billion over five years to embedded evaluation. OpenAI started publishing reports on unexpected model behavior. And according to the Wall Street Journal, Mark Zuckerberg, Elon Musk and Jensen Huang each spoke with President Trump and persuaded him to drop the oversight body, arguing that a common regulator would protect the lead of the biggest labs, and that companies can secure their own systems without one.

Whichever of these positions you agree with, none of them talks about the code that decides whether the agent in your application is allowed to send an email, rotate a key, merge a branch or move money.

That decision is not made by a lab, and it is not written in a policy; it is made by a function in your own code, and that function returns true or false.

I want to show you a bug in that function, and it’s not a thought experiment: an independent stress test found it in PIC, an open protocol I maintain for checking agent actions before execution. The schema accepted an uppercase SHA-256 digest, the reference runtime quietly normalized it and carried on, and the specification had never said whether that representation was valid. No cryptography failed. The protocol simply had more than one defensible meaning, which is harder to spot than broken cryptography, and I suspect many agent deployments running today have the same problem.

Stack Overflow’s April 2026 pulse survey of 1,100 developers and technologists found that 60% of them block agents from making unapproved system changes. That makes sense, but it leaves out the difficult part: what counts as approved, and will every component in the path agree on the answer?

Here is an agent proposing a payment. It supplies the tool it wants to call, the inputs that shaped the decision, and the evidence behind its claim that the payment is authorized.

A JSON Schema will confirm that provenance is an array, that every entry carries an identifier and a permitted trust value, and that the required fields are present. Unless the protocol adds a rule of its own, this payload is valid. The identifiers are not unique, and the evidence reference now points at two different entries.

Suppose the policy is simple: a payment may run only if the evidence behind its claim comes from a trusted source. (For now, take the trust labels in the payload at face value; I come back to who gets to set them later.) To apply that policy, the verifier has to work out which entry “invoice” refers to, and two reasonable engineers resolve it differently.

Neither lookup is a bug on its own; both are normal, idiomatic code, and the specification never said which entry should win. Given the payload above, A resolves the evidence to the trusted entry and the payment executes, while B resolves it to the untrusted entry and the payment is blocked. Both verifiers run the same protocol version, with the same policy and the same trusted keys; the only difference is how each one looks up an id. So the same bytes, checked against the same schema, give two different results, and an attacker only needs to know which service is on the other end of the call.

The schema did its job, but that job is limited. It checks the shape of the payload: that fields are present, have the right type, and hold allowed values. It can even express some rules between fields, such as “if this field is present, that one is required”. What it can’t express is a rule like the one this payload needs: the “invoice” cited as evidence in claims has to point to exactly one entry in provenance. Standard JSON Schema has no way to say that a value in one place must match exactly one value somewhere else in the document; and uniqueItems doesn’t help either, because the two entries have different trust values, so the schema sees them as two different items. Whether the payment goes through depends on that link, and the schema leaves it to each verifier to work out.

Security people have seen this many times: two components read the same input in two different ways, and the difference between them is where the vulnerability is. HTTP parameter pollution is one example, where a proxy reads the first id and the application reads the last. XML signature wrapping is another: the verifier checks the signature on one element, while the parser reads a different one. Then there is JWT alg confusion, where the token itself says which algorithm should be used to check it. And with HTTP request smuggling, a proxy and the server behind it disagree about where one request ends and the next one begins.

This kind of bug has been around for about twenty years. What we have learned from it is fairly simple: when two implementations need to agree on what the same bytes mean, any detail the specification leaves open can be read in two ways, and sooner or later an attacker will take advantage of that.

Agent protocols are now running into the same problem, and PIC did too: the stress test I mentioned found these gaps, and we closed them by tightening the specification and adding each case to the conformance tests I describe below. Most of these protocols are only a year or two old, and they haven’t yet been tested the way token and signature formats have been over the years; so I think a governance layer written this year should be checked with the same care we would give a new token format.

Developers normalize input constantly. We trim whitespace, lowercase identifiers, repair Base64 padding, accept alternate encodings, all because rejecting data that is obviously recoverable feels hostile. That instinct is right in a search box and dangerous at an authorization boundary.

Compare those strings exactly and the proposal fails. Call strip() on both first and it passes. Neither behavior is irrational on its own; the defect is that individual implementations get to choose.

With signatures, the problem is more serious than it looks. A SHA-256 digest is usually rendered as hexadecimal, and uppercase A through F denote the same number as lowercase a through f, so lowercasing the input looks harmless. But it’s not, because signatures cover bytes rather than numbers. If the producer signed a payload containing the uppercase form and the verifier lowercases before constructing its canonical payload, verification fails on an authentic proposal.

Flip it around and it gets worse: a verifier that checks the signature over the original string and then normalizes the value before applying the policy is no longer enforcing exactly what was signed. For a hex digest, lowercasing gives the same number, so nothing changes; but the same habit applied to other fields can change which policy rule matches (a trimmed or lowercased tool name, for example, can match an allow-list entry that the signed name would not), and at that point the policy is acting on a value the signer never signed.

Base64 has the same problem: some libraries tolerate embedded whitespace, some restore missing padding, some silently translate the URL-safe alphabet, while others reject all three. When a signature decides whether an action may execute, those choices made by your Base64 library become part of your authorization policy, even though nobody on your team wrote them or reviewed them.

It helps to separate two decisions here. The first is how the signed payload is turned into bytes. Standards such as RFC 8785 for JSON, XML c14n before it, and the deterministic encoding rules in COSE fix things like field order, number format, and escaping, so that every implementation signs exactly the same bytes; but they leave the contents of a string alone, so RFC 8785 keeps an uppercase digest uppercase. The second decision is which values the protocol accepts at all. Here you can either normalize them (for example, lowercase every digest before signing and before checking) or accept a single form and reject everything else; so uppercase hex fails, unpadded Base64 fails, and a tool name with a trailing space fails.

PIC does both. In its canonical attestation mode it signs a canonical serialization (it still keeps a legacy mode that verifies raw payload bytes), and for values it took the second route, for a simple reason: normalization is one more piece of code that has to be written again in every language that uses the protocol, and the history of c14n bugs suggests that different implementations of the same rules end up disagreeing, just as parsers do. With strict rejection, a payload in the wrong format is refused as soon as it arrives, with a clear error, instead of getting through and causing two verifiers to disagree later, when the problem is much harder to track down. If you are designing something similar, pick the approach you can describe completely in your specification; but do pick one, because otherwise the meaning of a signed proposal depends on whichever library happens to decode it.

The ambiguity that mattered most was not about encoding at all, but about who gets to declare an input trustworthy.

That field is easy to serialize, but on its own it tells the verifier almost nothing it can rely on. The proposal is produced upstream of the verifier, frequently by the very agent requesting permission to act. Honoring the field at face value lets the requester supply its own authority, which is the classic confused deputy problem, only written in JSON.

Trust has to be derived inside the enforcement boundary. The producer may identify a source and attach evidence, while the verifier decides whether that evidence leads back to something the operator actually trusts, such as a known signing key.

The difference between integrity (the data wasn’t changed) and authority (someone allowed to approve it actually did) is what matters most here. A matching hash proves the verifier received the bytes it expected, but it says nothing about who produced them, who approved them, or whether they should authorize a payment. An attacker can supply a file together with the correct hash of that same file; the integrity check passes, because nothing was changed, but nobody with any authority has approved anything.

A signature carries authority only when the verifier looks up the signing key in a list of keys the operator trusts (a list the proposal has no way to add to), and only when the signature covers the fields that matter: the tool, the arguments, the impact class, the relevant provenance, and the expiry. Otherwise a genuine signature can approve one thing while the action itself is something else; for example, a signature for a transfer of 500 could end up attached to a request for 50,000.

In other words, it is the verifier that marks an input as trusted, only after checking the evidence, and it should never take that label from the agent.

None of this is specific to my protocol. Take the Model Context Protocol: tool definitions describe their inputs with JSON Schema; there are separate SDKs written in several languages; hosts add their own approval prompts and allow-lists on top; and before the arguments reach the tool, they have usually passed through a model, a client, a transport and a host policy check. That is a lot of separate code reading the same data, and each piece can read it a little differently. The same is true if you wrote your own approval step, meaning the code that checks a tool call from the model before your application actually runs it.

You don’t need to adopt anyone’s protocol to check this. Take your own approval path and ask three questions. If the same field appears twice, which one wins, and does every component agree? If a tool name arrives with a trailing space, what happens, and is it the same thing in your CLI as in your HTTP bridge? If the payload says it is trusted, who decided that?

JSON Schema describes the structure of JSON data, and it is good at required properties, types, enumerations, patterns, and composition. Those controls still matter, because rejecting malformed input early leaves the semantic layer fewer cases to worry about.

But the questions that decide whether an agent should act usually involve more than one field, and sometimes more than one system. Does an evidence identifier resolve to exactly one provenance entry? Does the signature cover the same tool and arguments now being proposed? Was the signing key trusted at the moment of verification, not merely at generation? Which error wins when several checks fail at once?

If you force all of that into one schema, you get a document that looks precise but still doesn’t say how the runtime should behave. A stronger design separates three layers, each narrowing what the next is allowed to see:

Each of these layers needs its own code and tests, and a note in the documentation asking implementations to behave sensibly can’t replace any of them.

A written specification cannot cover every interpretation its authors failed to imagine. A second implementation finds those gaps within days, because another language, another library, and another developer make different reasonable choices.

The practical answer is a set of conformance tests you can run. Each vector holds the exact input, the expected verdict, and a stable error code where the proposal must fail. Every implementation runs the same vectors, and the suite deliberately covers the cases ordinary application tests never reach.

These vectors do more than catch regressions. They also define how the protocol behaves in the cases people could disagree about, and once an ambiguous case is settled, it becomes a permanent test rather than another paragraph that future implementers get to interpret for themselves.

The order in which errors are reported should be settled in the same way. One proposal can contain duplicate identifiers, the wrong tool name and an invalid signature all at once. If one implementation reports the duplicate identifier first and another reports the bad signature first, the operator gets different advice for the same input, depending only on which service they happened to call; and every adapter ends up with its own order. Deciding which check runs first, and writing that into the conformance tests, makes the behavior predictable and much easier to debug.

The verifier itself can be strict and still be undone by the code around it. For example, the CLI turns on strict checking but the HTTP bridge doesn’t; a framework adapter keeps an old, looser default months after the main verifier changed; or a caller turns off evidence checks to save time and keeps accepting inputs that say they are trusted, without noticing that the two together are unsafe.

So every way into the verifier (the CLI, the HTTP bridge, each framework adapter) has to start with the same strict settings. When the protocol changes, you may still need to accept an old field or an older, looser behavior for a while, so that people who built on the previous version have time to migrate. That is reasonable, but the old behavior should only apply when someone turns it on deliberately, and the software should show a clear warning whenever it is used. If a migration flag can bring back the old behavior without telling anyone, then whether a deployment is secure depends on how it happened to be configured, and that is rarely written down.

Earlier this year I argued that agents expose security checks organizations never actually wrote, because those checks had always lived in human judgment. Writing them down turns out to be only the first step: the code that replaces that judgment also has to mean the same thing everywhere it runs.

This is why one of those four positions matters more here than the others. The argument that each company should secure its own AI, without a shared body defining what secure means, is a description of many independent teams making many independently reasonable choices about normalization, trust derivation, and error precedence. A disagreement between companies is not the same thing as a disagreement between parsers; but in practice it leads to a similar situation, where different implementations give different answers to the same question.

The concern behind it is fair enough; a testing body set up by the biggest labs could well have helped those same labs stay ahead. But if nobody agrees on shared rules at the top, those rules have to be agreed lower down, by the people who write verifiers, in public and through conformance vectors that every implementation can run; and in practice, a good part of that work will fall to developers.

Policies, schemas, signatures, and human approvals are all useful, but none of them actually stops an agent until some code turns them into a yes or no before the tool runs. That code doesn’t have to predict everything an advanced model might do; it has a much shorter list of jobs: reject ambiguous identifiers, compare security-sensitive values exactly as specified, keep integrity and authority distinct, decide trust only from evidence the verifier can check itself, and, for the same proposal and policy, return the same verdict everywhere the protocol is implemented.

If any of these is left to each implementation, one service can allow a proposal that another one, with the same policy, blocks.

The public argument is about how fast AI should advance and who referees it, but the question for your own code is a simpler one: whether the systems controlling today’s agents can agree on what they are reading. You can find out by running the same test inputs through each of them and comparing the answers.

I Put a Bunch of AI Agents in Charge of a Fantasy Soccer Team. They’re Not Better Than Humans—Yet

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I have consulted my AI-powered crystal ball, and it’s telling me that frontier models are going to get a lot better at making predictions in the near future. Just ask my Premier League fantasy team.

A few weeks ago, I deployed a small horde of AI agents to craft and manage an experimental roster. Given the proliferation of AI agents into pretty much every aspect of our lives, I wanted to know if a swarm of bots could put together a winning team.

I first looked at the potential for AI here a few years ago, playing around with a tool from the UK’s Turing Institute that selected teams after modeling previous fantasy league data. Its suggestions were remarkably bonkers—it didn’t incorporate real-world information, like a player’s injury or a team’s change in management.

This time, while on a flight to visit family, I asked Codex for help. First, I fed the model a couple of relevant research papers. Then, I asked it to build a harness—instructions and code that guides different agents through various steps—that would check recent news and run a model to make predictions.

It quickly came up with a setup that seemed to collate and analyze way more information than I ever would have. And yet I was surprised by how dumb the initial team suggestions were. Leaving Erling Haaland on the bench? Really, Codex?

I then asked Codex to create a team of agents that did different things: several that read news and blog posts; others that analyzed recent results; more that ran models; and a bunch that critiqued suggestions. This seemed to significantly improve the suggestions. I tried increasing the number of agents to more than 100, hoping this would make the system smarter still, but it didn’t change much besides running up my API bill.

Still, the improvement from a few years ago was striking. According to Haifeng Xu, a computer scientist at the University of Chicago who studies the uses of AI in market situations, newer models may be even better at making predictions because they’re able to consume and analyze huge amounts of real-world information, and they’re able to orchestrate more complex systems for their guesses.

“AI is just getting smarter in understanding the world—I feel like that's the fundamental reason they’re improving,” Xu tells me.

Late last year, Xu was marveling at the growing popularity of prediction markets like Kalshi and Polymarket. He wondered how the best commercial models might fare on those markets, so alongside several colleagues, he began testing their forecasting skills.

They had models guess, for example, who would win the NBA Championship and who President Trump would meet with.

“Very quickly we started to realize that models did really well,” Xu tells me. The models were sometimes able to do better than the average person without any special instructions or guidance, he says. In their testing, GPT-5 was the best at making predictions, followed by Grok 4, Claude Sonnet, Gemini, and Llama.

The team went on to develop Prophet Arena, a benchmark that measures a model’s ability to make predictions. It’s one of a number of benchmarks designed to test AI’s future-gazing capabilities; another, Prediction Arena, from researchers at Harvard University and a company called Arcada Labs, recently gave frontier models $10,000 to wager on prediction markets over 57 days. The models consistently lost money in their experiment, but the researchers saw evidence of them improving consistently over time.

Xu tells me that AI can most effectively make predictions using a custom harness, and notes that having agents with unique tasks—reading the news, collecting other data, and analyzing the whole system, for instance—seems to be the most effective approach.

Interestingly, AI still often lags behind very skilled experts known as superforecasters. Xu explains that, although humans cannot consume as much information, their understanding of the real world—that is, of the dynamics behind a news story—is deeper.

That said, there’s growing evidence that the human superforecaster’s edge is eroding. A contest called the Metaculus Cup recently showed that AI can sometimes beat even the best human predictors. And a benchmark called ForecastBench could provide further evidence that AI can match human skills when the results of an ongoing contest conclude soon. In fact, I think we can expect the big labs to turn their attention to future-gazing as a powerful new way to measure progress in machine intelligence.

All of these predictive experiments seem to suggest that, like math, coding, and law, industries predicated on trading may soon be upended by AI. Perhaps the best human traders will take on a new kind of role: supervising and orchestrating AI agents capable of divining the future far better than they can.

For the time being, my AI’s suggestions still don’t seem all that impressive. Its team hasn’t outscored the average over the past weeks. I certainly won’t be using any of its suggestions in my own selections.

But AI will no doubt get better, perhaps making it all too easy to dominate fantasy league pretty soon. I’m kind of hoping it won’t, though, as it would spoil the fun of trying to earn bragging rights by winning all by yourself.

The China-nexus cyber espionage group known as Volt Typhoon has been attributed to a now-disrupted covert access network dubbed Silent Train that has been put to use for zero-day exploitation and strategic access.

"First identified in late 2023, Silent Train uses rotating chains of compromised SOHO, IoT, and enterprise infrastructure," Lumen Technologies Black Lotus Labs said in a report shared with The Hacker News. "It employs anti-forensic techniques, compartmentalized operations, and post-operation cleanup to minimize detection and attribution."

Its main purpose is to facilitate critical attack phases like vulnerability exploitation, credential access, data exfiltration, and to maintain continued access to victim environments. The activity stands out for two main reasons -

Hundreds of victims have been identified across telecommunications providers, government, defense contractors, satellite and mission-critical communication services, universities, IT firms, and critical infrastructure such as water, energy, transportation, maritime, rail, aviation, and emergency services.

Volt Typhoon (aka Bronze Silhouette, Insidious Taurus, UNC3236, Vanguard Panda, and Voltzite) is the moniker assigned to a China-linked state-sponsored group known for its targeting of critical infrastructure networks, particularly communications, energy, transportation, and water and wastewater systems sectors.

Known to be active since at least 2021, the threat actor was previously observed exploiting a file upload vulnerability in Versa Director as a zero-day (CVE-2024-39717) to deliver a bespoke web shell called VersaMem designed to harvest credentials and enable access to downstream customers.

Separately, Volt Typhoon is also suspected to have taken advantage of a likely zero-day vulnerability in Quest KACE before it was patched in October 2025. The campaign is estimated to have impacted more than 30 victims.

"Silent Train is built around rapidly rotating chains of compromised SOHO, IoT, and enterprise devices that are exploited on demand, used against carefully selected targets, cleaned up after each operation, and continuously refreshed," Black Lotus Labs said.

"This model has enabled Volt Typhoon to conduct exploitation and post-exploitation operations with exceptional operational security while minimizing opportunities for detection, attribution, and disruption."

A defining characteristic of these attacks is the repeated targeting of systems that can open the door to broad visibility, control, and access across large enterprise environments of strategic value to the U.S.

What's interesting is that the covert access capability began to take shape sometime around November 2023, just weeks before the first round of disruption targeting KV-botnet, an IoT botnet linked to multiple China-nexus state-sponsored threat actors, including Volt Typhoon.

Silent Train's operational activity is said to have surged in the wake of the botnet's second disruption in late January 2024, while simultaneously shifting to an ephemeral infrastructure comprising diverse device types and across geographies.

"Rather than continue using persistent infrastructure, Volt Typhoon shifted to a model using one-time-use chains of compromised devices that it could rebuild as needed," Black Lotus Labs said. "Volt Typhoon rapidly assembled and dismantled transient infrastructure, creating a resilient, secure, and compartmentalized framework that protected valuable access."

Described as a "layered, multi-hop framework," Silent Train is designed to let Volt Typhoon operators relay malicious traffic through several devices before it reaches the targets so as to obscure its origins from both victims and defenders. Each link in the chain serves a specific purpose -

No less than 27 device classes, ranging from SOHO and IoT devices (e.g., TP-Link, Intelbras, Ruckus Wireless, SATO, NETGEAR, Zyxel, and Cambium) to enterprise platforms (e.g., Fortinet, Cisco, PRTG, Mirth Connect, and CyberPanel), have been exploited and enrolled into the covert network.

"Silent Train stands out for its operational discipline, focusing on short-lived, selfmanaged sessions rather than persistent relays or C2 infrastructure," Black Lotus Labs said. Silent Train sessions lasted about four hours on average, with some lasting only minutes."

Put differently, the infrastructure points to deliberate compartmentalization across layers, confining some device types to certain roles that remain constant while the underlying infrastructure itself changes over time so as to complicate attribution efforts.

By early 2025, however, Silent Train is believed to have evolved from a completely ephemeral relay network to a hybrid architecture that retained enterprise-grade devices for an average of one to three months instead of changing them after each engagement.

Subsequent maturation of the platform introduced reverse-tunnel variants that used open-source proxies like Chisel and FRP to send traffic from infected devices to upstream infrastructure.

Within the U.S., Hawaii, California, and Texas have emerged as the focus areas of Silent Train operations, which boils down to the fact that these states host major military installations, telecommunications infrastructure, defense contractors, satellite communications providers, and critical logistics networks.

"Victimology indicates deliberate efforts to maintain access to critical ecosystems that support defense, logistics, communications, military and civilian operations, especially during heightened tensions or crises in the Indo-Pacific region," Black Lotus Labs said.

"The resulting picture is one of a highly structured framework built to acquire, protect, and sustain strategic access at scale. The Inside Story of Why Apple Finally Brought a Touch Display to the Mac

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It’s a bright day in Cupertino. Apple’s new CEO strolls to an outdoor seating area within the company’s otherworldly ring-shaped headquarters and pulls up a chair. An employee notes the angle and asks if he minds the sun. John Ternus doesn’t mind at all.

Nearly two months into his tenure, he’s been busy. While the tech world buzzes about AI agents and the upcoming IPO of a company reporting spectacular losses, Ternus’ leadership of a ridiculously profitable firm has so far been defined by a sequence of significant launches—the long-awaited foldable iPhone, a household device that can control digital appliances, new Mac Minis, and AirPods. Intentionally or not, all that activity sends a message that, even as AI dominates the public imagination, hardware products still rule.

Ternus doesn’t endorse that take, but he doesn’t object when I suggest it. “We’ve been working on these products for quite some time,” he says. “It feels like everyone here is just kind of riding high right now, enjoying it.”

I ask if Apple is about to break a long-imposed taboo and introduce a Macintosh with a touch screen.

We are referring to the new MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR Display that Apple is releasing today at an event in Los Angeles. It’s got a mighty M5 Pro or M5 Max chip and a show-stopping tandem OLED screen. (A tandem screen has multiple light-emitting layers, producing more dramatic contrasts.) But its most conspicuous feature is one that’s been deeply woven into iPhones, iPads, and even Apple Watches for years but not Apple’s laptops: a touch display. After years of resistance, a Mac screen has finally gone digital. As in fingers.

This turnaround is a big deal to Apple fans. Ternus shrugs off the drama. “The Mac is first and foremost an indirect input device, built around the trackpad and keyboard. But I think there’s times when it’s comfortable and handy to have touch.”

Ternus and Apple want to make sure that the Mac faithful don’t panic that the 42-year-old franchise is losing its identity to Apple’s gangbusters mobile products. “We needed to get to a point where adding touch didn’t make the product lesser in any way. It didn’t make it heavier. It didn’t make it thicker. That came together with the OLED screen, the best display we’ve ever put into a Mac,” Ternus told me. “All of these things, and, oh yeah, it has touch as well.”

Apple has a long history of refusing to implement certain desirable features, only to pivot when it feels the time is right. Steve Jobs once proclaimed iPods would never do video, until they did. He also once said “no one’s going to buy” a jumbo-size phone—and then Apple released a supersized iPhone 6. Then there was that time Jobs declared styluses inappropriate for tablets. Now the Apple Pencil works with iPads and the brand-new Duo.

The 2026 MacBook Pro instantly qualifies as the canonical example of this switcheroo. Apple executives had been on record regarding touch on PCs as a misbegotten idea. Never mind that just about every PC maker not headquartered in Cupertino has implemented touch on their laptops. And customers of Microsoft, Dell, Acer, Lenovo, and HP have snapped them up. Clearly, people wanted touch on their laptop displays. Even toddlers knew it—put some rug rats in front of a computer and their little arms shoot straight to the screen in futile attempts to swipe and scroll as they do on their iPads. People were baffled why Apple—the pioneer in touch displays since the iPhone—was so steadfast in its rejection.

In 2015, while doing a story about a new iMac, I pressed Phil Schiller, then the senior vice president of worldwide product marketing, on this very question. Usually, top Apple executives field such queries with cryptic remarks. In this case, Schiller began a filibuster. “I’m going to get a little bit philosophical,” he said, then launched into what I later described as the Grand Unified Theory of Apple Hardware.

He explained that every Apple hardware product—the Watch, iPad, iMac, MacBook—is basically a computer and a piece of glass. Each one has a specific function. And each one has an interface appropriate to the hardware. Multitouch is fine for products like the iPhone, he said—but not the Macintosh. He provided two reasons: ergonomics and the software interface.

“From the ergonomic standpoint, we have studied this pretty extensively, and we believe that on a desktop scenario, where you have a fixed keyboard, having to reach up to do touch interfaces is uncomfortable,” he told me.

As for the interface, Schiller argued that Apple’s two main operating systems were built on different design principles. The iPhone and iPad’s iOS was multitouch from the get-go, and didn’t use macOS conventions that require precision, such as cursors and tiny boxes to close windows. “These two worlds are different on purpose, and that’s a good thing—we can optimize around the best experience for each of those and not try to mesh them together into a least-common-denominator experience,” he told me.

A year later, when Apple introduced a MacBook Pro with the ill-fated “touch bar”—a dynamic strip of virtual function keys above the keyboard’s top row—Schiller doubled down on the view that touch should never find its way into a Mac display. He said that such a move would mean totally redesigning the menu bar for fingers, and that would clash with the experience of pointer devices like a touchpad or mouse. “You can’t optimize for both,” he said.

To double-check this assumption, Apple conducted user tests with touchscreens on Mac. “What the heck, we could be wrong,” Schiller told me then. “We’ve absolutely come away with the belief that it isn’t the right thing to do. Our instincts were correct.”

When I met with Apple executives Johny Srouji and Greg Joswiak this week to discuss the touchscreen MacBook Pro, you bet I invoked Schiller’s list of concerns. (Apple’s reps chose not to make Schiller, who now holds the title of “Apple Fellow,” available for an interview. In fact, they laughed when I asked.) It wasn’t only Schiller drawing a red line. In 2021, Ternus told journalist Joanna Stern, “The Mac is totally optimized for indirect input. We haven’t really felt a reason to change that.”

“Ten years is a very, very long time,” says Srouji, Apple’s chief hardware officer. “Many things have changed, whether it's the display technology, the custom silicon, et cetera.” Eventually, he told me, the confluence of chips, display tech, and software architecture “met our bar.” “I like my engineers to be gated by physics, not imagination or timelines,” he says.

Joswiak, who holds Schiller’s old title of SVP of worldwide marketing, concedes that touch was overdue, at least for users with sophisticated needs. Its pro customers—engineers, scientists, software developers, and media creators—were impatient with Apple for its failure to let them interact directly with the screen. “Most pros own an iPad, and almost every pro owns an iPhone,” Joswiak says. “So this is something that they’re natural with. There’s a generation of young creators and young people using our MacBook Pros that expect to touch.”

Between the lines, Srouji and Joswiak seem to consider their competitors’ touchscreen implementations shabby and lazy. The Apple ethos, as every one of its executives will happily explain to anyone who asks, is an expectation of quality that’s sky-high. “If you’re working on something easy, you’re wasting your time,” says Srouji, who has been at Apple since 2008 and has pretty much driven its innovation by spearheading the company’s charge to custom-build silicon.

“The easy things, I want to outsource,” he continues. “So the custom silicon is hard. The touch is hard. The display is hard. Everything we build is hard.” This swaggering philosophy dates from the Steve Jobs days and, in 2026, stands in contrast to the endgame of the ascendant AI industry, which hopes to turn over the toughest parts of industrial production to superintelligent entities.

Srouji presents Mac touch as a suite of innovations, starting with that screen that Ternus described as the Mac’s best ever. It also involves custom silicon and a rearchitected thermal system to prevent the display from feeling hot when people pinch, stretch, and swipe on it. Srouji contends that current touchscreen laptops suffer from “wobble” when fingers make contact with the display; to minimize this effect, Apple has redesigned the hinge on the MacBook Pro screen to stabilize it.

When I get a demo of the 16-inch version (as with previous Pros, it’s also available in a 14-inch version), I see what they mean about the display. It’s noticeably dense and bright, and the obsidian blacks are the deepest I’ve seen on a computer screen. In part because the OLED screen doesn’t require backlighting, this MacBook weighs less than its predecessor.

As a pair of Apple managers take the MacBook through its paces, I see how Apple has addressed some of Schiller’s decade-old concerns, like having to redesign the menu bar for fingers. While it looks the same, when you move a finger over a menu item, it expands to allow you to select your choice with precision. Some of the objects on the screen, like the traffic-light buttons that close or hide windows, also get bigger when you touch them.

They show me how the touch tech can handle precision tasks, like color grading in the video editing software DaVinci Resolve, or tweaking a soundtrack on Logic Pro, Apple’s high-end audio workstation. One innovation, not just for pros, is a new gesture to pull down the “Dynamic Island,” that black oval hosting ongoing tasks that has made its way to Mac from iPhone. An expanded version floats on the screen like a widget, enabling easy access to Siri responses and Spotlight search.

But what about greasy fingers messing up the screen? I was told that the new display has an anti-reflective outer coating that will require no more cleaning than your normal non-interactive screen.

Apple clearly hopes that the MacBook Pro launch puts an exclamation point on the burst of product announcements that Apple customers are still absorbing. For the foldable Duo, some customers are impatiently waiting for delivery dates that stretch into January. (Lines were long at Apple stores last Friday, when the device went on sale, and Joswiak personally greeted those who queued up at Apple’s Fifth Avenue store in New York City.)

I ask Srouji whether Apple is making a statement with its armada of new products, maybe as a way to say that in a year dominated by AI obsession, Apple is still here. He responds much as his new boss did, contending that Apple is simply sticking to its game plan. “We’re here to build the most amazing products in the world, and we want to ship them when we believe it’s ready and complete,” says Srouji. “That’s the statement we’re making. I think everyone knows we’re here.”

While longtime users will be happy to hear that a Macintosh now has a touchscreen, those with limited budgets will be disappointed. Apple’s current stance—that touch-on-a-Mac display is most appropriate to the “pro” crowd—rationalizes an astronomical price tag: The new MacBook Pro starts at $2,899 for the 14-inch version and $3,399 for the 16-inch model. Financial analyst Neil Cybart, who got an early MacBook Pro briefing, thinks that serious professionals will pay up. “People are in search of value, and that means going for products that leverage technology to improve what they’re doing,” he says.

When I ask Ternus about the timeline, or even the possibility, of seeing touch displays on less exorbitantly priced MacBook Airs and iMacs, he shakes his head. “You’ve been doing this a long time,” he says. “You know I will never tell you anything about our future.”

I don’t know why I bothered to ask. If Ternus said that touch would never happen on a MacBook Air, who would believe him? I’d be back at The Ring a year later, hearing that Apple had finally figured out a way to do it right.

Google to Allow One-Tap Android App Installs From Approved Third-Party Stores Outside U.S.

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Third-party Android app stores can now apply to Google for permission to install apps with one tap, without Android's usual confirmation screens, and to update them in the background.

To qualify, a store must pass identity checks and send Google its full catalog, along with every new app and update, for ongoing malware scanning. Approved stores get the features from December 1, but only on devices running Android 17 QPR2 or later in supported countries, which do not include the US.

Today, a user installing apps from a third-party store must first enable the store to install apps, then confirm each install and each update. From December 1, a store in Google's Registered App Stores program gets that permission automatically once it is approved, and its updates run in the background under the user's own update settings.

Google announced the program in March, alongside changes it said resolved its disputes with Epic Games. Joining is optional, and stores that stay out will continue to operate as they do today.

Under Google's rules, the store must still get the user's explicit consent for each install and "must not install apps silently without user consent." Large downloads and background installs must show a visible notification while they run.

If an install is started on another device, the store must show a notification that does not close automatically.

IT administrators can block registered stores from being installed on managed devices. Google's program pages do not say whether the block also applies to a store that is already installed.

Beyond the catalog scanning, Google's requirements say a store must:

Each store stays responsible and liable for everything in its catalog. Google won't moderate the content in registered stores.

If more than 1% of install attempts or more than 2% of a store's apps are found to be potentially harmful over a 30-day period, Google charges additional fees to screen and clean up the catalog.

A store that fails to meet any requirement, including paying on time, can be removed from the program until the issue is resolved. Google gives notice and a chance to fix it first.

Google's program pages do not describe how submitted apps are scanned, how quickly new uploads are checked, or what results the checks produce.

Registered stores also get a Google tool, the Android Developer ID API, to check whether each app in their catalog comes from a verified developer and can be installed. Google recommends that stores block installs of apps that fail the check.

Android developer verification is a separate Google program, now in its first phase with seven app stores. Since September 30, certified Android devices in Brazil, Indonesia, Singapore and Thailand have checked that apps from those stores come from verified developers, according to Google. Google plans to extend the checks worldwide in 2027.

Availability follows each user's home country. Short trips to unsupported countries do not turn off one-tap installs or automatic updates.

Android 17 QPR2 is one of Android's quarterly platform releases (QPRs). Its latest version, released October 16, is still a beta, and Google's release notes give no date for the final release.

In the US, a separate Google program for distributing third-party stores through Google Play has been in effect since July 22.
