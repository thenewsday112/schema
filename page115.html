Making Failure Diagnosis Reliable with Pensive AI

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

By Praneeth Yenugutala, Victor Dusa, Anastasia Samoilova, Megan Ren, Justin Wei, Marcelo Mayworm

At Netflix, data and machine learning practitioners run millions of workloads each day across orchestration systems and compute engines such as Titus, Spark, Trino, and Druid. On peak days, roughly 100,000 workloads fail. When workloads fail, the failure itself is only the beginning. Someone still needs to find the useful signal in several logs, decide whether the cause sits in their code or the platform, and determine whether to retry, reconfigure, or escalate. Even a small share of difficult failures can consume engineering time, waste compute, and interrupt critical data pipelines.

Our first answer to that problem was Pensive, an auto-diagnosis and remediation system in Netflix’s data platform. Pensive collected logs from failed workloads, used regular expressions and hand-authored Java rules to extract and classify errors, then passed retry or remediation guidance to products such as Maestro. It made ~ 50% of failures self-service and, in some cases, automatic.

Rules work well for known patterns, but their coverage grows only when someone recognizes a new failure mode, understands the logs, writes a rule, and keeps it current as engines and log formats change. Over one three-month period, Pensive processed roughly seven million job-failure classifications, yet about half fell into the unknown bucket. We needed a way to expand coverage without giving up the deterministic parts of Pensive that already worked.

To solve this ambiguous and complex problem at Netflix scale, we invested in Pensive AI to add AI-assisted error extractions and focused diagnosis and remediation guidance to our data and ML practitioners. The goal was not simply to place a model in front of a large collection of logs. We needed bounded extraction, predictable evaluation inputs, responsive product behavior, and quality checks that could follow the system from local development through production. Those requirements shaped every layer of the design, which we will discuss in this blog. Pensive AI wraps AI around deterministic processes, which gives broader coverage, low latency, controlled AI costs, and easier maintenance across the software development lifecycle. Building on this foundation, we plan to explore more agentic debugging experiences, as Part 6 describes.

Early results are encouraging. AI error extraction now covers 100% of failures, including the failures that legacy Pensive classified as unknown. Failed step attempts where no one used Pensive AI averaged 321% more manual debug-log exploration actions than attempts where someone did (an observational comparison, not a causal claim).

The two paths complement each other. Error extraction improves an existing capability and is always on. It lets the user of a failed Maestro workload quickly check the parts of the logs that may point to an immediate root cause. It has low latency and low cost, and it can help in a large share of cases. On-demand debugging enables a new capability. It looks at symptoms across the logs and adds the LLM’s own knowledge and reasoning for a deeper analysis, at slightly higher latency and cost. Extraction gives a fast first look at every failure, and on-demand debugging triggered by the user goes deeper when that first look is not enough.

Part 1: Finding the useful errors

Legacy Pensive rules are precise, but each one captures a particular log shape. A simplified rule looks like this:

- name: SparkContainerOOM
logSource: GENIE_STDERR
restartable: false
regex: "Container.*is running beyond physical memory limits.*Killing container"
summary: Spark AM container runs out of memory

With Pensive AI, we wanted to find useful errors that had no corresponding rule and adapt more easily when familiar errors changed shape after a platform upgrade. Data Portal (our UI for data pipelines) surfaces these extracted errors when available and links back to the source logs, giving practitioners a focused starting point for triage. That raises a question: how can we ask a model to identify an error without asking it to repeat a long stack trace token by token?

Producing output tokens is much slower than reading input tokens. A direct transcription approach would make long errors expensive and slow. Instead, Pensive numbers the lines of each log and gives the model a sequence of {lineNumber, text} records. The model identifies the first and last lines of the error span, and Pensive reconstructs the selected text from its own log copy, leading to lower cost and better latency. This way, the model always returns two integers (start and end line number), whether the selected logs span a few lines or hundreds of lines. The displayed content comes directly from the original log, reducing hallucinations. The model can still choose the wrong range, which is why extraction quality remains an integral measurement for our AI evals, as we will discuss later.

For orchestrated workflows, “Pensive AI Error Extraction” runs automatically whenever a step fails. A step attempt is one execution of a step within a Maestro workflow. Maestro already calls Pensive for a regex-derived error and restartability classification, so AI extraction runs in the same request and returns its results alongside the established fields. The overall Pensive response must return within Maestro’s hard 90-second post-processing timeout for a failed step attempt.

That deadline shaped the extraction path. Pensive extracts errors from the logs in parallel to reduce latency, and the per-log calls share one deadline set well inside Maestro’s timeout limit. A per-log extraction call that misses the deadline is excluded from the result, allowing the overall request to return on time.

The data contract between Pensive AI and the various consumers needed the same care. The legacy data model contained one flattened error string; AI extraction instead returns a list of errors, one for each log source, with deep links back to the originating logs. Changing the old field in place would have broken existing clients. We introduced the new data model beside the legacy non-AI error extraction data model and continued producing both. Data Portal, our user interface for monitoring and observing data pipelines, preferred AI errors when available and fell back to the legacy value when they were not during the rollout. That allowed a gradual rollout and ensured that historical failures still display an error, since we chose not to backfill the old data due to log retention issues.

The migration is still in progress today. The Data Portal UI surface used for failed Maestro steps reads the new AI extracted error field today, while the query editor (for editing and running SQL), Workbench notebooks (managed compute platform and UI to edit and run jupyter based notebooks) and some slack clients calling Pensive still use the legacy error extraction path. Each client needs a compatible schema change before it can consume the new data. Keeping both fields populated lets teams adopt the new behavior at their own pace without forcing a coordinated cutover as we continue evolving Pensive AI.

Part 2: Full failure diagnosis and the product experience

Beyond error extraction, we built Pensive AI Debugging, a tool that analyzes failed step attempts and provides root-cause analysis and suggested remediations. It is available through programmatic APIs, MCP, and the product UI (Data Portal), all backed by a common entry point across the Netflix Data Platform. This shared model supports consistent behavior and custom product experiences.

This diagnosis still has to arrive quickly and remain consistent across investigations. A debugging response can take tens of seconds or a minute to generate, compared with a few seconds for error extraction. We run full AI-based debugging on demand instead of automatically, which keeps costs under control because most failed steps are never opened. We treat the failed step attempt, not the person who triggered it, as the unit of work, so everyone investigating the same failed attempt sees the same diagnosis.

That choice gives us a stable unit of work for caching. We cache each debugging response by step attempt. A second request becomes a key-value read instead of another AI inference call. The cache also prevents duplicate work, reducing latency and lowering costs further. This also means that a debugging generated in the Data Portal is cached and immediately retrieved for the MCP path instead of incurring another AI inference call. A conditional write creates an in-progress record, one caller starts generation, and other callers wait for the shared result to avoid conflicts when concurrent requests come in for the same step attempt failure. If generation remains in progress past an abandonment timeout, Pensive treats the record as abandoned and reclaims it. Completed responses expire after 90 days to help control costs and optimize storage because debugging requests for much older workloads are uncommon.

Pensive AI supports multiple ways to initiate debugging responses. A caller can (1) read a cached response, (2) generate one if no cache entry exists, or (3) regenerate and overwrite an existing response. Those semantics support the product UI, programmatic access, and the evaluation harness while keeping the response associated with a single immutable workload failure.

For users, the most important behavior is that a long-running answer feels alive. Pensive streams the response as it is produced. The service emits server-sent events, and the GraphQL layer republishes them as a WebSocket subscription for the Data Portal client.

Each event carries the complete response generated so far. That duplicates a small amount of text, but it keeps the client simple: it does not need a replay buffer or token reassembly logic, and the next event corrects the display after a dropped or late event. Generation is independent of the connection that requested it. A disconnect does not cancel the work, and the final answer still reaches the cache. A hard reload can show a loading state and poll for the completed result. Within the single-page application backing the Data Portal UI, active streams are maintained independently of view and component lifecycle, so navigation does not interrupt a response in progress.

Part 3: Measuring and improving the behavior we want

LLM-assisted systems need more than a demo or a few working examples. Before shipping in production, we need confidence in both error extraction and debugging guidance, and we need to know that we have not regressed on failures that legacy Pensive already handled. We built separate evaluation frameworks focused on the two distinct jobs.

Building labeled data is often the expensive part of evaluation. For error extraction, legacy Pensive’s hand-written rules provide reference labels for failures they recognize. They also serve as excellent ground truth in most cases. We run those rules over production logs and compare their extracted spans with Pensive AI’s output. This gives us a reliable regression set without relabeling every example by hand, though it is not an absolute ground truth (since our human-built rules could also have been stale). For now, we keep these rules frozen as a label source. This lets us continue to build comparison evals and run them on top of the existing system.

Stable inputs are essential for repeatable evaluation experiments where we A/B test new models/configurations or context injections to improve response quality. The logs Pensive AI uses can expire after as few as seven days or as many as 30 days. Production Pensive AI requests archive fetched logs to S3 object storage asynchronously during workload execution time so the upload is not on the user-facing critical path. Uploads do not overwrite an existing object, preserving the exact snapshot used to create an evaluation case. Evals use those archived logs, so score changes remain attributable to the system under test. The archive also prevents parallel experiments from overloading the source systems and lets us run them without affecting production. Cached S3 log inputs further reduce costs when running evaluations.

Concretely, we measure four complementary signals for error extraction:

  • Inclusion: An LLM judge compares the legacy Pensive error for a failure with the errors Pensive AI extracted and determines whether the known error is still represented, even when wording differs.
  • Precision: A second judge sees only the extracted AI errors and evaluates whether each is meaningful and actionable on its own.
  • Repeated noise: A script looks across the whole dataset for lines that recur too often. A generic status line may look harmless in one answer but become distracting if it appears in a large share of failures. Lines appearing in more than about 30% of workflows are candidates for explicit exclusion. In practice, the exclusion list has only three lines, each of which otherwise appeared in nearly every extracted error.
  • Latency: We record the latency of every extraction call. Quality scores alone cannot reveal when an apparent improvement requires too much additional work, which often correlates with increased AI costs.

This combination gives us an actionable feedback loop. Prompt changes can reduce general noise, while the dataset-level view exposes the stubborn boilerplate that prompt changes do not eliminate. Existing rules act as a regression floor, and archived logs give us a repeatable way to compare changes. At Pensive AI’s scale, model and system choices must satisfy quality, latency, maintainability and cost constraints together.

To seed the debugging ground-truth dataset for evaluation, we mine internal engineering-support Slack threads and use an LLM to identify threads that link to an orchestrated workflow, describe a resolved problem, and match the class of issue Pensive AI handles. A second model synthesizes a reference debugging response from the relevant discussion. Paired with logs from that workflow failure, it becomes a concrete evaluation row in our evals dataset. This approach works only retrospectively, because Pensive AI in production aims to provide debugging guidance before such a Slack discussion happens.

We also made the debugging generation process visible. Tracing follows the request through Pensive, with child spans for each log fetch and extraction tool call. Our tracing backend based on Braintrust brings the prompt, completion, tool calls, and latency into a single view. During early product development, that visibility helped us inspect low-performing debugging responses in context instead of guessing which input or operation caused them. Tracing helped us improve the responses and fill context gaps even before we had comprehensive evals. In production, the API and UI return the trace identifier with the response, so feedback can lead directly to the trace for in-depth analysis.

Part 4: Keeping quality in the release path

Evaluation is not only for experiments with LLM orchestration, models, prompts, or context changes. Changes to log selection, trimming logic, or a source system can change what the model sees and therefore change extraction quality. We use the same evaluation harness and evaluators throughout local development and Pensive AI deployment so those effects are visible before users encounter them.

For each pull request, the code branch is deployed to an isolated preview environment and a CI job runs an experiment with our evals against that isolated environment. Precision and inclusion thresholds gate the merge. The job also runs after a build is promoted, catching interactions that were not present when individual pull requests were evaluated in isolation. If the promoted build misses its thresholds, it alerts the on-call engineer to investigate or roll back.

The evaluator runs on a schedule against production to surface gradual drift or external changes. Developers can also trigger evaluation experiments remotely while iterating, using the same deployed-environment path as CI. Consistent infrastructure makes the results comparable across local iteration, preview builds, promoted builds, and production.

Part 5: Looking for feedback signal from users

The system is only valuable if it reduces the amount of manual debugging and log chasing done by our users. We wanted a measurement that was available from product telemetry and close to the behavior Pensive AI was designed to change. Manual debug-log exploration became one of the main signals. The Pensive interface already offers links to logs for a failed step attempt. To estimate whether the feature reduced raw-log hunting, we compared failed step attempts where someone interacted with Pensive AI with those where no one did. The second group averaged 321% more manual debug-log exploration actions. This observational comparison does not establish causality or a specific reduction in mean time to resolution, but it suggests that focused diagnosis often allows practitioners to inspect fewer raw logs. Programmatic MCP access also lets teams build operational flows with their terminals or custom agentic experiences around the same diagnosis service.

Another more explicit signal we collect is thumbs up and thumbs down on the debugging response. These feedback signals land directly alongside the internal traces so we can quickly associate feedback to the complete log of inputs/outputs and the path the AI took to arrive at the answer. More importantly, we created an internal business-hours alerting system where each thumbs down response sends us a Slack alert. This was valuable to resolve a lot of rough edges and context gaps initially in the product and is helping us improve the product continuously even today.

Part 6: Future Work building on these foundations

Pensive AI is the foundation for more work that goes beyond diagnosis and recommended remediations. We are exploring “Pensive AI Deepthink,” an opt-in managed agentic experience with higher cost and latency. It builds on the precise, low-latency analysis produced by Pensive AI Debugging and gathers additional context from internal documentation, manuals, past Slack discussions, source code, read-only warehouse queries, and Spark execution details. A routing step directs the investigation to the appropriate skill, such as Spark failures, data-audit failures, or late workflows. This keeps the shared starting point stable and deterministic while allowing the investigation to become more specialized, further improving recall. We are also in talks with the Spark team to leverage Pensive AI responses for failure debugging analysis and A/B comparison for upcoming platform migrations.

We also plan to invest in “Pensive AI Error Classification”. Hard-to-maintain legacy rules also classify failures by restartability and by whether they are platform or user caused. Maestro acts on those labels and changes its retry behavior accordingly like failing fast or tuning the default number of retries (platform errors are tried more than user errors by default). Error classifications must meet a higher bar than error extractions and debugging alone. Moving the majority of the error classifications to AI would let us retire most of the remaining rule-maintenance burden and avoid retrying unknown failures that were unlikely to succeed. For restartability, the costliest error is classifying a recoverable failure as non-restartable, because Maestro will turn a step that could have been recovered into a hard failure, leading to end user alerts and manual remediations. The evaluation framework will need to be tuned around that outcome before AI-enabled classifications can take on those decisions. Equally important, we must avoid retrying non-transient failures that will not succeed, because retries waste compute and delay data pipelines.

Our work on Pensive AI in Data Platform addresses concrete user pain points in debugging, improves maintainability for platform owners, and improves precision and recall for error extraction. Pensive AI extends coverage to failures that legacy Pensive previously classified as unknown. Reliability comes from the system around the AI model calls: bounded error extractions, compatible client data contracts, responsive delivery, reproducible evaluations, caching and tracing, and release controls that keep quality and cost visible as the platform changes.

Multi‑Ratio Video Resizing for Mobile Feed and Marketing

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Serdar Cellat, Yuchen Xie, Bernadette Marcu, Blaid Burgess, Cody Jarrett, Carlos Pereira, Qing Shi, Samuel Carvajal, Manuel Pamplona, Dorothy Jung, Feiko Ritsema, Benjamin Klein, Alex Woo, Eugene Lok, Anna Pulido, Rebecka Swe, Sanford Holsapple, Ranjith Kumar Shetty

Introduction

At Netflix, a crucial part of our mission is ensuring our content looks stunning on any device. Vertical formats are increasingly important for Netflix, as we have 1.2B followers across our social media accounts, and have recently rolled out Clips, our vertical video feed in the Netflix mobile app. Traditionally, creating vertical assets meant editors and producers manually reframed horizontal videos in editing tools, a process that doesn’t scale as our catalog and use cases grow. To support both our in‑app feed and external marketing campaigns at scale, we built an automated multi‑ratio video cropping system, tightly integrated into our internal production tools, that can generate high‑quality vertical and other aspect‑ratio variants with minimal manual effort.

Our Video Resizing Approach

Converting cinematic 16:9 footage into mobile‑friendly aspect ratios takes more than a simple center crop. Filmmaking often involves dynamic character blocking and complex camera movement. We need an intelligent “virtual camera” that can follow the key action, respond to how each scene is composed, and produce crops that feel as intentional as the original shot.

We address this challenge by framing video resizing as a tracking and temporal optimization problem. Our pipeline processes each asset in three main stages, designed to produce 9:16, 4:5, and 1:1 crops in a single pass, without incurring additional computational overhead.

Shot Detection and Splitting

First, we segment the video into atomic shots using a neural‑network–based shot boundary detector. In many cases, shots contain multiple characters conversing on opposite sides of the frame. To better handle these scenarios, we use an Active Speaker Detection based approach to logically split a single continuous shot into multiple sub‑shots. This allows our virtual camera to cut and re-focus on different speakers as the conversation flows. Our Active Speaker Detection leverages an internal face detector and LipFlap to reliably identify active speakers in both live‑action and animated titles.

Focus Region Discovery

Once shots and sub‑shots are defined, we identify the most important region in each frame. We combine face detections with visual saliency maps to filter and prioritize the faces that should be in focus. In scenes with multiple characters, we further rank faces using signals such as active speaker status and relative size. Our model aggregates these cues with shot‑level content understanding to establish a continuous “focus region” for the virtual camera.

Temporal Reframing

Once we’ve established the focus region, the next challenge is deciding how the crop should move over time. Tracking the focus region on a strict frame-by-frame basis would produce a shaky, distracting viewing experience, so we apply a set of heuristics that consider how the focus region moves over the course of a shot. When its displacement is small, we lock a static crop window that consistently covers the relevant area. When there is significant motion, we compute a dynamic tracking window and smooth its path so that any panning feels intentional and cinematic.

Tooling and Workflow Integration

We integrated the resizing algorithm directly into our internal Clips tool, so editors and producers can access it as part of their existing workflow. The interface enables them to automatically generate multiple aspect‑ratio versions of a clip with smart reframing. A unified in‑app module provides intuitive shot‑reframing controls, allowing users to review the algorithm’s crops, and quickly make targeted adjustments where needed.

For more complex shots or custom campaigns, producers can export the resizing parameters as XML and load them directly into external timeline editors such as Adobe Premiere. This allows agencies and external partners to start from the algorithm’s framing decisions instead of recreating crops manually or relying on tools like Premiere’s Auto Reframe. In our internal test, our resizing algorithm outperforms Auto Reframe on a curated evaluation set, so the XML export effectively brings those higher‑quality crops into external workflows. By bringing automated resizing and manual refinement into a single place, this pipeline significantly reduces production time and manual effort while preserving creative control.

Conclusion

Our work on multi-ratio video resizing is one step in a broader effort to make content creation more scalable, without compromising creative intent. By pairing algorithm-driven reframing with editor-friendly tools, we’ve reduced the time teams spend on repetitive resizing tasks and unlocked faster experimentation across mobile feeds and marketing campaigns. As we continue to broaden these capabilities to new formats and workflows, our goal remains the same: to give creatives more time to tell great stories, and to deliver a consistently high-quality viewing experience for our members and fans.

Attackers Use Microsoft Teams IT Support Scams to Deploy Malware and Ransomware

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Cybersecurity researchers are calling attention to what they say is a "recurring social engineering pattern" across hundreds of cases in which threat actors use Microsoft Teams' external chat creation feature to conduct impersonation scams or to deliver malware.

"A consistent pattern of attacker behavior has emerged across these incidents: the use of the same throwaway tenants, the same 'IT Support (Internal)' display names, the same remote-access tools, and increasingly, the same payload domains," Arctic Wolf Labs said in a report shared with The Hacker News.

The attacks consistently employ IT-themed display names or friendly-sounding first names to subvert external-user warnings in Teams. These messages originate from Teams identities established through newly created or acquired Microsoft 365 tenants.

Nearly 30–40% of the cases investigated by the cybersecurity company involve the use of *.onmicrosoft[.]com, a permanent fallback domain automatically generated by Microsoft when a Microsoft 365 tenant is created. These domains masquerade as IT infrastructure, opting for a help desk- or support-themed word as the tenant name to increase their credibility -

As observed in many cases before, these Teams sessions are preceded by mail bombing, where the victim's inbox is flooded with hundreds of newsletter or subscription confirmations in a short span of time. The approach is designed to induce a false sense of urgency, with the Teams IT support message arriving soon after to help resolve the problem.

In recent iterations, the attackers have been observed adding the target organization's domain or company name in their display name to lend the messages additional legitimacy.

"We also observed a recent shift toward paid top-level domains (TLDs) for tenant creation," Arctic Wolf said. "Of the paid domains we identified in these types of scams over the past few months, approximately 50 percent of fake identities used the .top TLD, around 40 percent used a standard .com domain, and just over five percent used multi-level domains such as .com.pl, .com.vn, and .com.br."

The activity aligns with that of STAC4749, has a history of creating IT-themed cloud domains under the ".top" TLD and leveraging plausible employee usernames to make the accounts appear authentic. These attacks have led to the deployment of Chaos ransomware, according to Sophos.

The attacks specifically go after organizations where external access is enabled. This also involves an element of vishing to trick victims to granting them hands-on-keyboard access through a legitimate remote monitoring and management (RMM) tool like Quick Assist, AnyDesk, ScreenConnect (ConnectWise), TeamViewer, Atera, Zoho Assist, NetSupport, and LogMeIn.

The remote access is abused to conduct reconnaissance, deliver additional payloads like the GoGRPC backdoor, facilitate lateral movement to domain controllers, and deploy additional RMM tools like Level for redundancy, before exfiltrating sensitive data and dropping the ransomware binary.

"In a subset of cases, attackers leveraged Microsoft's notes," Arctic Wolf said. "Attackers generally avoid pasting a URL directly into Teams, which would trigger Safe Links / ZAP scanning, and instead navigate to the payload themselves during the remote session. Consequently, endpoint and identity telemetry are essential monitoring surfaces alongside message scanning.'

In one case identified by the cybersecurity vendor, the threat actor is said to have instructed the user to run a PowerShell command to download a file and then launch it. The PowerShell command is designed to download an MSI installer from Microsoft Azure Blob Storage.

When the victim runs the installer, it launches a PowerShell script responsible for extracting and executing a Python implant embedded within it. The backdoor then runs another MSI transaction that issues LDAP queries to Active Directory for all computer and user objects and then transmits the information to an external server.

"External Microsoft Teams chat has become an effective initial-access vector because it exploits trust rather than a software vulnerability," Arctic Wolf said.

"Across the activity examined, attackers combined email bombing, convincing IT-support impersonation, newly created tenants and domains, and rapid social engineering to move victims from a seemingly routine chat to remote access, payload execution, and, in some cases, broader compromise."

This2Shell, That2Shell, Everything2Shell. That's pretty much how the last week went. Critical bugs that let attackers run code remotely are showing up everywhere. Hackers are scanning for targets, some flaws are already being used in attacks, and security teams have more urgent patches than they can keep up with.

And that's not even the full story. A leaked key led to a software supply-chain attack. AI agents are being tricked into following harmful instructions. Old bugs are still being used to break into systems, and some attacks are so simple it's hard to believe they still work. Some websites even went offline to avoid taking chances.

It's been a rough week for anyone trying to keep systems safe. Here's what happened.

PHP2Shell Flaws Puts Organizations on Alert — The PHP Foundation released patches for two critical vulnerabilities in PHP, CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, that could lead to unauthenticated remote code execution. The security flaws have been codenamed PHP2Shell. Since public disclosure, the shortcomings have witnessed widespread scanning efforts, with Cloudflare stating it detected millions of exploitation attempts after public disclosure. To date, no successful exploitation has been observed. The severity of the flaws, coupled with the ubiquity of PHP on the web, prompted several websites to go offline as a precautionary measure.

Two Critical Wasmtime Flaws Could Let Malicious WebAssembly Modules Escape Sandboxes

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Two critical flaws in Wasmtime, a WebAssembly runtime that can be built into other software, could allow a malicious WebAssembly module to escape the sandbox intended to contain it, the project's maintainers say.

An attacker would first need a vulnerable Wasmtime build to compile a module they control, and one of the flaws affects only ARM64 systems. Fixed versions were released on October 19 and 20, and the maintainers have not reported any attacks.

Wasmtime runs WebAssembly code outside web browsers. Both bugs are in Cranelift, the compiler it uses by default to turn WebAssembly into machine code. Wasmtime's maintainers at the Bytecode Alliance rated each flaw 9.9 out of 10 on the CVSS scale in advisories published on October 19.

The sandbox is meant to keep a module inside set limits. A module can only use its own memory and the host functions it is given, and its code can only jump to checked locations.

Because both flaws need a module the attacker controls, the risk is highest for services that compile modules written by other people. Fastly, for example, says its Compute platform runs customer code using Wasmtime. The advisories do not name any affected services or specify what an attacker could access after escaping the sandbox.

Neither flaw was in the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog kept by the U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) as of October 26.

The two flaws were fixed at different times, so whether your version is affected depends on your release line and, for one flaw, your processor architecture.

Wasmtime supports long-term support (LTS) releases, which are numbered in multiples of 12, for two years. Other releases get two months. Security fixes are guaranteed for supported releases.

Only the 36, 48, 49, and 50 release lines are still supported. Every other line, including the 24.x LTS line whose support ended on August 20, 2026, got no fix.

ARM64 hosts that updated to 36.0.7, 42.0.2, or 43.0.1 in April to fix an earlier Cranelift sandbox escape on ARM64 are still affected by CVE-2026-115893.

Update to the fixed release for your line: 36.0.19, 48.0.7, or 49.0.4. Wasmtime 50.0.0, released on October 20, is fixed for both flaws. Hosts on an unsupported line need to move to one of these releases.

Wasmtime can be built into other programs as a library, so updating the command-line tool does not fix them. Each program that includes Wasmtime needs to be rebuilt with a fixed version.

Neither advisory lists a workaround. Until a host is updated, the risk comes from any module an outsider can supply.

Both flaws are listed in RustSec, the database the cargo-audit tool uses to check Cargo.lock files, as RUSTSEC-2026-0380 and RUSTSEC-2026-0379.

As of October 26, GitHub's advisory database had a reviewed entry for CVE-2026-115889 but none for CVE-2026-115893. GitHub says only reviewed entries trigger Dependabot alerts, so Dependabot users should not expect an alert for the ARM64 flaw.

Spin, an open-source tool for building serverless WebAssembly apps that runs on Wasmtime, moved to Wasmtime 50.0.0 in version 4.2.4 on October 21.

CVE-2026-115889 comes from how Cranelift stored the numbers it uses to track values inside a function. Before version 43, it packed these numbers into 24-bit fields, and in release builds, a number too large for its field overflowed without any error.

Cranelift could then refer to the wrong value, producing incorrect machine code. A very large WebAssembly function, or one crafted to use many values, can use this to force type confusion and escape the sandbox, according to the advisory.

Wasmtime's main development line had already removed the problem. A change merged on February 18 widened the fields to 32 bits and shipped in Wasmtime 43.0.0 on March 20. Its description presented it as a way to compile larger functions and did not mention security.

The 36.x LTS line got the same change on October 19. The advisory does not say when the security impact was recognized.

CVE-2026-115893 involves br_table, a WebAssembly instruction that jumps to one of many places in the code based on an index. An index outside the table goes to a default target. On ARM64, Cranelift sent these out-of-range indexes to the default with a branch that can only reach about 1 MiB.

A br_table with more than about 256,000 targets made the jump table too big for that reach, so the branch silently pointed to the wrong place.

Any Wasmtime engine on ARM64 with default settings "can be forced to jump to arbitrary code by a user-controlled Wasm module," the advisory says. The bug has been present since Wasmtime first supported ARM64 in 2020.

The fix sends out-of-range indexes through the jump table itself, so no long branch is needed. Fixed versions also refuse to compile a br_table with more than 65,520 targets.

Between alerts: What Attackers Do in the Spaces Ordinary Detection Misses

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Every SOC struggles with a pile of alerts. Very few of those alerts explain how an attack started or how it progressed. Network data, like that from a network detection and response (NDR) platform, connects individual alerts into one timeline, and it feeds the defensive systems that can tie all events together into one coordinated view.

SOC teams often first see the shape of an intrusion through a series of smaller alerts that are triggered (e.g., when a signature matches a known malware hash, a threshold flags an unusual spike in failed logins, or an endpoint agent detects suspicious activity). When viewed through the SIEM dashboard, an attack often begins as a series of distinct events with related or overlapping timestamps.

Artificial intelligence has accelerated modern attacks, enabling attackers to automate reconnaissance, craft targeted payloads, and execute scripts in seconds. Yet, speed does not obfuscate the need for stealth. To evade detection, adversaries deliberately draw out operations, hide inside standard admin tooling, and work in the quiet intervals between alarms.

Adversaries might spend days or weeks inside an enterprise environment before an action generates a high-priority security alert. When an alert finally surfaces, it doesn’t necessarily mark the start of a compromise. It may, in fact, be one event in a much longer sequence. Analysts who investigate only the triggering event may miss the chained activity that explains how the intrusion developed.

Between initial access and a high-severity alert, adversary activity can blend into ordinary business operations. Adversaries understand the operational limits of security teams, and they exploit them; if every DNS query, LDAP request, SMB connection, or authentication attempt generated an investigation, SOC queues would quickly become (even more) unmanageable.

Tradecraft purposely takes advantage of that reality.

Adversaries can spread reconnaissance across hours or distributed sources. Instead of launching an obvious scan, they may use a compromised account to query a domain controller over LDAP at a modest pace to map users, groups, and systems. A handful of Kerberos service ticket requests can yield credentials to crack offline, well outside the SOC’s view. Valid credentials then open the door to SMB or RDP, the same services administrators rely on every day, and the attacker moves from host to host. Files may be gathered on one internal system and compressed. Later, that system may reach an external destination over HTTPS or DNS at regular intervals, with just enough variation in timing to resemble normal traffic as it exfiltrates data.

Each step borrows something the business already depends on.

While individual actions may not necessarily look suspicious, their significance often emerges from relationships and sequences. For instance, a host that communicates with a new system once may be unremarkable. A host that queries several internal services, authenticates to a peer, establishes an SMB session, and then connects to an external destination presents a different investigative picture. The activity becomes more meaningful when viewed across time.

When siloed tools see only slices of this type of transaction, they might miss the bigger picture. Context only becomes readily apparent when activities and signals are combined and correlated via network observation.

What’s more, attackers increasingly use AI to automate reconnaissance and move faster. Defenders need to apply AI to the same problem, but it needs detailed network evidence from an NDR to search.

SecOps teams don’t have to turn detection thresholds down and drown analysts in more alerts just to spot adversary activity. Doing so would be untenable anyway. A SOC can’t investigate every DNS query, authentication request, or connection as though it were an incident. In addition, many teams operate with tools that show an alert fired yet offer sparse detail on how the activity behind it unfolded.

A rentable Android spyware kit called uCreator takes over Telegram and WeChat accounts by hiding its code inside working copies of the two apps, researcher Ovi found. It only reaches people who install one of these tampered copies, spread outside the official app stores, or who run WeChat inside the kit makers' own container app.

The kit's Telegram part matches Baohuo, a backdoor that Doctor Web reported in October 2025. Doctor Web found Baohuo in tampered copies of Telegram X, an Android app from Telegram. At the time, the company estimated that more than 58,000 devices were infected.

The Hacker News compared the two reports. Doctor Web's technical description of Baohuo lists the same code namespace, com.ucreator.tgjar, the same command-and-control (C2) server, and matching command names as Ovi's analysis.

Ovi's analysis, published October 21, 2026, does not mention Doctor Web's findings, and Doctor Web did not name the kit. It is not clear whether the kit's makers or one of their customers ran the campaign Doctor Web tracked.

The tampered apps spread through sideloading, where a person installs an app file directly instead of from an app store, and through social engineering, Ovi found. He could not confirm how every victim was infected.

A second route is the kit makers' own container app, called 洋葱盒子 ("Onion Box"), which runs other apps inside itself.

Some of the tampered Telegram X copies use the same package name as the real app on Google Play, so the name alone does not give them away.

Doctor Web found that Baohuo copies had been spreading since mid-2024 through in-app ads that led to fake app store sites. Those sites promoted Telegram X as a dating and video chat app.

The sites' banners were available only in Portuguese, for users in Brazil, and in Indonesian. Even so, Doctor Web's 2025 map of infected devices by country put India first, at 22.8%, ahead of Brazil at 20.5% and Indonesia at 9.6%.

Baohuo copies also turned up in the third-party stores APKPure, ApkSum and AndroidP. On APKPure, one was listed under the official developer's name, although its digital signature was different.

Ovi got access to one of the kit's command servers. It held data on victims apparently located in China and the United States, but not enough for a reliable count. He calls his report "an analysis of technical capability, not a measurement of victim impact."

The apparent targets, still unconfirmed, are users of mobile wallets and messaging apps in China and Southeast Asia, including WeCom, QQ, WhatsApp, LINE, Zalo, KakaoTalk, Viber, GCash and MoMo. He found code supporting about 20 apps, but has published code only for Telegram and WeChat.

The kit's hooking engine is the part that lets its code intercept an app's functions. Ovi checked it on a Pixel 10 Pro running Android 17. It could not find an internal Android function it relies on, because newer Android versions changed that function.

Another tool, Frida, could still hook apps the same way on that phone. Ovi therefore describes this as extra work for the kit's makers, not proof that Android 17 blocks the attack.

The newest sample, dated July 29, 2026, still carried the old engine. He recovered samples built for Android 16 but did not report testing that version. He has not tested phones from Chinese makers.

Inside a tampered Telegram app, the kit's code starts with the app and runs with the app's own identity and permissions, Ovi found. That gives it the same access as the signed-in app.

Each tampered app also runs a local command server inside itself. The code for each target app is downloaded after installation, which makes the installer file harder to analyze on its own.

Attackers can send any database query to the app's message store and get chat history back.

Telegram's two-step verification (2FA) password is entered in the app, which uses it to generate a login proof on the phone. The kit hooks that step and copies the password in readable form before the app hashes it.

The code can also hide the attacker's session from the device list that the tampered app shows the user.

Doctor Web's description of Baohuo covers the same device-hiding. The backdoor also sends the account's phone number, name and password to its server every three minutes.

Neither Ovi nor Doctor Web says whether a hidden session still appears on the user's other, untampered Telegram apps. Neither gives steps for securing an account taken over this way.

Ovi says the code shows what the kit can do, not that attackers have used every step from start to finish.

This part goes after the account itself, not its chat history.

Ovi and another researcher, Jurre, also found the back-end code behind the kit on a self-hosted GitLab server. It was the one part of the setup not limited to approved IP addresses.

Commands go to phones in the kit's phone farm, a set of phones the operation controls, and to victims' phones. There, the kit captures login authorizations, adds friends in bulk, collects contact and profile data, and backs up whole accounts.

A scheduler sets time windows, random daily limits, and per-account budgets, so the activity looks human and gets past the platforms' risk checks. The code calls this "account cultivation," in Ovi's translation.

About three dozen developers, all working on China time, appear in the code's history, and some were still adding changes in October 2026.

The leaked back end covers personal WeChat and WeCom accounts. Ovi says it did not appear to cover Telegram, and only one of several GitLab servers was exposed.

Microsoft 365 Token-Replay Attacks Compromise 97 Accounts at 79 Organizations

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Cybersecurity researchers have disclosed a Microsoft 365 campaign that has been observed leveraging an adversary-in-the-middle (AitM) relay to capture sign-in sessions and then replay the stolen tokens to break into email accounts at 79 organizations.

Huntress, which observed the activity between July 14 and September 22, 2026, said it detected 497 successful Microsoft 365 sign-in events across the victim organizations from over 200,000 login attempts, with the attackers ultimately compromising 97 accounts and registering 48 rogue Entra devices.

"Rather than guessing passwords, the attacker replayed sign-in tokens stolen from victims, and we've traced more than 200,000 failed login attempts from the campaign window back to the 193.26.115[.]0/24 IP range," Huntress researchers Andrea Hatcher, Susannah Matt, Matt Poto, and Andrew Schwartz said.

The attack has been found to employ an AitM login page, which sits between the victims and legitimate Microsoft infrastructure to capture the entered credentials and MFA codes and relay the information to the real login page.

The end goal is to collect the access token issued by Microsoft following the sign-in process so that it doesn't have to bypass MFA a second time. The "proof of login" token is subsequently replayed by the attacker from their own devices within minutes to conduct automated sign-in attempts against Microsoft 365 services.

The approach has been codenamed token spraying, as it involves the attacker replaying many stolen tokens at scale across several organizations in a manner that's analogous to password spraying.

Upon gaining successful access, the attackers moved to add their own authenticator app as a form of persistence to complete MFA challenges for new sign-ins and overcome scenarios where the stolen session token expires or gets revoked by the victim.

In no less than 19 organizations, the victim's existing MFA methods were removed and a new method registered within three hours of the first sign-in. In one case observed on July 21, 2026, the threat actors activated a new authenticator app named "iPhone 13" less than 51 minutes after the session was hijacked.

"Rogue devices served the same purpose," the researchers said. "The threat actor registered at least 48 fake computers in victim organizations' Microsoft directories. A registered device can give the attacker a more durable way to request tokens than the stolen session alone, and it stays in the directory until someone deletes it."

Furthermore, the attackers have also been spotted setting up hidden email rules in victims' inboxes with names such as ".," "sss," "ss," "iT," ",.," "........," "s," and "d" to delete incoming mail with certain keywords referencing payroll, deposit, and bonus, or move them to obscure folders like RSS Feeds and Conversation History.

In one compromised mailbox, the email rule marked all incoming messages as read and deleted them. The mailbox was then used to send outbound spam to hundreds of external recipients before Microsoft Entra ID Protection rated the account high risk.

Huntress said the tokens originate from multiple sources, including AitM relays behind fake sign-in pages, device-code phishing, and social engineering during a legitimate login process.

The campaign was first observed from a Romanian IP address "193.26.115[.]221," which, according to VirusTotal, is used by a remote access trojan called AsyncRAT. The IP address is associated with a German hosting provider named 1337 Services GmbH.

Data from GreyNoise currently lists 29 IP addresses matching the hosting provider, out of which 10 have been flagged as malicious and five are marked suspicious. Notably, the entity is linked to StarkRDP and rdp[.]sh, which allows paying customers to rent virtual servers.

The domains tied to both StarkRDP and rdp[.]sh were taken down, alongside websites for Nulled, Cracked, and Sellix, as part of an international law enforcement operation dubbed Talent in January 2025. As of writing, the two platforms are operational, with the former now rebranded as exy[.]sh.

"StarkRDP has always been operating by the law and is not involved in any of these alleged crimes, and the legal process will confirm this," the StarkRDP Telegram account said in a post dated January 30, according to independent cybersecurity journalist Brian Krebs.

"The only things that were seized is [sic] the website server and our domain. Unfortunately, no one can tell who took it and with whom we can talk about it. Therefore, we will restart operation soon, under a different name, to close the chapter [of] 'StarkRDP.'"

Organizations who suspect a token replay compromise are recommended to revoke sessions and reset the password, audit Entra devices, review MFA methods, check for unexpected inbox rules or new enterprise applications, and restrict device-code workflow with Conditional Access policies.

"Conditional Access Policies (CAP) can help fill some of the security gaps that MFA can't, as long as they're appropriately implemented," Huntress researchers said. "Businesses setting up CAPs should require MFA (or block) for All Users, All Cloud Apps, and All Client App types, unconditionally."

Gheist Uses Stolen GitHub Tokens to Steal Private Repository Secrets via Actions Logs

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An automated campaign is using stolen GitHub tokens to plant workflows that print a repository's secrets into its GitHub Actions logs, which the attackers then download before deleting the runs. The attacks have succeeded in private repositories and GitHub Enterprise environments, according to an analysis by cloud security company Wiz.

A GitHub safeguard that pauses suspicious workflow runs stopped every public-repository run Wiz checked, but it does not cover private repositories or GitHub Enterprise Server.

Each workflow sits on a new branch named ci-sync- followed by six random characters. Deleting a branch, or the run it started, does not undo the exposure. Every secret the repository could read should be rotated, and the token that pushed the branch revoked.

Wiz has tracked the campaign since September 15 and calls it Gheist, after the user agent gheist that the attackers' tool sends with GitHub API requests.

Wiz has recovered more than 13,500 planted branches from more than 850 compromised accounts, from personal projects to the private repositories of enterprise organizations. Wiz's analysis does not say how many organizations had secrets exposed.

In private repositories and on GitHub Enterprise Server, GitHub audit logs show runs on the planted branches finishing and then being deleted by the gheist user agent. AWS CloudTrail logs show the organization's own workflows also running on those branches and assuming AWS roles, a sign that GitHub Actions ran there without approval.

In enterprise organizations, the tool cycled through tokens from several users and automation accounts within minutes and cloned private repositories before planting the workflow. Hours later, cloud credentials from the same organization were used to enumerate AWS Secrets Manager. Wiz's analysis does not say where those credentials came from or who used them.

As of October 24, the campaign was still running and changing. About two-thirds of the accounts involved were no longer available on GitHub. Wiz reported the activity to GitHub and says their disappearance likely reflects a response.

Anyone with write access to a GitHub repository can read every secret configured in it, according to GitHub's documentation. A stolen token with that access is enough.

For each repository a token can reach, the tool creates a ci-sync- branch and commits one file, .github/workflows/sync-check.yml, with the message ci: sync config. The commit is unsigned and includes the token owner's name; pushing it starts the workflow.

The workflow prints the job's environment and every secret the job can read, encoded in base32. GitHub masks secrets in logs, but its documentation says this is not guaranteed once a value has been encoded or otherwise changed. Wiz says the base32 text no longer matches the original secret, so it prints in full.

The workflow sets an empty permissions block. That limits the job's GITHUB_TOKEN, which the attackers do not need, and does not stop the job from reading secrets.

One version of the tool also runs a job for each deployment environment in the repository, so secrets limited to those environments print too. These are often production credentials, according to Wiz.

The tool then downloads the log through GitHub's API and deletes the run seconds later. Because the secrets leave through GitHub itself, there is no attacker server or outbound connection to block.

Since July 28, GitHub has held workflow runs it identifies as potentially malicious until a collaborator with write access approves them. GitHub says the hold "currently applies to public repositories on github.com only" and that GitHub Enterprise Server does not have it at this time.

Every public Gheist run Wiz checked, across all versions of the tool, was still waiting for approval, so its payload never ran.

The hold only catches runs that GitHub flags. A new variant of GhostAction, a separate campaign, ran in public repositories until GitHub began blocking it on October 9, according to research from security company GitGuardian. Wiz says Gheist is distinct from GhostAction, which uses different workflow files.

Around October 19, one version of the tool began pushing a second job meant for self-hosted runners, the machines organizations run themselves to process GitHub Actions jobs. It appears to target only repositories that have such a runner registered.

The job is written to search the runner machine for credentials, including 31 types of tokens: private keys, process environments, AWS instance role credentials, runner credential files, and the Kubernetes service account token. Wiz describes the job as buggy, and its analysis reports no successful run.

The sources Wiz could trace for the stolen tokens include infostealer logs and tokens exposed in earlier supply-chain attacks, such as the March compromise of the Trivy vulnerability scanner.

Other attackers also use the same accounts. On October 6, 391 accounts, many of them also used by Gheist, renamed about 1,461 repositories to appear to be cracked software and spread malware. The renaming wiped 610 of the planted branches.

Some victims also appear to be infected with PolinRider, malware that researchers have linked to North Korea. On October 19 and 20, three of them re-pushed their Gheist commits with a loader added to a vite.config.js file.

Megalodon, a campaign that pushed secret-stealing workflows into thousands of GitHub repositories in May, had also hit 57 of the accounts.

Versions of the workflow that list a repository's deployment environments vary by repository, so they have no fixed hash.

Scope your Personal Access Tokens

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A Personal Access Token can do anything you can do in your Supabase account, across every organization and project you belong to. Until now, every tool you gave one to could access everything in your account, even when it only needed to perform a single, read-only task. Your CI pipelines, deployment scripts, and AI coding agents all have different jobs and their access should reflect that.

Now you can scope a token so what it's permitted to do matches the tool's job. If a coding agent only needs to read one project's database, you hand it a token limited to that project and that one read permission.

Token scoping became generally available on September 28, and we announced it at Supabase Select on October 2. Every token you create in the Dashboard is now scoped by default, although you can still create one with full account access if you need to. Nearly nine out of 10 tokens created there since launch have been scoped.

Take the coding agent from above. It needs to inspect tables, generate types, and run read-only SQL, but it shouldn't be able to write to your database or access other projects. A deploy script would get a separate token with the write permissions its workflow needs.

Head to your access tokens page to build the agent's token. Choose its resources, permissions, and expiry, then review its access:

You can grant access across multiple organizations, but the Dashboard recommends a project-level scope. Since the agent is only working on one app, select that single project.

Next, you define what the token can do within that project. The Dashboard offers presets that set all permissions at once (No access, Read-only, and Full access), but because "Read-only" includes secrets like API keys, start with the No access preset. From there, toggle "Database" to read.

You should never let a temporary tool hold a permanent key. You can set a custom expiration date up to one year out. If the agent's work is done in a week, its token should be too.

Before you hit create, the review screen shows you what you're authorizing. Expand any permission to see the specific Management API endpoints and MCP tools it covers. For the agent, "Database read" covers the tools needed to list tables, generate types, and run read-only SQL.

You'll also see a risk banner rating the token's combination of reach and write access as Low, Medium, or High.

Copy the token value before closing the page because it's shown only once. Its scope and permissions are fixed at creation. To change them, create a replacement token.

A token's permissions are strictly bounded by your own user account. Every request evaluates both the token's scope and your current user role.

If you leave an organization or lose permission to read a project's database, the agent loses that access too. If your access is later restored, the token works again. The token's detail view marks any resource you can no longer reach.

The same check applies when you create a token. The Dashboard flags any permission you select that your current role doesn't have. You can still add it to the token but the token can't use it until your role includes it.

A token created with a scope starts with the prefix sbp_fc and works in your existing workflows using standard bearer authentication.

If an API request is missing a permission, the 403 response names it in a missing_permissions field. Check that your own role has that permission and then add it to the replacement token. A request for a project outside the token's scope gets a plain 403.

Each tool checks permissions before executing and returns a tool error if denied. With the agent's Database read permission it can use execute_sql for read-only queries. A SQL write returns a Postgres permission error rather than a 403. Running apply_migration needs a separate Migrations read-write permission.

The browser flow of supabase login still creates a token with full account access today but we're updating it to create one with the scope you choose. Until then, create the token in the Dashboard and pass it with supabase login --token. For CI environments, set the SUPABASE_ACCESS_TOKEN environment variable which overrides any locally saved tokens. Database commands that rely on SUPABASE_DB_PASSWORD bypass token permissions entirely. If your CI workflow includes supabase link, your token also needs read access to Project Settings, API Keys, and API Key Secrets.

For a full map of permissions to endpoints and MCP tools, see the Personal Access Tokens documentation.

Your existing integrations keep working, and no deprecation or shutoff date applies to them. Every existing token works until it expires or you delete it.

In the Dashboard, tokens with full account access carry a "Legacy" badge, and the "Create legacy token" option is still there for a workflow that needs it.

Choose an agent or integration and create a token for it, limited to its project and the permissions its job needs. If it already has a token with full account access, create a replacement and swap it in.

If a permission you need is missing, or the creation flow doesn't fit your workflow, submit a support ticket.

Our Response To The eMudhra Key Disclosure Incident

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Stories about how and why companies use Go

Roland Shoemaker, on behalf of the Go Security team 26 October 2026

On October 19, the Go team initiated an expedited security release to protect Go users and their applications from a critical public key infrastructure (PKI) compromise involving certificates issued by eMudhra.

Within approximately seven and a half hours of being notified, we shipped a targeted patch, which was included in our first-ever URGENT track security release.

Here is the context behind the incident, why Go stepped in at the runtime level to protect our users and how our incident response unfolded.

On October 17, Mozilla added eight intermediate certificates (six used for TLS, and two used for code signing) issued by a root certificate controlled by eMudhra to OneCRL, effectively blocking their usage in Firefox. Soon after, a number of security researchers noticed this and began investigating what might’ve caused this.

On October 19, the Go Security team was made aware of these investigations and their conclusions, which showed that the keys associated with these intermediates were trivially weak, allowing recovery of the private keys in microseconds.

Due to the fact that the eMudhra root certificate was present in most trusted root programs, recovering the private keys would allow any attacker to sign arbitrary TLS certificates for any domain name, without the usual requirement to show proof of control of said domain name. This would allow an attacker to man-in-the-middle (MITM) TLS connections and present a valid certificate for a domain name they did not control, effectively allowing them to impersonate any TLS server the targeted machine attempted to connect to.

Go ships a TLS certificate verifier in the standard library. When users use net/http or crypto/tls to connect to a TLS service, the verifier is called to check if the certificates the other side of the connection presents are valid. On Windows and macOS we delegate these checks to the platform APIs, and on Linux, which lacks platform APIs, we use our own verifier implementation.

Since Linux does not have a uniform built-in mechanism to handle revocation checking, and in order to provide a timely fix for users on other platforms that might not receive platform updates, we decided the best way to protect Go users was to do an URGENT track security release which similarly removed trust.

A typical Go security release follows a multi-day process, with advanced pre-announcements in order to allow those who rely on Go to prepare. In this case, because we believed that private key exposure was imminent, our team chose to use the URGENT track for the first time in order to ship protections before active public exploitation could begin.

Our Security team was alerted by an independent external researcher, citing unreported actions by Mozilla and other organizations to remove trust in eight eMudhra intermediates. It became clear quickly that this was due to weak private keys they used.

Mozilla independently reported the issue to our Security team, confirming the issue, and asking that we not immediately disclose the issue, but encouraging us to release a fix as soon as possible.

We finalized a patch which blocked the usage of certificates which chained to the compromised intermediates, and thanks to the researcher who initially alerted us to the issue, had a set of certificates signed using the recovered keys to test against.

We began the process to release minor Go versions 1.27.3 and 1.26.10 containing our patch. Due to the urgent nature of this issue and its exploitability, and with the belief that the private keys would likely become publicly known within hours, we decided an immediate release was the most prudent option.

We finalized the release and announced it publicly. At this time the underlying issue was still not widely known. In an effort to give other defenders a chance to protect as many users as possible without revealing to attackers how the issue could be exploited, our patch and communications were somewhat opaque, warning users that we were “blocking trust in a small set of intermediate certificates due to specific security concerns around their operation” and urging users to immediately upgrade to this release.

In under seven and a half hours from initial notification, patched Go releases were live worldwide, giving users everything they needed to secure their deployments before the keys became public.

By the morning of October 20, numerous independent researchers had publicly disclosed the weak keys.

This was our first ever URGENT track security release, and during the release process we ran into a number of issues which caused the release to take longer than expected.

While we are happy we were able to get a release out the same day we were notified of the issue, in the modern security environment we feel that we should be able to provide releases in a more compressed timeline. The issues we ran into caused our release process to take just under four and a quarter hours from start to finish.

These issues had previously not been problematic, since the typical release process is split up over a number of days.

We’ve subsequently made a number of changes to improve our release efficiency, which should significantly reduce the time it takes us to push out a release, enhancing our ability to quickly respond to urgent security threats.

PRIVATE track security fixes are pre-announced three to seven days before their release. URGENT track fixes happen on a shorter time schedule, but we could have pre-announced the upcoming release when we began the release process, giving users at least a few hours to prepare.

While on Windows and macOS we rely on the revocation mechanisms the platform APIs provide, on Linux we don’t currently have a similar mechanism. The lack of this support was one of our major drivers to issue an expedited security release, as we did not want to leave Linux users exposed to this issue longer than was necessary.

Hence we are planning on prioritizing work to bring parity to revocation support on Linux, to help mitigate this class of issues in the future. Part of this work will involve investigating adding support for the upki project, which aims to “provide reliable, privacy-preserving and efficient certificate revocation” for Linux (see issue #82402).

If you run Go binaries which interact with network services using TLS (such as directly via net/http or crypto/tls or via a dependency such as gRPC), ensure you are running on the latest minor releases. Binaries rebuilt with Go 1.27.3 and 1.26.10 will automatically reject certificates issued by the compromised eMudhra intermediates.

You can verify your current toolchain version using go version and you can check the version of Go a binary was built with using go version -m .

The govulncheck tool can also be used to determine if a binary is affected by this issue:

For us, securing Go and its users extends beyond fixing direct security vulnerabilities in the runtime and libraries we ship. In order to keep the ecosystem secure, we believe we also need to provide users with the right tools to write secure software, look towards the future of cryptographic algorithms and protocols, and proactively respond to ecosystem wide issues when we can.

We are grateful to the independent security researchers and the Mozilla security team for this rapid notification and coordination during this incident.

How to fund Rust contributors

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There is always so much to do in the Rust Project! New features to implement, bugs to fix, performance optimizations to make, issues to triage… It takes a lot of work to keep our high bar of quality while also shipping new features and improvements. And the workload keeps increasing with the continuous growth of Rust adoption.

As is unfortunately common in open source projects, a large fraction of that work is currently done by volunteers. That causes various issues, such as people burning out or complex features taking many years to ship.

Unsurprisingly, one of the best ways to help improve that is to get more people who work on Rust funded! This year, several funding initiatives started ramping up, and we already experience many positive effects. They helped us improve the maintenance status of critical Rust projects and make significant progress on several long-awaited features, such as allocators, the never type, the next trait solver or the Polonius borrow checker. However, only a fraction of people working on Rust are currently funded for their work.

We know that there are Rust users, both individuals and companies, who would like to help improve Rust by funding it, but it might not always be obvious how exactly to do that. This post thus provides suggestions on how you can support the development of Rust in three ways.

If you're unsure which option fits, or would like to discuss funding on behalf of your company, please contact the Funding team.

Maintenance is a continuous process required to keep Rust (or any software project for that matter) working. It consists of two main activities. The first is general housekeeping, such as ensuring that Continuous Integration works, dependencies are kept up-to-date and issues, bugs and security incidents are promptly triaged and resolved. The second is performing refactorings and code reviews, which ensures that the codebase will be able to evolve and continuously ship new features, without accruing extensive technical debt.

Having quality maintenance is crucial for the long-term health of Rust, yet it is typically very difficult to find funding for it; funders often want to sponsor specific new features, rather than just "keeping the lights on". However, without sufficient maintenance, it becomes much more difficult, and much slower, to ship new features. Improving the maintenance of Rust thus also helps us ship features and improvements faster, though the effects are sometimes only observable over longer periods of time.

Funding maintenance is a great choice if you want to let us decide how best to improve Rust. The Rust Funding team continuously looks for Rust teams and projects that require help with their maintenance efforts, and allocates available funds to Rust maintainers who could help improve the situation.

To fund Rust maintainers, consider donating to the Rust Foundation Maintainers Fund or sponsoring the RustNL Maintainers Team.

There are always many Rust improvements and new features that are in progress. We track some of those initiatives through Rust Project Goals. Each Goal describes a specific improvement to Rust: what it is, who is going to develop and drive it forward and which Rust teams are willing to help reviewing and maintaining it.

Some of those Goals are looking for funding. Either to help their owner dedicate time and focus to push the given feature forward, or to fund maintainers who will perform refactorings necessary to unblock that feature and review its implementation. Often funding is required for both. When a Goal is properly funded, the probability that it will achieve its milestones is much higher, which then translates in the given improvement being shipped sooner to Rust users.

Funding Project Goals is a great choice if you want to help push forward specific Rust features or Rust toolchain improvements that will benefit you or your company.

To fund contributors working on Project Goals, consider taking a look at Goals that need funding and reaching out to their funding contact. If in doubt how to proceed, reach out to the Rust Funding team.

There are hundreds of people in the Rust Project who continuously work on improving Rust. Some of them have personal fundraising profiles (e.g. on GitHub Sponsors), which allows you to support them directly.

There might be various reasons for supporting a specific person. Maybe they are maintaining a crate that you love to use, maybe you like reading their blog posts, or maybe you are simply a fan of the work that they do. Whatever the reason, we are sure that they would appreciate your support!

Funding individual contributors is a great choice if you want to directly support Rust contributors whose work you appreciate.

To fund individual Rust contributors, check out their GitHub profile or personal website to see what options there are for supporting them. You can also examine our list of Rust Project members who can be supported through GitHub Sponsors[1].

We hope that this post is useful to anyone who would potentially like to invest funds into making Rust better. You can also find a shortened version of the "how to fund Rust guide" at the Funding page of the Rust website, or check out a RustConf 2026 talk by Niko Matsakis about how Project Goals and their funding works.

If you are a company looking to fund the development of Rust, consider joining the Rust Commercial Network. Joining the network is free, and Rust Foundation membership is not required. It can help you find opportunities for funding improvements in specific areas related to Rust usage (e.g. user interfaces, language interop, supply chain security, etc.) and collaborate on funding Rust development with other companies.

There are of course other ways of helping Rust other than donating funds. For example, you can check out Project Goals that are looking for contributors or more generally check our contributor onboarding guide. Companies can also help by giving employees paid time to contribute upstream, or by hiring Rust contributors to do that work. One possible model is the Engineer in Residence role, that was recently established in the Rust Foundation.

Beyond API Compatibility: Building a Tool to Measure the Blast Radius of Library Changes

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

By Roberto Perez Alcolea, Java Platform Team

At Netflix, shared Java libraries support applications developed across a large, independently evolving repository ecosystem. A library change begins in one repository, but its impact can reach many others. Producers can see the API diff in front of them. What they often cannot see is who still depends on the symbols that changed.

The need for that visibility grows as coding agents take on more of the software development lifecycle. An agent can accelerate a local edit, but without cross-repository context it cannot judge downstream risk or help plan consumer migrations. The evidence a producer uses to assess a change can also guide the agent working on it.

Consider a common case: removing a deprecated API. Deprecated entry points expand the surface producers must test, document, and support, and they can constrain how a library evolves.

Suppose a replacement has been available for several releases and the old method cannot express options required by new behavior. The library’s tests pass, and a compatibility checker flags the removal. The producer still cannot answer the questions that determine what to do next:

  • Does any consumer still call this method?
  • Are consumers concentrated on one version or spread across several generations?
  • Does another library implement or wrap the API, multiplying its impact?
  • Is direct coordination sufficient, or does this require a deprecation and migration campaign?

At small scale, producers can ask nearby teams or search a handful of repositories. Across many repositories, it is harder to find every caller of a method and the library version each one uses. Those answers are scattered across consumer codebases and builds.

Without that evidence, a producer may release a breaking change and discover the impact through consumer failures, or preserve an old API indefinitely because the risk is unknown. Libraries need to evolve, and their producers need evidence that helps them do so intentionally.

We built Quasar to index symbol definitions and references across Netflix repositories. For library producers, that shared index helps answer two questions:

  1. What changed in the library’s public API?
  2. Which repositories use the exact symbols that changed?

The first identifies a potentially incompatible change. The second estimates its observed blast radius. Together, they turn a generic compatibility warning into evidence a producer can act on before merging or releasing.

From API Changes to Consumer Impact

Existing techniques each see a different part of the problem. Binary and source compatibility tools identify contract changes, but cannot tell who uses the changed API. Dependency graphs show declared reach, but cannot prove that code calls a particular method. Text search may miss overloads, inheritance, or the artifact version behind a call. Consumer builds provide strong validation, but running them across the fleet is expensive.

Quasar builds on SCIP, an open interchange format for semantic code intelligence. Language-specific indexers encode definitions, references, and relationships using stable symbol identifiers. Quasar retains API definitions for library versions and commits alongside references from consumer repositories. That shared identity lets it connect an API change in one repository to exact usages elsewhere, even when those repositories were indexed independently.

On each enrolled CI build, language-specific indexers submit SCIP indexes to Quasar’s ingestion service. It writes API definitions and consumer references as Parquet in Amazon S3, retaining the symbol identity, dependency version, and source location needed to investigate a change.

During pull-request review, Quasar compares indexed base and head definitions and counts direct consumer references with Amazon Athena. Quasar exposes those findings through its REST and GraphQL APIs and MCP server; follow-up usage queries provide dependency-version breakdowns and source locations. A separate compatibility-impact assessment can also follow implementations and their callers when a producer supplies a changed JVM symbol.

Consider this simplified API change:

public interface Client {
- Result getLegacyValue(Request request);
+ Result getValue(Request request, Options options);
}

For a detailed compatibility-impact assessment of this removal, a producer-side tool supplies the old method descriptor. Quasar constructs its canonical symbol identity. This abridged excerpt from the direct-reference portion of Quasar’s Amazon Athena query shows the essential match:

SELECT c.change_id, 'DIRECT_REFERENCE' AS evidence_kind,
       r.scm_type, r.org, r.repo, r.dep_version, r.file_path
FROM requested_changes c
JOIN quasar.symbol_refs r
  ON r.symbol_canonical = c.target_canonical

requested_changes contains the API changes submitted by the producer. The SQL excerpt finds repositories that call the removed method. Quasar also checks implementations and calls through them, so it can count each affected repository once. It returns sample dependency versions and file locations to help investigate the result.

For the Client change, this tells the producer which repositories still use getLegacyValue, which versions they use, and where some of those calls occur. That helps the producer decide whether to coordinate a migration before removing the method.

Bringing the Analysis Before Release

Version-to-version comparison is valuable when planning an upgrade or writing a migration guide. Producers benefit even more when feedback arrives during code review.

For pull-request analysis, Quasar stores API definition snapshots keyed by commit. Given a base and head commit, it classifies symbols as added, removed, or signature-modified. Removals and modifications then become candidates for consumer-impact analysis.

An incompatible change is not automatically a high-risk change. Quasar reports the observed impact so the producer can decide what to do; it does not enforce a release policy. “No references observed” means only that Quasar found none in the indexed population. Reflection, configuration, dynamic loading, stale branches, or incomplete indexing can hide consumers. The result needs to show whether the requested commits were indexed, how fresh the consumer indexes are, and whether evidence was truncated.

Pull-request review requires indexed snapshots for the exact base and head commits. If either is unavailable, Quasar reports that it cannot compare them; it does not substitute a default-branch snapshot. This keeps a missing comparison from looking like a clean API diff.

Return to our Client change. This illustrative report brings together signals available through Quasar's API comparison, consumer-usage queries, and library-level criticality. It is not a literal response from one API:

Together, those signals change the producer’s plan. Instead of merging the removal and waiting for failures, the producer can retain and deprecate the old method, publish a migration path, coordinate a broader migration, and track the long-tail versions before removal. File paths and available line numbers turn that scope into a concrete work list for engineers or coding agents. The same API diff paired with weak or stale evidence would lead to a different action: refresh the indexes before deciding. The value is not merely detecting the removal; it is choosing a proportionate next step.

A Polyglot Foundation with SCIP

The shared SCIP format also lets Quasar index several language ecosystems. Each language indexer resolves its own code, while Quasar can use the resulting symbol identities to find references across repositories.

Conceptually, client-lib indexes Client#getLegacyValue(Request) as a definition, while checkout-service indexes a reference to the same symbol. Quasar can then connect the library API to its consumer across repositories.

Relationships are especially useful when generated code separates the API a producer owns from the symbols consumers invoke. For example, Quasar can trace a Protobuf definition through its generated Java members to the repositories that use them, even when consumer code never names the .proto element.

SCIP records symbols and their references; it does not determine whether an API change violates an ecosystem’s compatibility rules. Today, Quasar compares indexed JVM and Protobuf API definitions and traces Protobuf changes through generated Java to find affected repositories. A separate JVM compatibility-impact path uses breaks identified by a producer-side tool to query affected repositories. Go, Python, JavaScript/TypeScript, and Rust support usage queries, but Quasar does not yet assess their breaking API changes.

Moving Risk Assessment Earlier

Today, Quasar surfaces API-change findings on pull requests, including observed consumer counts and confidence. Producers can use its other queries to inspect dependency versions and source locations, with library criticality as broader context. Together, those signals support a decision before merge.

We want to move that context earlier. An agent writing library code should recognize when it is changing a public symbol and consult Quasar before the change becomes a pull request. Quasar’s APIs and Model Context Protocol (MCP) integration provide an access path, but access alone does not create awareness. The coding workflow must also recognize public API edits so the agent knows when to query impact and when to involve the producer.

Earlier guidance will not replace pull-request analysis. The PR assessment will still verify the concrete diff and catch changes that were not recognized during coding. Coverage, freshness, ambiguity, unsupported platforms, and truncation must travel through both checks in machine-readable form. An agent should not turn “no observed usages in the available index” into “safe to remove.”

Today, agents can query Quasar through MCP when prompted. We are working toward making that context part of the coding workflow, then using identified call sites to prepare migration changes. Quasar supplies semantic evidence, an agent reduces migration toil, and the library producer remains responsible for API and release decisions.

What We Learned

Building this workflow led us to four practical lessons.

  • Separate compatibility from reach. A removed method can break the API contract even when Quasar finds no callers. A list of affected repositories and their library versions shows the migration work ahead. Producers need both signals to choose a release path.
  • Make the evidence inspectable. A repository count is more useful with sample call sites and a version breakdown. The result also needs to show which commits were indexed, how fresh the consumer indexes are, and whether evidence was truncated. Otherwise, “no usage found” can be mistaken for proof that nobody is affected.
  • Keep usage and compatibility separate across languages. SCIP lets Quasar find symbol references in several ecosystems. Deciding whether an API change is breaking requires compatibility rules or a trusted producer-side signal for that ecosystem.
  • Bring evidence to the producer while choices are open. Pull-request findings can prompt a producer to retain an API, publish a migration path, or coordinate consumers before merge. Moving the same evidence into the coding workflow could shape the edit earlier. After release, it still helps locate consumers that need to migrate.

Today, Quasar indexes JVM, Go, Python, JavaScript/TypeScript, and Rust code for cross-repository usage analysis. It compares JVM APIs and traces Protobuf changes through generated Java. Producers can review change findings through Quasar’s REST and GraphQL APIs or its MCP server, then investigate affected repositories and call sites. We are extending this foundation toward more ecosystem-specific compatibility checks, deeper code-review integration, and agent-assisted consumer migrations that keep release approval with the producer.

Quasar is internal to Netflix, but the pattern is portable: index code semantically, retain API definitions, compare them at the point of change, query affected consumers, and report the quality of the evidence. Library producers need more than a compatibility verdict. They need to know who may be affected and have enough time to act before release.

A video player seeking to the middle of a movie asks for a few megabytes. Under our old range caching, that one request could make Cloudflare pull 128 MiB from the origin and move it between data centers, sometimes after the viewer had already left. Range requests are how large files get delivered, and caching them naively easily leads to wasted network bandwidth and wasted disk operations when running a CDN at scale.

So we rebuilt how our cache handles them. For R2, Cloudflare's object storage, p99 time to first byte dropped by about half, and a follow-up improvement cut tiered cacheÂ traffic for R2Â and similar workloads by about 6%.

Today, we're making that new implementation available as Origin Range Requests, a self-service feature on all plans. There's no range size to tune and no need to contact support. Turn it on with a Cache Rule, and Cloudflare handles the rest.

An HTTP range request asks for part of a resource instead of the whole resource. The client sends a normal GETÂ with a RangeÂ header:

Range requests are common for large objects. A video player can request bytes around a seek position instead of the whole file. A download manager can resume after an interruption. A package manager can fetch part of an artifact. A client can inspect the end of a file without downloading everything before it.

The client decides whether to request a range, and Cloudflare does not change that request. Instead, Origin Range Requests changes how Cloudflare satisfies it.

Cloudflare has supported range-request caching for years via static zone configuration.

The previous implementation resembled the cache-slicing described in an nginx blog post: each client request was mapped to fixed-size ranges of the file, with the range size and file extensions configured per zone.

The size of each range created a tradeoff: small ranges led to many small origin requests and memory pressure on Cloudflare from coalescing all of the requests in-memory, while large ranges caused Cloudflare to download much more data than the client requested. Updates to the range size itself would invalidate cache entries, since the size was part of the cache key.

This design also meant people requesting content through our network could have unpleasant experiences accessing these files. Concurrent requests that fell inside the same range, no matter how far apart each clientâs requested range was, could block each other. Our nginx-based cache also couldnât combine range caching with concurrent streaming, a feature we built to let multiple clients read a file from cache while it's still being written. Turning both on together cut responses short, so each range had to be fully downloaded before anyone could read it, and popular files needed a separate origin fetch for every range.

The extra bytes could also cross several parts of our distributed cache: from the origin to one data center, between cache tiers, and then toward the client. Every hop multiplied the waste. No value works equally well for a small seek, a sequential download, a large media file, and every origin and cache topology.

Our internal cache limitations were directly exposed to customers, who should not need to understand our capabilities in-depth to have files served efficiently.

Fortunately, our transition from nginx to a Pingora-based caching server created an opportunity to address the implementation problems that had forced a hard-to-use setting onto customers.

The fix came from something Pingora was already doing. To make it easy to resume interrupted downloads, it stores every cached file on disk in fixed-size chunks. If the cache already thinks in chunks, the range size doesn't need to be a setting at all, it can just be the chunk!

For a range request, Cloudflare checks which requested bytes are already cached. It serves those bytes directly. For missing bytes, it fetches origin ranges aligned to the cache's fixed-sized-chunk boundaries, stores the result in the same chunks used for ordinary cache storage, and returns only the bytes requested by the client.

For example, a request for bytes 150-250Â can cause Cloudflare to fetch the aligned chunk containing those bytes. The downstream response still contains only 150-250.

This does not eliminate extra work for requests smaller than our chunk size â such a request can still require fetching the whole chunk containing it. We also are taking on the cost of more work for non-aligned ranges. The difference is that the extra work is bounded and predictable, and no longer determined by a customer-selected range size that can cause unpredictable effects on us and origins.

Parsing the RangeÂ header is straightforward. The harder problem is producing one correct response when the requested bytes are split across cached data, an origin response, and downloads already in progress.

This is a coordinator, not a single cache lookup. One request can use a cached prefix, an in-flight download, and a newly fetched suffix. The internal origin response can be larger than the downstream response, but the downstream status and headers describe only the requested range.

If an existing download covers another request's bytes, the second request can attach to that download instead of starting another origin fetch. This shares work during bursts for popular large files.

The design uses the existing cache data model. Headers identify the asset version, the body is stored in bounded chunks, and metadata records which ranges were written successfully.

Composed range responses are valid only when all bytes belong to the same representation. The coordinator checks that each origin response matches the requested range, that the total asset size is stable, and that the entity tag remains consistent across subrequests.

The If-RangeÂ header lets a client request a range only if the representation still matches a known validator. If it does not match, Cloudflare returns the full representation instead of combining bytes from different versions.

Cloudflare also avoids composing ranges for a representation it is about to decompress because the range over a set of compressed bytes would be different from those selected by the range in a decompressed response. In these cases, Cloudflare serves the transformed full response rather than advertising a range it cannot honor correctly.

Before opening Origin Range Requests to everyone, we moved existing users of the old range-caching feature onto the new implementation, starting with the workloads that hurt us most.

Those workloads had a lot of concurrent range requests against very large files, often on very spiky traffic. Configured range sizes on the old system ran from tens of kilobytes to tens of gigabytes. With a large range size, a request that wouldn't fill a floppy disk could make us download a few DVDs' worth of data from the origin and move it across several data centers, sometimes after the client had already given up and gone elsewhere. With a small range size, one request turned into a long line of tiny origin requests, each holding memory while it waited. Both patterns caused real operational incidents for us.

However, many data sovereignty regulations require that certain types of data be processed only within a given geographical region. Cloudflare pioneered the concept of geo-restricted transport layer security (TLS) keys back in 2017 with GeoKey Manager. For instance, a customer could choose to only unwrap their private keys in India. GeoKey Manager evolved again in 2022 with ciphertext policy attribute based encryptionÂ (ABE). No production-ready post-quantum ABE scheme is available today,Â and customers do not need a large number of regions. A simpler design meets the requirement and is easier to maintain and operate. Cloudflare therefore uses hybrid ML-KEM-768Â and X25519Â protection with two kinds of keys, out of which ML-KEM-768 is a NISTÂ standard that also opens doors to highly sensitive customers. A regional root key is a symmetric secret tied to one region, and an application-specific key pair is derived from it for a single service, such as GatewayÂ or Tunnel.Â This protects private-key material against harvest now, decrypt later while preserving regional key-use boundaries.

In its new form, GeoKey Manager is the key foundationÂ for Cloudflareâs data sovereignty solutions. It controls which region protected decryption keys can be used from and when. During initialization, the edge key service (the component on each Cloudflare edge server that unwraps customer private keys and uses them to serve requests) verifiesÂ that it has loaded every regional root key required by its configuration. If a configured key is missing or malformed, the service does not start. After initialization, an individual request also fails closed (Cloudflare rejects it rather than risk unwrapping a key out of region) if its metadata identifies a regional root key that is not locally available. This guarantees that a regional root key never leaves its region, no matter what.

Cloudflare needs to maintain high performance while keeping our customersâ regionalized data secure at scale. This is where the next version of GeoKey Manager comes in. We are enabling fastÂ performance while logically separating our network by using post-quantum cryptography to restrict where customersâ private keys are unwrapped.Â Most of our regions will be available for customers to regionalize their workflow, and there may be product specific caveats. Every wrapped customer private key carries metadata identifying its service context, regional boundary, and epoch.

The service context is the Cloudflare service that uses the key, such as Tunnel or Gateway. The regional boundary is the approved set of Cloudflare data centers where the key may be unwrapped and used, such as data centers in Germany or the European Union. The epoch identifies which generation of the regional root key the key belongs to; each rotation creates a new epoch.Â

For example, Acme Co. might have one key for TunnelÂ in Germany (acme0001) and another for GatewayÂ in Germany (acme0002). Road Runner Inc. might have one key for Tunnel in Germany (runner0001) and another for Gateway in the European Union (runner0002).

Therefore, compromising the Tunnel-Germany application-specific private key could expose both acme0001 and runner0001, but not the Gateway keys. Compromising the Germany regional root key could affect every application-specific key derived from that root for the same epoch, including acme0001, acme0002, and runner0001.

In the current architecture, the application-specific key pair is asymmetric. The public component wraps the customer's private key material, while the private component is required to unwrap it. The resulting wrapped private key is distributed with its service context (Access, Gateway, Tunnel, Cloudflare One client), region, and epoch.

Before an edge server can receive a regional root key, provisioning service evaluates three inputs:

Zinc updates its membership records for each edge server. This asynchronous process adds new servers as the network grows and removes servers when they are decommissioned. In practice, an edge server serves requests for a region only once its configuration and key provisioning are complete.

The trust boundary (all services that can access the regional root key) includes the key-management service, the provisioning service, and authorized edge key services because each can access regional root keys. The key-management service uses them to derive application-specific keys, the provisioning service stores and distributes them, and edge key services use locally authorized copies of them. Quicksilver remains outside this boundary because it receives only wrapped private keys and associated metadata.

When regional membership changes, the provisioning service immediately synchronizes the serverâs Linux kernel keyring with the latest authorized regional root keys. The edge key service caches these keys in process memory until it restarts or the cache expires. It then reloads the current authorized set from the kernel keyring.

Single-tenant Titus: VM-level isolation on a multi-tenant container platform

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Samartha Chandrashekar & Nick Parker

You click play on a Friday night, and in the second that follows, dozens of services answer in milliseconds. Most of them run on Titus, our internal container platform. Titus is, in essence, a Kubernetes-based container hosting platform: we keep both the control plane and the data plane (the container hosts that actually run containers) patched and up to date, so application teams get a consistent and reliable container experience. They hand us a container, and we run it, with no host, cluster, or kernel to own. Titus launches millions of containers every week across tens of thousands of EC2 instances in multiple AWS regions. It is multi-tenant by default: many containers from many teams share each host. That density is what makes container compute efficient, and it is why Titus has been the default home for so much of Netflix’s compute for years.

For most workloads, sharing a host works well. For a few, it does not, and those few include some of the most demanding real-time systems we run. This post is about them, and about single-tenant Titus, a mode that gives a task its own virtual machine while keeping everything else about Titus the same: the API, the deployment path, and the managed experience where the Compute/Platform team, not the application team, owns the host.

We will walk through why shared-kernel isolation runs out of road for certain workloads, how we measure isolation rather than asserting it, the options we weighed (microVMs, gVisor, EC2, and more shared-kernel tuning) and why a dedicated VM inside Titus won, how the mode is built on the Titus federation layer and autoscaling, and what we have seen along three axes: isolation, fleet efficiency, and the operational cost of keeping the fleet patched.

The cost of good neighbors

Multi-tenancy is a tradeoff. Packing many containers onto a large bare-metal instance keeps utilization high, but it also means those containers share a single Linux kernel and the underlying hardware. Titus isolates them with the tools the kernel provides: cgroups cap CPU, memory, and disk; namespaces give each container its own view of the system; seccomp and AppArmor restrict the system calls a container can make; the Completely Fair Scheduler divides CPU time; the iocost controller meters disk IO; and traffic shaping meters each container’s slice of the network. Each container also gets its own identity, IAM role, and security groups, so tenants are isolated for security and resource management.

These mechanisms provide isolation for what the kernel can partition. They cannot fully partition what is shared in hardware. A group of cores shares a last-level cache and a path to memory, so a container that suddenly pulls a lot of data from RAM evicts a neighbor’s cache lines and slows it down, even when both stay inside their CPU limits. There is no cgroup for memory bandwidth or cache residency, as there is for CPU shares. The interference is real, it is hardware, and it does not show up as either container exceeding its quota.

A noisy neighbor and a co-located app each stay within their cgroup CPU and memory limits, but both draw on a shared last-level cache and memory bandwidth that lack cgroup fences. The noisy neighbor takes most of it, so the co-located app stalls and its tail latency rises even though no one exceeded a quota.

We have written about this before. Predictive CPU isolation of containers at Netflix moved some placement decisions out of the kernel and into a service that forecasts each container’s CPU demand and re-pins containers to cores to reduce cache interference, formulated as a combinatorial optimization solved every minute. Noisy Neighbor Detection with eBPF instruments the kernel scheduler to surface per-container run-queue latency, so we can see a victim being starved in close to real time. And Mount Mayhem traced container-startup slowness to a global kernel lock amplified by modern CPU cache topology. Each of these raised the floor for everyone on the platform, and we are continuing that work.

A decade of shared-kernel isolation, at Netflix and across the industry, has not made co-tenancy invisible, because a noisy neighbor is not one problem but a family of them. Almost every physical resource a host shares is its own axis of interference, and a cgroup can fence very few of them:

  • CPU performance indeterminacy. Co-located threads contend for the run queue, so scheduling latency rises even while a container remains within its CPU quota. Separately, a neighbor with a large working set evicts a tenant’s last-level cache lines and saturates memory bandwidth, neither of which a CPU share can partition.
  • Network bandwidth and packets per second. Per-container shaping competes for link bandwidth, and receives processing runs in softirq context, charging one container’s packet work to whatever CPU a neighbor is running on. A flood of small packets exhausts a core’s softirq budget long before any bandwidth cap is reached.
  • Storage bandwidth and IOPS. A neighbor saturating a device’s queue serializes everyone’s I/O, and on cloud block storage, the per-volume and per-instance throughput ceilings are a shared budget in their own right.
  • Long tail. Page-cache reclaim, NUMA and cross-socket interconnect traffic, TLB-shootdown interrupts, PCIe and GPU contention, package-level power and thermal throttling, and contention on kernel global locks (the class of problem behind Mount Mayhem) each degrade neighbors in their own way.

What unites them is that they are physical, shared, and below the level a quota can reach. A platform can cap how much CPU time or memory a container is granted; it cannot hand each tenant its own slice of the memory bus, its own softirq budget, or its own portion of the last-level cache.

Of these, the network dimension has bitten hardest in our experience. Per-container bandwidth shaping uses a hierarchical token bucket (HTB) queueing discipline that serializes traffic with a global lock to account for each container’s bytes and packets. Under a flood of small packets from a single container, contention for that lock can burn kernel CPU and starve co-located containers of the time they need to process their own traffic, resulting in TCP retransmits and tail-latency spikes on services that did nothing wrong. The mechanism meant to prevent noisy neighbors becomes one.

The workloads most exposed to this are stateful, high-throughput streaming jobs: real-time data pipelines and stream processors that continuously move large volumes of data and maintain in-memory state. When one of these is perturbed, recovery is not cheap. A stream processor that falls behind or loses its place may have to restore from a checkpoint and pause processing for many minutes to catch up. For these jobs, intermittent interference from a neighbor is not a small slowdown that averages out; it is a stop-the-world event that pages an on-call engineer. The same teams report, accurately, that they never saw this on a plain EC2 instance.

Why we kept coming back to the VM

Full VM isolation, where a workload gets its own host and kernel, is the standard our customers are already happy with. The question was not whether VM isolation works; it was how to offer it without giving up what makes Titus worth using. We weighed four paths.

  1. Keep improving shared-kernel isolation. This helps every workload on the platform, needs no opt-in, and we are still investing in it (more on the roadmap below). Its limitation is structural. Each round of tuning narrows the gap without closing it, because the underlying hardware remains shared. Cache, memory bandwidth, and the kernel’s own shaping and scheduling locks are not things a container boundary can fully contain. Shared-kernel isolation is the right default and the wrong promise for a workload that cannot tolerate a bad minute.
  2. microVMs (Firecracker, Kata Containers). Lightweight VMs boot in about 150 milliseconds by emulating far less hardware than traditional VMs, which is why they power serverless platforms. They were the most appealing alternative on paper, and we carefully reviewed them. Three things made the cost hard to justify. First, the hardware virtualization they rely on is available only on bare-metal instances or through nested virtualization, and nested virtualization was not ready on the instance types we run at Netflix at the time, so adopting microVMs broadly would have constrained us to a narrow slice of the fleet. Second, there is no managed microVM service we could build on, which means we would have to run and maintain a virtualization layer ourselves: patching the hypervisor, live-migrating guests, and qualifying emulated devices. That is exactly the kind of operational surface we prefer not to own when we can avoid it. Third, even setting those aside, microVMs add virtualization overhead on CPU- and IO-bound work and do not support GPUs. Excellent isolation, but at a price in flexibility and operational load we were not willing to pay.
  3. gVisor. A user-space kernel that intercepts system calls provides sandbox-grade isolation without a hypervisor, thereby avoiding the bare-metal constraint. The catch is performance and dependency. Intercepting syscalls in user space adds overhead on IO-heavy paths, which are precisely the workloads we most need to protect, and the open-source project lags behind its internal counterpart in fixes and is effectively maintained by a single vendor. The isolation we would gain came with a throughput tax on the exact jobs we were trying to help.
  4. Move the workload to EC2. It is possible to run containers directly on EC2 instances. The problem is that the application team takes back the operational load that Titus exists to carry: choosing instance types, patching hosts, and sizing and scaling the fleet. For a team that came to Titus precisely to avoid those things, this is a regression in the experience, and the move between Titus and EC2 has historically been a one-way door that teams are reluctant to cross.

Spectrum of isolation options from lighter and more shared to stronger isolation: shared-kernel containers, then gVisor, then microVMs (Firecracker or Kata), then a full VM as used by single-tenant Titus, which is highlighted. Each option is annotated with its launch latency, overhead, and the entity that operates it; single-tenant Titus is a dedicated EC2 instance with no additional virtualization overhead.

There is also a question of what kind of isolation we actually need. microVMs and gVisor are built for security isolation between mutually distrusting tenants, the case where one tenant might actively try to attack or escape into another. Among Netflix teams, that threat model is more than the situation warrants. What these workloads need is resource and performance isolation: a guarantee that a neighbor cannot steal their CPU, cache, memory bandwidth, or network, not protection from a hostile co-tenant. A dedicated EC2 VM delivers exactly that, with hardware we do not have to emulate and a kernel we do not have to sandbox. We pay for the isolation we need rather than for a stronger security boundary.

microVMs and gVisor asked for a large, bespoke investment with real risk in order to approximate something we could get directly: a VM. Using EC2 to run containers provides the VM, but takes away the managed experience. So we took the direct route and brought single-tenant VM isolation into Titus itself, under the existing API and the existing shared-responsibility model.

How single-tenant Titus works

The idea is simple to state: a single-tenant task runs on its own EC2 instance, sized to fit it, with nothing else scheduled alongside. That instance is still a Titus container host that the Compute/Platform team owns and patches. The team running the workload sees the same Titus API and the same deployment path. Nothing about how they package or ship the job changes.

Side-by-side anatomy of a multi-tenant Titus, where multiple teams’ containers share one Linux kernel and the hardware beneath it and contend with one another, versus a single-tenant node where one task plus its platform sidecars owns the whole machine with no co-tenants.

Most of the work happens in the federation layer, the part of Titus that decides where a job runs (which cell, which capacity) before it is scheduled onto a node.

When a job is eligible for single tenancy, three things happen.

  1. Federation decides eligibility and the envelope. Eligibility is governed by an allowlist maintained by the Compute/Platform team; a team opts in by targeting a single-tenant workload group rather than flipping a flag in the public API. For an eligible task, federation tags it as single-tenant and records the set of instance families it may run on, ensuring consistent performance across the workload. Consolidating this decision in federation follows the same principle we use for routing in general: placement intelligence belongs in one place, above the individual cells, where it can account for capacity and availability across the region.
  2. Autoscaling provisions a dedicated node. Given a pending single-tenant task, our autoscaling implementation launches the smallest instance that satisfies the task’s requested CPU, memory, and GPU from the allowed families for the task just in time. Taking the tightest fit keeps the unused remainder of each dedicated node small. The next section covers this in detail.
  3. The scheduler enforces one task per node. Single-tenant pods are configured with pod anti-affinity to prevent colocation with other pods, along with a priority class that exempts the task from preemption. Single-tenant nodes are also tainted to prevent unrelated workloads from being placed there, with only single-tenant workloads tolerating this taint. We also disable defragmentation/repacking for these nodes, so a single-tenant task is never relocated to repack the fleet. Together, these mean a single-tenant task has its host to itself for its entire life, undisturbed by a neighbor or by the platform’s own bin-packing.

Two design choices are worth dwelling on. First, single tenancy is allowlisted on purpose. If it were a free checkbox in the API, most teams would tick it, because who wouldn’t want a quieter host? And the efficiency that multi-tenancy exists to provide would erode across the fleet. Keeping the Compute/Platform team in control lets us direct dedicated isolation to workloads that need it and steer the rest toward shared capacity, while keeping the public API surface small as we learn. Second, we do not silently resize anyone’s job to fill its node. The job keeps the resources it requested; we recommend sizing it close to a node shape so the dedicated instance is well utilized, while keeping the workload portable and running identically whether in single-tenant mode or not, regardless of the host’s dimensions. Moving in and out of the mode is a configuration change, not a rewrite.

A few perpetrators, a lot of collateral damage

Noisy-neighbor pain is not spread evenly. In practice, a small number of workloads cause most of the disruption: a job that floods the host with small packets, churns the page cache, or saturates memory bandwidth degrades the performance of every other container unlucky enough to share its host. Some of these are also among the workloads most affected by contention, because a job can be both a source of interference and sensitive to it. The shape of the problem is a few perpetrators and many victims.

That shape is what makes single tenancy a precise lever rather than a blunt one. We do not need to give every workload its own machine. We need to pull the handful of perpetrators and the most disruption-sensitive victims onto dedicated nodes. Doing so protects their former neighbors directly and removes the reason the platform was spreading sensitive workloads apart with anti-affinity placement in the first place. The hosts left behind are quieter, and because they no longer have to be kept partly empty to honor spread rules, they pack more densely.

A few dedicated nodes buy isolation for the perpetrators and a calmer, denser fleet for everyone else.

Provisioning many small nodes

Single tenancy trades fewer large, shared instances for more small, dedicated ones, and that shift has consequences across the stack.

The first is the control-plane load. More nodes mean more for the Titus control plane to track and more VM launches against the EC2 control plane, the latter mattering most during a regional failover, when we scale up the fastest and can least afford a throttle. Titus is already a federated system: each region is divided into independent cells, which limit how large any single control plane can get, and let us add capacity by adding cells rather than growing a single stack without limit. We migrate workloads in stages rather than all at once, and we exercise the launch-rate path under controlled load on a schedule rather than discovering its limits during an incident.

The second consequence is capacity management, where the design changed the most. With one task per node, the fleet now spans many instance shapes whose demand shifts continuously as jobs come and go. Pre-staging static pools of every shape would be slow to manage and wasteful, so we instead provision single-tenant nodes on demand. It handles provisioning end-to-end, and several of its features line up with exactly what single tenancy needs:

  • Just-in-time, right-sized provisioning. Single-tenant jobs are configured with a list of supported instance families based on their performance requirements. At launch, they are mapped to the best-fit node size by comparing requested resources against the selected instance families. New nodes of that instance type are brought up by the autoscaler to meet the pending demand. Single-tenant pods are configured with anti-affinity to all other pods, so the scheduler naturally lands one pod per autoscaled node. Newly vacated nodes may be reused by any pending deploys before they’re automatically torn down. The node pools themselves are shared across single-tenant workloads and describe a catalog of available instance families and sizes.
  • Single tenant node pools. Single tenant may run in separate clusters or be shared with multitenant clusters. The pools are configured with a list of supported instance-type permutations and are pinned to a Titus container host AMI. This is where the managed experience lives: the host image and its configuration are ours, declared once and applied to every node created.
  • Consolidation management. In multi-tenant environments, we default to a repack/defrag behavior that periodically relocates workloads. This doesn’t apply in single tenant, where nodes are explicitly each running at most one workload. We therefore reclaim only nodes vacated by workloads that have scaled in or terminated. Node termination is performed in two stages, allowing nodes to be reused for pending workloads before they are fully terminated.
  • Drift-based patching. When we publish a new container host AMI, we support a “mutable” deploy to existing nodes without affecting any hosted containers, and an “immutable” deploy that updates the target AMI for newly created nodes. This means that many node upgrades don’t require disrupting workloads, but we reserve the right to roll the fleet with our users if needed.

The launch-latency tax, and why warm pools are harder here

Provisioning on demand does not make a node appear instantly, and launch latency is the tradeoff single tenancy asks us to manage most carefully. Bringing a single-tenant task to the point where it can serve traffic has two parts: standing the node up (booting a fresh EC2 instance, then pulling and extracting the container image, which is the largest part of host setup for most jobs) and the application’s own startup (a JVM loading and JIT-compiling its classes, a cache filling, and so on). A fresh instance takes tens of seconds before the image pull even begins.

In multi-tenant Titus, most of this is hidden. A new task usually lands on a Titus container host that is already running and already has the image layers cached, so starting it takes only a few seconds rather than minutes; the host boot was paid for once, long ago, and amortized across every task that has run there since. Single tenancy gives up that hiding place. Every single-tenant task waits for its own machine to boot and pull its image.

The usual remedy is a warm pool: keep some instances booted and idle so a pending task lands on one immediately. This works well for the multi-tenant fleet precisely because that fleet is close to homogeneous. A handful of large shapes cover almost everything, since any task can be packed onto a big shared host. A small amount of warm capacity in a few shapes gives a high hit rate, and its cost is spread across the many tasks that will use it.

Single tenancy inverts those economics, and this is the part worth being explicit about.

Because every task gets a right-sized node, the fleet spans many instance shapes (several families across many sizes), and a warm instance only helps a pending task whose shape matches it. A warm r7i.2xlarge does nothing for a task that needs an m7a.8xlarge. To achieve a good hit rate, we would need to hold idle capacity in every family-and-size bucket, and idle capacity in a bucket with no task to fill is pure cost. The very property that makes single tenancy efficient at steady state, tight best-fit sizing, works against warm-pool reuse: more distinct shapes mean more buckets and a lower hit rate per dollar of standing capacity. And the pressure peaks exactly when it is least welcome. During a regional failover or a live event, when many tasks need fresh nodes at once, a thin warm pool spread across many shapes drains immediately, and the rest of the demand pays the full cold-boot cost.

So we treat warm capacity as a small, targeted lever rather than a default. We keep a little pre-warmed capacity for the most latency-sensitive single-tenant starts, and we deliberately narrow the menu of shapes to a curated set of sizes rather than the full continuum, so warm capacity concentrates into fewer buckets and trades a little best-fit efficiency for a better hit rate. Everything else provisions just-in-time and accepts the boot.

The more durable wins are in cutting the launch cost itself rather than pre-paying for it, and they help both single- and multi-tenant nodes, which is the better place to spend the effort:

  • Faster image distribution. The image pull dominates the host setup, so newer OCI image formats and lazy-pulling snapshotters such as stargz and SOCI, which fetch only the bytes a container actually reads at startup rather than the whole image up front, reduce the largest component of the gap.
  • Checkpoint and restore. For processes that are slow to initialize, snapshotting a warmed-up application with a tool like CRIU and restoring it, rather than cold-starting it, removes most of the second half of launch latency.
  • Instance type selection. In multitenant configurations, we have historically used metal instances, which offer slightly better performance but come with a slower startup time. In a single-tenant configuration, node startup latency is more important, so we have moved away from these instances.
  • Scheduling optimization. Through regular stress testing of fleet scaling, we found a scheduling bottleneck caused by Kubernetes preemption logic, leading to nodes being scaled up quickly but then left idle while waiting for workloads to be scheduled on them. This was ultimately unnecessary, as our single-tenant pools do not run preemptible workloads. Disabling preemption greatly improved scheduling performance when under strain.

None of this makes the tradeoff disappear, and it is worth stating plainly: a single-tenant node is slower to stand up than a slot on an already-warm shared host, and hiding that with warm pools costs more per node here than it does on the multi-tenant fleet, because the warm pool cannot be as homogeneous. But for the long-running services that single tenancy targets, paying that cost once at launch in exchange for a quiet life afterward is a trade these teams are glad to make.

Measuring the difference

Isolation claims are easy to assert and hard to trust, so we measure them with a repeatable benchmark and a single score.

The method is a differential one. We run a reference container, a healthy and representative application, and record its throughput and tail latency while it runs alone. Then we run it again next to a havoc container engineered to be a bad neighbor: a UDP flood through XDP to stress the network-shaping path, pointer-chasing and STREAM to pollute the last-level cache and saturate memory bandwidth, fsync storms against the disk, stress-ng to abuse the scheduler, and GPU-burn to monopolize an accelerator. We only trust a run once the host has reached a steady state, which we confirm with the kernel’s Pressure Stall Information: we wait until cpu_some or mem_some shows that tasks are actually stalling on a contended resource, not merely that the resource is busy. Each scenario is run many times with fixed seeds, and we report the median with a bootstrapped confidence interval, because noisy-neighbor distributions are heavy-tailed and a simple average can mask the spikes that matter.

From the reference container’s numbers, we derive a simple measure of how well isolation held. Throughput efficiency is the fraction of operations per second the app retains under attack; latency efficiency is the ratio of its undisturbed P99 to its P99 under pressure. A neighbor that doubles the reference app’s P99 drops the latency efficiency. As an illustration, a reference app whose P99 latency goes down next to a havoc container achieves a certain latency efficiency, and one whose throughput falls from a to b operations per second achieves a certain throughput efficiency. The composite score is a weighted blend of the two, with a latency-sensitive preset that gives greater weight to tail latency for interactive and streaming-path services. A perfectly isolated system scores 100%, and a single hard failure invalidates the run regardless of the average.

Noisy-neighbor isolation

cgroups container isolation isn’t perfect, and applications may experience worse performance due to sharing a machine with a determined neighbor. This could extend to disk and/or network IO where the same volumes or interfaces may be shared across workloads. For a distributed workload, this can lead to measurable inconsistencies in performance depending on where the replicas are placed. For more sensitive workloads, this variance can mean the difference between stability and getting paged.

The single-tenant option makes it possible to better isolate workloads that cause problems for their neighbors and/or are more sensitive to such problems. This can be used as a stopgap until the specific regression problem is resolved and consistency is restored, or kept permanent for load patterns that aren’t worth supporting in a shared environment.

Efficiency

The second effect is fleet efficiency, and it is the one people expect to go the wrong way. Dedicating a node to a single task consumes that node’s slack, so the naive expectation is that utilization falls. In aggregate, it can move the other way, for a reason related to how we dealt with poor isolation before single tenancy existed.

To protect sensitive workloads on shared hosts, the platform used anti-affinity placement, scattering them across the fleet to reduce colocation of the same workload on the same nodes. Spreading fights bin-packing: it strains capacity, because a host kept partly empty to honor an anti-affinity rule cannot be filled by a workload that would otherwise have fit there. When isolation-sensitive workloads move to dedicated nodes, the need for spreading and fragmentation goes away, and the remaining multi-tenant fleet can pack more densely.

Another source of fragmentation is services that are sensitive to disruption. These are typically stateful services where recovery is expensive. As these services become more common in a given population, they can lead to nodes being held up by pods with strict disruption requirements. This can lead to an inability to defrag and recompact the pool during off-peak times. The symptoms and resulting drop in fleet efficiency caused by disruption-sensitive workloads mirror what’s observed for workloads with anti-affinity rules.

Depending on the magnitude of the anti-affinity and/or disruption budget problem, moving such workloads to single-tenant pools can result in equivalent or even improved placement when all costs have been accounted for. It also becomes easier to quantify the cost to end-users. Rather than trying to attribute fleet-wide fragmentation to individual workloads, we can directly quantify waste as single-tenant node overhead.

The theory and the practice diverge here, and it was the practice that decided it. In principle, the efficiency ceiling is higher for multi-tenant bin-packing: pack enough tasks together, and a host fills almost completely. We do not reach that ceiling. AWS offers a discrete set of instance sizes, so a large shared host rarely matches the exact sum of the requests we place on it, and the gap between a host’s capacity and what we can actually pack onto it is a bin-packing hole we cannot fill. At the granularity of large instances, those holes are sizable. Right-sizing an instance to a single workload, picking the closest fit from many shapes instead of draping many workloads over one big host, captured more of the available efficiency in our experience than the higher theoretical ceiling ever delivered. The clean theory favored multi-tenancy; our measured fleet favored right-sizing.

Container-host maintenance

The third effect is operational, and it shows up in how we keep the fleet patched. A multi-tenant host is hard to take down for maintenance precisely because it is full: draining it means relocating many tenants at once, several of which carry disruption budgets that limit how fast they can move, so a routine patch becomes a careful, coordinated evacuation. A single-tenant host runs exactly one task, with consolidation and defragmentation turned off, so draining it means relocating only the one workload, and we can automatically handle that when a new AMI ships. The remaining multi-tenant hosts are easier to maintain, too, because the workloads that were most sensitive to disruption are no longer stranded on them.

What it changes, and what it doesn’t

Single-tenant mode is a configuration, not a migration. A team moves a workload onto a dedicated node, and back, with a labeling change, without rewriting anything and without leaving Titus. That turns the isolation decision into a reversible setting, which is the opposite of the one-way door between Titus and EC2 that teams used to face. A team can try single tenancy for a quarter, measure it, and step back if the trade-off isn’t worth it for them.

What does not change is the contract. The Compute/Platform team still selects the instance type, patches the host, and sizes and scales the fleet underneath. The application team still uses the Titus API, the same delivery pipeline, and the same observability. Single tenancy is a new point on the isolation axis offered within the existing managed experience, not a separate product that the team has to learn and operate.

When a dedicated node is the wrong call

A dedicated node is a real cost, and single tenancy is not free isolation. Two things are worth weighing before reaching for it.

First, a single-tenant task does not get the whole machine; it gets the machine minus overhead. Every Titus container host, dedicated or shared, runs the platform sidecars, system services, the kubelet, and the host environment itself, and that overhead is a roughly fixed slice of CPU and a few gigabytes of memory. On a shared host, that slice is paid once and amortized across the many tasks running there. On a dedicated node, a single task handles it. Because the cost is fixed rather than proportional, it is punishing as a fraction of a small instance and negligible on a large one: a small dedicated node can lose tens of percent of its resources to overhead, while on the largest shapes it rounds to a couple of percent.

Second, because a task is placed on the smallest instance that fits it, there is usually a gap between what the task asked for and the next available shape, and on a dedicated node, that gap is pure waste, with no neighbors to soak it up. The two effects compound: fixed overhead plus the best-fit remainder set a ceiling on how much of a dedicated node a workload can actually use, and that ceiling is lowest exactly where people are tempted to reach for isolation casually, on small instances. However, this sizing waste can be reduced through coordination with the service owner as needed.

The guidance follows from the arithmetic. Single tenancy pays off for large, long-running, performance-sensitive, disruption-sensitive workloads, sized close to a node shape and run as fewer, larger replicas rather than many small ones. It is the wrong call for small, bursty, or highly elastic workloads, where the overhead fraction is high, the dedicated node sits mostly idle, and multi-tenant packing is both cheaper and faster to scale. This is also why the mode is allowlisted rather than a self-serve checkbox: the platform can keep workloads on the side of that line where they belong, and leave the ones that would gain little from a node of their own on the shared fleet, where they are cheaper to run.

Who’s using it

The first adopters are the workloads that motivated the work: real-time data infrastructure. Managed Apache Flink stream processing, streaming data connectors, and telemetry and logging pipelines were both the most frequent sources of network contention and the most damaged by it, because their recovery is expensive and their disruption budgets are tight. These are stateful, high-throughput jobs where a few seconds of starvation can cascade into minutes of catch-up, and where the platform’s own repacking used to be a source of disruption in its own right.

Moving them onto dedicated nodes removed the class of problem rather than mitigating it. The teams running them report stable operation, having previously seen intermittent, hard-to-attribute interference, and their jobs are no longer relocated by fleet repacking, removing a second, self-inflicted source of disruption. Latency-sensitive serving paths and a handful of GraphQL and edge services have followed for the same reason: a predictable tail matters more to them than a few points of bin-packing efficiency.

This is an ongoing rollout, not a finished one. We are widening the set of eligible workloads as we learn, using the largest production jobs as the stress test for the platform’s scaling assumptions, and watching the control-plane and EC2 launch-rate metrics as the count of small nodes grows. We have also been running periodic stress tests to exercise scaling ahead of real user workload, and making improvements to autoscaling and scheduling logic along the way.

What’s next

We are not done on either side of the tradeoff. On single tenancy, we are reducing launch latency with smarter pre-warming of common shapes, broadening the allowlist as scaling data holds up, and extending the model beyond long-running services as cost attribution and capacity stories mature. On the multi-tenant side, which remains the right default for most workloads, we are addressing the dimensions that hurt the most. The largest is the network: replacing HTB’s global-lock shaping with an eBPF-based Expected Departure Time approach that rate-limits by timestamping packets rather than contending on a lock, and pairing it with a lower-overhead container network device. We are also evaluating hardware cache and memory-bandwidth partitioning (Intel RDT and AMD PQoS) to give the kernel a real lever on the last-level cache, hardware GPU partitioning (NVIDIA MIG) for accelerator isolation, and continued work on the cgroup v2 IO controller for disk. This isolation benchmark lets us rank these by return on effort rather than by intuition, and show customers, in the numbers, that the shared fleet is getting quieter over time.

Single-tenant Titus is also a step toward something larger. Netflix’s Platform teams offer two ways to get compute: virtual machines on EC2 & containers on Titus. We want to replace that with a single Compute API that takes intent rather than mechanism into account: how much isolation a workload needs, how quickly it must launch, how long it can run without disruption, and what business domain it belongs to. The platform then makes placement and runtime choices, and can make globally optimal ones because it sees the whole picture.

Conclusion

Multi-tenancy is the right default for a container platform, and we will keep making it better for everyone. But the right default is not right for everything. Single-tenant Titus gives the workloads that need it the isolation of a dedicated VM, provisioned and patched by our existing node management, under the same API and the same managed experience, without taking away the freedom to change their mind. We can now point to the difference rather than describe it: a tail that stops moving under a noisy neighbor, a fleet that packs more densely once spreading is retired, and hosts that are faster and safer to patch. For the streaming and data systems at the heart of Netflix’s real-time platform, a room of one’s own turned out to be the feature that mattered.

The Regression Tests Nobody Writes: Capturing How Software Actually Behaves

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Here’s an awkward question for any codebase that’s been around a few years. Do your tests describe what the system does? Or what somebody, at some point, thought it should do?

Not the same thing. Not even close, usually. Most regression tests get written right alongside a feature, by people writing down their intentions. This function returns that. This endpoint rejects bad input. Perfectly good tests! But give a system a few years in production and it picks up a whole extra layer of behavior that nobody planned and nobody wrote down. Quirks. Weird edge cases. A default value someone picked in 2019 for reasons that are now completely lost. And I’d bet money some of your users, or some other team’s service, quietly depends on every one of them.

That’s where a lot of the really nasty regressions live. And funnily enough, there’s a kind of test built for exactly that gap. Almost nobody writes it.

You’ve probably heard of Hyrum’s Law, named after Hyrum Wright at Google. The gist: once enough people use your API, every observable behavior of your system ends up being depended on by somebody. Not just the documented contract. All of it. The order results come back in. The exact wording of an error. How long a call takes. Whether an empty field shows up as null or just... doesn’t show up.

If you’ve ever maintained something popular, you’ve lived this. You fix a thing that was obviously wrong, ship it, feel good about yourself, and within the hour there’s a bug report from someone whose integration was built on the wrong thing. To them, you just broke their stuff. And are they wrong? Not really.

Regular regression tests can’t help much here. They only check what somebody thought to check. Everything accidental slips right through.

Michael Feathers put a name on this back in Working Effectively with Legacy Code. He called them characterization tests, and the idea is almost cheeky. Before you touch code you don’t fully understand, you write tests that capture what it does right now. Not what it should do. What it actually does.

Run the code, see what comes out, pin that in a test. Looks like a bug? Pin it anyway, maybe leave yourself a note. Correctness isn’t the goal here. The goal is a safety net that yells the moment behavior shifts.

It’s kind of a mental flip, honestly. A normal test says “here’s what I want, code, please live up to it.” A characterization test says “here’s what’s real, and if you change it, you’d better have a reason.”

So if they’re that useful, why doesn’t everyone have a pile of them? Mostly human reasons.

They’re a slog to write. Characterizing one gnarly function means throwing a bunch of inputs at it and carefully noting every output. Characterizing a whole service? Now you’re doing that across every endpoint, every odd case, every conversation it has with some downstream system. Who’s got time for that?

They also just feel wrong. Writing a test that asserts behavior you’re pretty sure is a bug goes against every instinct a developer’s got. It takes a real shift to see that pinning the bug is the whole point. You’re not saying it’s fine. You’re making sure that if it ever changes, someone decided to change it.

People have been chipping away at the slog for years. Golden master testing, which some folks call approval testing, grabs a big chunk of output once, saves it as the blessed version, and checks every future run against it. Frontend snapshot tests run on the same instinct. Both make characterization way cheaper, since you’re capturing output all at once instead of asserting on it one field at a time.

Where they really shine is stuff you can call directly. A function. A report generator. A rendered component. Services are trickier, though. A service’s behavior isn’t just what it returns. It’s how it reacts to real requests, and everything it does along the way. Which queries it runs. Which other services it calls. Which messages it fires off.

And this is where it gets fun if you run services in production. The best record of how your service actually behaves isn’t sitting in a test file anywhere. It’s in the traffic.

Think about it. Every real request and response is basically a characterization test that hasn’t been written yet. Capture what comes into a service and what goes back out, and you’ve pinned how it behaves for real inputs, including all the strange ones nobody would ever dream up by hand. Capture what it sends to its own dependencies too, and now you’ve got its side effects pinned as well.

Recording down at the network level makes this doable without touching the application code, and it catches database and queue traffic right along with HTTP. Replay those recordings later, with dependencies answered from that same capture, and you’ve got a characterization suite that behaves the same way every run. Same inputs in, same downstream behavior. If a response changes, or the service suddenly makes an extra database call, it shows up in the diff.

That last bit doesn’t get enough credit. Some of the most expensive regressions keep the response exactly the same while quietly changing what happens underneath. A new query that’s harmless in testing and brutal at scale, say. A suite built on real traffic catches those too.

They fail differently than normal tests, and that takes a bit of getting used to.

A red characterization test doesn’t mean “bug.” It means “behavior changed.” And the very next question is always, did we mean that? Sometimes yes. You fixed the quirk on purpose, you warned the folks relying on it, and the new behavior becomes the new baseline. Sometimes no, and congrats, you just caught a regression no intent-based test was ever going to notice.

Either way you’re better off than finding out from a user. Teams that review these diffs in pull requests tend to pick up a really healthy habit: behavior changes stop being accidents and start being decisions. The diff turns into a little conversation. Did we actually want this?

Noise needs some attention, though. Timestamps and generated IDs change every run, and they’ll drown out the real signal if you let them. Ignore those specific fields, and nothing broader, and the suite stays honest.

A handful of situations keep coming up where these tests really earn their keep.

Refactoring legacy code is the classic one, the case Feathers was writing about in the first place. Pin the behavior, then move things around without the cold sweat.

Framework and library upgrades are another. A new major version of your web framework or ORM can nudge behavior in tiny ways intent-based tests never look at, like how something serializes or what order results come back in.

Rewrites might be the best case of all. When a team ports a service to a new language or a new architecture, the old service’s recorded traffic basically becomes the spec for the new one. Fire the same requests at both. Any difference is either a deliberate improvement or a regression. No more arguing about what the old system was “supposed” to do.

And then there’s AI-assisted changes, which are only getting more common. When an assistant drafts a big refactor and updates the tests in the same go, a baseline captured beforehand is one of the very few checks that doesn’t share the same assumptions as the change itself.

Here’s how I’ve come to see it. Every long-lived codebase has two specs. One lives in its tests. The other lives in its behavior. Most regression testing only protects the first, and for any system with real users, the second one is often the one that bites.

Want to try it? Pick a service you’re a little scared to change. Before your next refactor, capture what it really does, run your change against that baseline, and look hard at every difference. I’d bet you find at least one behavior you didn’t know you had. Much nicer to meet it in a diff than in a bug report.
