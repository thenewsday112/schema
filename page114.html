Microsoft Authenticator Flaw Let Permissionless Android Apps Steal Work Account Tokens

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Microsoft has fixed a flaw in its Authenticator app for Android that let a malicious app with no permissions pose as a trusted Microsoft app, such as Teams, and collect sign-in tokens for a victim's work account.

Microsoft tracks the flaw as CVE-2026-80097 and fixed it in Authenticator for Android version 6.2606.4246, so anyone using the app should update.

In the researchers' tests, the tokens gave full access to the account's email, files, and calendar. Microsoft says exploitation also needs the victim to complete a sign-in step in Authenticator, and that the flaw has not been used in attacks.

The research comes from Ofek Levin, working through a fellowship program at the security firm Enclave, which published the technical details on October 23, 2026. Microsoft assigned the CVE on September 8 and rated it 8.6 on the CVSS scale, which it labels "Important."

On Android, Authenticator does more than show six-digit codes. It also acts as a broker: when Teams, Outlook, or another Microsoft app needs to sign a user in, it asks Authenticator, and Authenticator hands back a token, a digital pass that proves the user is logged in. Because the broker controls access to the entire account, it should be careful about which app it grants a token to.

The flaw let the wrong app get one. Authenticator exposes a component, an activity named AccountChooserActivity, that any app on the device can start. When the attacker's app started it, Authenticator built its token request from values the attacker's app supplied, including the name of the app it claimed to be. The app said it was Teams, and the broker believed it.

A signature check was supposed to stop this, but it did not. The check looked up the real signature of the app being impersonated and confirmed the request matched it. Because Teams was genuinely installed on the device, its real signature was there to read, so the check passed.

Authenticator could also see which app had actually made the request, Enclave found, but it did not use that to decide whether to hand over a token.

In Enclave's tests, posing as Teams and requesting Microsoft Graph tokens returned permissions to read and send mail, read and write files, and read and write calendars, among others.

Microsoft describes the result more generally: a successful attacker could authenticate as the signed-in user and reach that user's resources. The tokens apply to the work account signed into Authenticator on that device, not to other accounts.

The two accounts disagree on one point a reader should weigh. Enclave reported that its proof of concept returned tokens without any user interaction, other than the presence of a signed-in account.

Microsoft's advisory says exploitation requires installing a malicious app on the device and convincing the user to complete an authentication flow in Authenticator. Its CVSS score also indicates that the flaw requires user interaction.

Microsoft says the flaw has not been exploited and was not publicly disclosed before the fix, and rates exploitation as "less likely." It was part of the company's September 2026 Patch Tuesday and was not one of the two Windows flaws flagged that month as already under attack. The vulnerability is not listed in the U.S. CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog as of October 26, 2026.

The fix is to update Microsoft Authenticator for Android to version 6.2606.4246 or later, as linked in Microsoft's advisory. Organizations that manage Android devices can require that minimum version through their device management. Microsoft's advisory gives the fix but does not say whether updating cancels any token a malicious app may already have obtained, so that remains unclear from the available sources.

Keystone to Destroy 18,835 Hardware Wallets After Hackers Steal Device Verification Key

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An attacker who gained access to Keystone's website obtained the key that its hardware wallets use to prove they are genuine, the company said in a statement on October 26.

Someone with that key could build counterfeit devices that pass the online check owners use to verify a Keystone. A buyer who trusted a counterfeit could have their crypto stolen, but the incident did not involve owners' recovery phrases or wallet private keys.

Keystone will destroy 18,835 unsold devices that carry the key, and all new devices will get a new one.

Keystone found the unauthorized access on October 20, and its advice to owners depends on when a device arrived. For a device received after that date with the old key, the company said: "Even if such a device passes authentication, we cannot completely rule out the possibility that it is counterfeit."

Owners of such a device should contact Keystone support and use it only after the company confirms it is genuine.

A device delivered before October 20 that passes the check can be considered genuine and used normally, according to Keystone. The company has not said when the attacker first got in.

The attacker exploited a vulnerability in a third-party data monitoring tool used by Keystone's website to escalate privileges, according to the company. That let the attacker obtain customer data and the key.

Keystone blocked the attacker's access on October 20 and has since fixed the vulnerability. It has not said where the key was stored.

The owner scans a QR code on Keystone's verification page with the device, then enters the code the device displays on the page.

In Keystone's open-source firmware, the QR code carries that code in encrypted form, and the device decrypts it with its stored key. So a correct code shows that the device holds the key.

The key is used only for this check. It plays no part in creating recovery phrases, deriving private keys, or signing transactions.

Anyone with a copy of the key could produce the right codes, so a counterfeit device carrying it could pass the check. Such devices could be sold or shipped to customers, leading to the theft of their crypto, Keystone said. The company has not reported any counterfeit devices or thefts.

The key is written into each device during manufacturing and cannot be erased or rewritten. The firmware keeps it in memory, which can be written only once, and refuses to accept a second key. So devices already made cannot be switched to the new key.

Keystone first disclosed the breach on October 24, saying a third party had obtained customers' shipping information.

The incident affected 25,964 buyer records from purchases on Keystone's website between 2024 and 2026, the company said on October 26. The records hold names, contact details, shipping addresses, and order details. Keystone's online store runs on Shopify, which stored and managed the records.

Payment card and bank details and government ID numbers were not involved, Keystone said. Purchase records from Amazon and Keystone's other sales channels were not affected.

Keystone has also included 12,873 unpaid checkout records in its response as a precaution. These hold names, contact details, and shipping information from purchases that were never completed.

Some of the affected data should already have been deleted. Keystone had promised to delete shipping information 180 days after each order.

An automated tool Keystone built to erase records in bulk did not work reliably. Some deletions failed, and Keystone did not catch the failures in time.

Keystone is emailing affected customers and plans a page where people can check whether their information was affected.

AI-Generated Fake Login Pages Are Making Account Takeovers Easier

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Fake login pages are nothing new, but generative AI is making them easier to build and more convincing. Attackers can recreate familiar login screens, impersonate trusted brands and tailor phishing attempts to specific employees or organizations.

Some fake login pages go beyond stealing passwords. They can also capture MFA codes or hijack authenticated sessions, allowing attackers to gain access to corporate accounts even when additional security measures are in place.

Attackers have long used fake login pages to steal credentials, but generative AI makes it easier to create convincing replicas of legitimate authentication portals. From company logos and page layouts to login prompts, these fraudulent pages can closely resemble the websites employees regularly access.

AI also makes it easier to tailor phishing campaigns to specific organizations or employees. For example, attackers can create personalized IT notifications that direct employees to a fraudulent login page using the company's branding and familiar authentication prompts.

These attacks often rely on lookalike domains that closely resemble legitimate web addresses, with subtle spelling changes or additional characters. Combined with realistic login pages and targeted messaging, these tactics make phishing attempts harder to recognize.

While these techniques aren't new, generative AI lets attackers create, customize and adapt phishing campaigns more quickly and at a larger scale. The 2026 Verizon Data Breach Investigations Report found that phishing accounted for 44% of the AI-assisted initial-access techniques examined.

Fake login pages can capture more than usernames and passwords. Some replicate multi-step authentication processes to collect MFA codes, which attackers can attempt to use before they expire to gain unauthorized access to corporate accounts and applications.

This makes session hijacking particularly dangerous. Even with MFA enabled, attackers can potentially access accounts by stealing active session tokens. This lets them hijack an authenticated session without entering a password or completing another MFA challenge.

Security awareness training helps employees recognize phishing attempts, but convincing login page replicas can be difficult to distinguish from legitimate websites. Even experienced users can overlook subtle warning signs, particularly when a fraudulent page uses familiar branding and authentication prompts.

Email filters, URL blocklists and website reputation services can stop many known phishing threats, but newly registered domains and short-lived phishing sites may evade detection. Attackers also distribute malicious links through text messages, collaboration platforms and search ads, letting phishing attempts bypass email-based defenses.

URL inspection provides another layer of protection, but lookalike domains can be difficult to identify. A valid HTTPS connection also doesn't guarantee that a website is legitimate.

These gaps highlight the need for security controls that can evaluate suspicious websites and detect potential credential-harvesting attempts before users enter their login information.

Organizations can reduce the risk of credential theft by strengthening authentication, detecting suspicious login pages and limiting what attackers can access if an account is compromised.

Phishing protection needs to extend beyond email filters and traditional detection methods to the point where users enter their credentials.

How solid-state transformers could help power the AI boom

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

New technology could help cut power losses and reinvent a staple of the grid.

Transformers are a crucial but nearly invisible part of our grid. The devices help efficiently move power across long distances, but they’re complicated and in short supply. And given that their basic design hasn’t changed much for about a century, they could use an upgrade.

As the world races to meet rising electricity demand from EVs, data centers, and more, researchers and companies are looking to modernize transformers. New, solid-state versions could help relieve some of the supply concerns and make sure the grid can handle the growing pressure.

Conventional transformers generally rely on huge amounts of specialized steel alloys and miles of copper wire to do their work of transferring electricity between circuits. Solid-state models, in comparison, combine magnetic components with high-power semiconductors, making them more compact and allowing for more control over the flow of electricity.

These devices are still in development, and can’t replace all traditional transformers today, especially the largest ones that work at the high voltages necessary for long-distance transmission. But a new generation of transformer technology could be a crucial part of our future grid.

Moving electricity through the grid isn’t perfectly efficient: As electricity flows through power lines, some gets lost as heat. How much varies—the higher the voltage, the lower the current, and the less electricity gets lost. So a key function for the grid is stepping voltage up and down.

Consider, for example, your local solar plant. For that electricity to efficiently travel to your home hundreds of miles away, it needs to reach a higher voltage than what’s generated by the solar plant. Enter transformers.

In its path from the power plant to your outlets, electricity might go through one transformer at the solar farm and another at a substation nearby, stepping up the voltage twice to reach a high enough level to travel on high-voltage transmission lines. Solar panels also produce direct current (DC) electricity, which needs to be converted to alternating current (AC) via an inverter before it flows into a transformer. Closer to its destination, the voltage will be stepped down again, probably at least twice: once at another substation and again at a smaller transformer in your neighborhood.

If you’re in the US, electricity from a solar plant might be converted to AC by an inverter at around 480 volts, get stepped up to 115,000 volts for the transmission lines, and finally get stepped back down to 120 volts and 240 volts for your home.

Conventional transformers do this work using what essentially amount to loops of wire. The core of a transformer is typically made of a special type of steel called grain-oriented electrical steel. Coils of copper wire sit on either end of that core.

The number of loops in each coil determines the voltage on either end of the transformer: If there are more loops on the secondary coil than the primary, the voltage steps up, and if there are fewer, it steps down. When incoming electricity passes through loops on the upstream end of the transformer, that forms a magnetic field in the core. That field induces a voltage in the loops on the other side of the device, which is what allows the energy to continue flowing at a new voltage. This process is called magnetic induction.

Transformers form the backbone of electricity transmission and distribution, says Srdjan Lukic, a professor of electrical and computer engineering at North Carolina State University. In addition to doing the job of stepping up and down voltage, transformers also often provide what’s called galvanic isolation, meaning there’s no direct electrical path flowing through them. That can help limit disturbances from moving through the grid, improving safety.

The problem is that today’s transformers are big and bulky, and they’re also difficult and expensive to replace, Lukic says.

Solid-state transformers essentially add so-called power electronics—semiconductors that convert and control electricity to the typical blueprint of today’s transformers.

While a conventional transformer operates at the frequency of the part of the grid it’s connected to, a solid-state transformer actually controls the frequency of the current. Typically, a solid-state transformer first converts incoming AC power from the grid to DC power, and then to a higher-frequency AC power using switches made of a semiconductor like silicon carbide.

From there, a magnetic component changes the voltage. Because the advanced semiconductors allow transformers to exert so much control over the frequency and stability of the current, the magnetic core can be much smaller than in a conventional transformer. Some come in at roughly one-tenth the size but have the same capacity, Lukic says.

Finally, additional power electronics convert the electricity from high-frequency AC to its required form—generally back to DC power and finally to lower-frequency AC.

Today, stepping voltage up and down is many transformers’ only job. But solid-state transformers can do much more. They absorb the jobs of devices like rectifiers and inverters (which convert AC to DC power and vice versa), shrinking the total footprint of the electrical equipment and potentially cutting the amount of power lost in the system.

In August, the New York Power Authority and the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) tested a one-megawatt solid-state transformer under realistic utility conditions at EPRI’s test site. The device was built in Lukic’s lab, and it was the first such test for a transformer of that capacity, he says.

The prototype was roughly 98% efficient—meaning it lost only 2% of the energy fed in. An equivalent conventional transformer and rectifier, by comparison, would be about 96% efficient together, Lukic says. A difference of 2% might seem small, but it is significant, since it means the solid-state transformer could cut power losses from conventional equipment in half, Lukic says.

One major goal of the project was to help utilities gain more confidence in these new components, Lukic says. That’s crucial, since these devices could be used on the power grid in the future. In the near term, solid-state transformers could be used within data centers to provide electricity for server racks.

The prototype is still a relatively low-voltage system, operating at about 15,000 volts. Industrial facilities like larger data centers often require higher-voltage connections, and transmission lines typically operate at hundreds of thousands of volts. Adapting solid-state transformers to these higher voltages will require further engineering development. But the test was a successful demonstration that solid-state transformers can work under real conditions, Lukic says.

Companies developing solid-state transformers are largely focused on data centers right now, Lukic says. There’s an influx of money to support developing the new technology, and the voltage requirements aren’t as high as they will be for larger transformers that will be needed for long-distance transmission.

In February, Heron Power raised $140 million to commercialize its solid-state transformer, which the company calls the Heron Link. The technology could be used in data centers, solar power arrays, and grid batteries, says Drew Baglino, the founder and CEO. The company is starting to test its technology at larger scales now, Baglino says. Ten megawatt-hours’ worth of batteries at Heron’s headquarters in Scotts Valley, California, will soon undergo testing with Heron transformers. The company is also planning a pilot with a nearby solar farm, Baglino says.

One challenge for solid-state transformers is that their power electronics have more failure modes than conventional transformers. In a traditional device, a failure means that the entire device will typically be taken offline, resulting in power interruptions. But Heron designs with that in mind, Baglino says: The company uses a modular design, with many small devices that work together. If one fails, the rest of the system keeps working until the affected bit can be replaced, which doesn’t require shutting the whole thing down.

Heron Power is currently building a $100 million factory in California to make advanced transformers. The facility should begin production in 2027 and reach its full production rate of 40 gigawatts’ worth of transformers per year in 2029.

In recent years, companies that make grid equipment have put $1.7 billion into the US supply chain for power transformers, which are used in high-voltage transmission, says Benjamin Boucher, a supply chain analyst at Wood Mackenzie. There’s been an additional $1.3 billion invested for distribution transformers, the smaller devices that help transform power for homes and businesses, he adds.

But even this investment isn’t keeping pace with the rapid growth of the grid. Wait times for large conventional transformers—the kind needed to connect new power plants, long-distance transmission, and high-demand sites like data centers—are stretching as long as five years.

“Transformers have become one of the biggest bottlenecks for utilities, generation developers, and even data centers,” Boucher says. The big challenge is that it’s difficult to get new transformer manufacturing up and running, because it’s a quite technical process that requires a lot of specialized labor. So even facilities announced years ago are still years from opening, Boucher says.

Solid-state transformers could help ease some of that pressure. But high-voltage devices needed for power plant connections and transmission lines are the holy grail for solid-state transformers, Lukic says. Existing solid-state technology can’t be used for those applications yet, so the industry will need to develop new materials and designs to support high voltages and handle electricity safely in a compact device.

“We’re facing a big risk related to grid infrastructure,” Boucher says. “Without the transformers, you don’t really have an electrical grid.”

OpenAI's compaction feature, which shrinks a long conversation to save space, could be turned into a way to copy a model's hidden reasoning out in plain text, security firm Xint has shown.

OpenAI's reasoning models work through a problem before answering, and the service normally returns only the raw reasoning as an encrypted item the user cannot read. The method got past the protections OpenAI added in August against this kind of reasoning theft. OpenAI confirmed the problem and fixed it on October 23, according to Xint.

Xint ran the technique against gpt-5.6-sol, one of OpenAI's reasoning models, using math and algorithm puzzles its researchers wrote themselves. The reasoning it pulled out came from those same requests, and Xint did not claim the method could reach another user's reasoning.

Xint says it cannot prove the copied text is word-for-word the model's real reasoning, because it never had the original to compare against. Two cheaper models each repeated the item and produced the same text, which rules out mistakes in the copying step but leaves open the possibility that compaction reworded the text.

Compaction is an OpenAI feature that uses a model to squeeze a long conversation into a smaller, encrypted item that carries the context forward.

Xint sent a task to gpt-5.6-sol and kept the encrypted reasoning it returned. It then called the compaction step on the same model and told it to copy that reasoning into the compacted item word-for-word, with every space changed to a tab to get past OpenAI's usage-policy filters.

Because compaction items can move between models, Xint passed the item to the cheaper gpt-4.1 and gpt-4o models and asked each to repeat its input exactly, which they did.

The method worked around a fix OpenAI had already shipped. In August, researchers behind a paper called Stolen Thoughts showed that encrypted reasoning from a strong model could be replayed into a weaker model from the same company, which would then repeat it as plain text. OpenAI and others blocked that cross-model replay soon after.

Compaction got past that block, Xint said, because the compaction step runs on the same model that made the reasoning, so it never triggers the cross-model check.

The idea built on Xint's earlier work, called GhostCompact, which showed the compaction step can be pushed to run tasks of the attacker's choosing. Xint has published the code in its GitHub repository.

The fix was on OpenAI's side, so developers who build on the API do not need to change anything. Xint called it an undisclosed fix and said OpenAI paid the researchers a $6,000 reward.

Xint tested only gpt-5.6-sol and did not check whether GPT-6 Astra, OpenAI's newer model, is affected. It is not known whether anyone used the method before OpenAI closed it. Xint also argued that fixing this one path may not be enough because, in its view, any server-side step that reads a model's decrypted reasoning and follows the user's instructions could leak it.

OpenAI keeps raw reasoning hidden partly to stop distillation, where one company trains a cheaper model on another's outputs. Raw reasoning makes better training data than the final answer alone, because it shows how the model worked through the problem, which is what makes it worth stealing for a rival.

OpenAI said in September that it had disrupted a July campaign that tried to expose its models' hidden reasoning, and linked a core part of it to people associated with China's Moonshot AI.

Anthropic and a joint U.S. government advisory have each reported China-based labs targeting the hidden reasoning of American models. None of that is tied to Xint's compaction method, which is a demonstration rather than an attack seen in the wild.

Several PDF implementations advertise AES-256, but generate the 256-bit key from either a very small state space, or from insecure random sources.

We found that several PDF implementations generate their AES-256 file keys from state spaces small enough to enumerate. In MuPDF, only 9 bits of random state affect the actual AES-256 key. Other PDF editors like Foxit base their AES-256 key on time based randomness and thus also lead to keys that are trivial to recover.

PDF encryption protects the strings and datastreams in a PDF document, including metadata unless it is explicitly left unencrypted. The password protection mechanism has gone through a bunch of revisions over the years, moving from RC4 to AES and hardening password hashing against brute force attacks. The current version, revision 6, uses AES-256.

When a PDF is encrypted with AES-256, the password does not encrypt the document directly. The implementation generates a random 32-byte file key, which encrypts the content. The password and a salt are then hashed to derive a separate key, which encrypts this random 32-byte file key. Revision 6 makes this hashing deliberately slow; the older AES-256 revision 5 does not.

A PDF can have a user password for viewing and an owner password for changing permissions. Either unlocks the same file key, so the restrictions are up to the reader: with the viewing password, you can also make an unrestricted copy.

We found that this randomness is often generated very insecurely, allowing passwordless decryption of protected PDFs.

To fix our configuration, we made the pool hold the full context.Â We adjusted our GLM 5.3 configuration to match our GLM 5.2 configuration, which freed enough GPU memory that the prefill KV pool now holds about 2.04 million tokens, roughly twice the 1,048,576-token context length. To fix the engine, we also made the rejection path safe.Â Relying on pool size alone is fragile: any change to quantization, tensor parallelism, CUDA graph sizes, or a future model version changes the pool size in a way that's easy to miss. The fix was already on SGLang main in #36988, merged September 6 for a related failure path but not yet released. We validated both changes against the researcher's reproduction script and our own canary-marker tests before restoring the model.

Workers AI runs GLM 5.3 with SGLang in a disaggregated configuration: a pool of prefill GPUs processes the prompt and produces the model's KV cache for it, then hands that cache over remote direct memory access (RDMA), via Mooncake, to a pool of decode GPUs that generate the response. We have written about this architecture before in our posts on high-performance LLMsÂ andÂ smaller, faster, safer models.

The KV cache in GPU memory is a shared pool of fixed-size pages. Each request is assigned pages when it is admitted and releases them when it finishes. Pages are not currently zeroed between requests; the next request that gets a page simply overwrites it. This approach is common among inference engines and is necessary to achieve the best performance, but it means that the inference engine is responsible for tracking ownership of these memory pages. KV cache page ownership and lifetime tracking has to deal with not just shared ownership within a node (KV cache offload and token generation both access the memory with different lifetimes), but also ownership that is technically shared with the remote prefill node (which holds a simultaneous mutable reference to the page).

Furthermore, the size of this pool is not a configuration value. SGLang sizes it at startup from whatever GPU memory remains after loading the model weights. The model's advertised context length is a separate configuration value. Nothing forces the two to agree, and it is our job to check that they do.

On our GLM 5.3 prefill nodes with our configuration, the pool came to 681,280 tokens while the model's context length is 1,048,576 tokens. The selected KV pool size is printed by SGLang in logs during startup and exposed as a metric. We were aware of this size mismatch; however, during pre-launch testing we observed successful HTTP status codes and a streaming body with no error frames during benchmarking. We assumed that the engine was capable of gracefully handling the difference between the KV cache pool and requested context window via streaming or some other mechanism.

This bug belongs to a class of KV cache memory-safety bugs that Cloudflare has been working to address for several months. In each case, a page is freed, reused, or read while something else still has a claim on it. We see two common causes.

The first is page lifetime within a single node. Several tasks on a decode node can hold the same page at once: token generation, offload to host memory, the KV transfer receiver, and the abort and error paths. If one of them frees the page while another is still using it, a new request can be given that page while it is still being read or written. Our earlier fixes for decode offload (#24226, #30899) and double release on grammar errors (#30937) were of this kind.

The second is a race across nodes. When a request is aborted while prefill is still writing its KV cache to decode over RDMA, the writes already sent cannot be recalled. If decode frees those pages right away, those pages can be given to another request, and the late writes land in that request's KV cache. Cloudflare has contributed to the effort to fix this race through proposed fixes (#31357, #38961) and a reproduction script (#32564). The bug in this incident was different: a rejected request went through the handoff every time, rather than by a timing accident. The company tells a vivid (and in many ways scary) story about a future it portrays as inevitable but one that has not yet happened, often co-opted from well-worn science fiction memes. It presents its technology as mysterious and dangerous, and insists that it is the only company trustworthy enough to tame it. (What investor wouldn’t want in on that?)

If you skim through the long sections about risks in the IPO prospectus, it’s clear this is no ordinary offering and no ordinary company. The way Anthropic tells it, business success depends not only on the firm’s ability to translate its technology into profitable products but also to save the world from what it has made.

Take another example. Last year, Chris Olah, another Anthropic cofounder, was invited to address the Vatican, where he told Pope Leo XIV and other religious scholars and officials what his company was learning about its technology. “I will be honest: We keep finding things that are mysterious, even unsettling,” he said. “We find evidence of introspection. We find internal states that functionally mirror joy, satisfaction, fear, grief, and unease.”

Never mind that many researchers would scoff at such an overt suggestion that Anthropic’s software has humanlike inner experience. This view of its technology makes its way into the prospectus as one more risk to its business. Anthropic states that: “In addition, given our focus on developing frontier AI, and the way we approach model sentience and moral status, we may face heightened scrutiny or pressure in connection with these debates and could elect to adopt policies, safeguards, or limitations regarding model development, deployment, or modification that go beyond existing legal requirements, which could constrain our operations, increase costs, or limit the commercial potential of our models, platform, and applications.”

In other words, if Claude wakes up, it could be a problem.

I’m not saying they’re necessarily wrong about any of this (although it’s pretty wild to read about machine sentience in an IPO prospectus.) But in a world where nobody knows what’s coming, companies like Anthropic get to fill the vacuum. When so little seems certain, what it says is snatched up and clung to like pieces of a sinking ship.

It’s worth taking a moment to ask: What is Anthropic? It might sound like a starter question on a family quiz show, but it’s far harder to answer than you might think.

The question has been on my mind since reading an exchange on X last week kicked off by Nathan Calvin, who is chief strategy officer at Encode AI, an organization based in Washington, DC, that advocates for regulation. Calvin was unhappy about the legislation that Anthropic proposed in a draft bill it circulated in secret to US lawmakers (which was then leaked).

Anthropic wants to be seen as the good guy, Calvin said. But why should we trust it? “What is Anthropic?” he asked.

Here’s one answer: Anthropic is a company whose IPO values it at more than $1.5 trillion, netting its cofounders, including Clark and Olah, vast wealth.

But Anthropic has always insisted it is more than that. As Calvin noted: Anthropic might instead be considered an AI safety organization focused on developing powerful technology that is reliable and (almost) risk-free. Or it might be considered a group of people hell-bent on creating a technology with superhuman capabilities before anyone else—maybe because they believe they’re the most responsible people to do so, or maybe because they have big egos.

In fact, Anthropic is all of these things. As CEO Dario Amodei put it in a reply to Calvin’s post: “Our strategy has always been what I’ve called the ‘race to the top’—by showing how it’s possible to build the technology responsibly, we can raise the bar for the entire industry. The forces that drive the race to the top are hugely more effective if the company practicing them is at the frontier; that’s one lesson I’ve learned repeatedly. And looking at that strategy as a whole, I’m proud of our track record and stand by it.”

Amodei makes it sound as if he’s got it all tied up, but playing multiple roles is hard. There are huge tensions between the priorities of an AI safety organization and a profit-driven megacorp, for example. (Amodei admits in his post that he has made mistakes.)

Ultimately, the stories Anthropic tells itself should help us understand the stories it tells us. They should help us figure out how much to trust it to self-regulate or whether we should be forcing it to give external safety testers access to its models on our terms rather than theirs. They should help us give it the benefit of the doubt (or not) when what looks like a self-serving piece of legislation comes to light.

But I think the stories reveal a multiheaded hydra. Anthropic is many things to many people, and I’m not sure that makes it more trustworthy. In many ways, a good old-fashioned technology company—without the science fiction role-play, without the woo-woo—would be far easier to read.
