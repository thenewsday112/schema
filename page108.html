The $5.8 billion joint venture between Rivian and Volkswagen Group is expanding to include AI assistants.

Rivian is now licensing elements of its AI voice assistant technology to Volkswagen Group as part of the broader joint venture, according to the company’s third-quarter investor presentation released Thursday.

Rivian didn’t disclose the licensing fees, payment terms, or which Volkswagen vehicles will use the technology. However, the AI assistant, which recently rolled out to Rivian vehicles, could make its Volkswagen debut in the automaker’s low-cost EV called the ID EVERY1. The small four-door hatchback is slated to be the first VW vehicle to use Rivian’s software and electrical architecture under the joint venture established in 2024.

The joint venture between Rivian and VW Group has already expanded once before, at least financially. The companies initially announced a joint venture valued up to $5 billion, but within months VW upped its commitment to invest up to $5.8 billion in Rivian by 2027. The initial tie-up, formalized through an independent company that kicked off in November 2024, focused on bringing Rivian’s electrical architecture and software to Volkswagen’s next generation of vehicles.

Rivian described the expansion to include elements of its AI assistant technology as “a proof point for the extensibility of the platform we’ve built in-house.”

Rivian spent two years developing its AI assistant before showing it off last year. That development work continued longer than expected, with the assistant finally rolling out in May 2026 to its flagship R1S SUV, R1T pickup truck, and in October to its new midsize R2 SUV.

Rivian has big aspirations for its AI assistant, which allows drivers and passengers to interact with the vehicle and control certain functions. Rivian founder and CEO RJ Scaringe told TechCrunch onstage at Disrupt 2026 that the company has created its own in-house agentic framework that understands what action a person wants to take when they talk to the vehicle, and routes it to the right part of the car’s software. That capability could eventually allow Rivian vehicles to run AI agents created by other companies, including Anthropic.

The in-house AI assistant program also fits into Rivian’s push to become more vertically integrated — a strategy the company emphasized in its third-quarter earnings report.

“Our relentless focus on vertical integration remains a core competitive differentiator, allowing Rivian to seamlessly unify our hardware and software architecture to deliver significant value to our customers,” the company noted in its investor presentation.

That strategy led to Rivian overhauling its flagship R1T truck and R1S SUV, changing everything from the battery pack and suspension system to the electrical architecture, sensor stack, and software user interface. Its new electric architecture system and compute platform slashed the amount of wiring used and reduced the total number of electronic control units, or computers, used to control the vehicle from 17 different ECUs to seven. That foundation, Rivian argued, would allow it to build vehicles that can handle the computing demands required for advanced software and automated driving.

That technology caught the attention (and money) of VW, which has struggled to offer customers a modern software-centric vehicle found in competitors like Tesla.

Rivian made that vertical-integration case repeatedly in its investor presentation, noting that because it controls the entire technology stack, including compute, sensors, displays, lighting, and sound, “it can roll out rapid, vehicle-wide OTA updates that go well beyond the basics.”

Rivian’s new R2 SUV was its best-selling vehicle in the third quarter, the company said Thursday, marking an auspicious start for the all-important EV.

While Rivian didn’t say exactly how many R2s it sold over the three-month period, the company is confident enough in the pace of sales that it raised its delivery guidance for Wall Street. Rivian had previously expected to deliver between 65,000 and 70,000 EVs this year. On Thursday, it bumped up that floor to 67,000.

It’s the second time Rivian has raised its delivery guidance this year, and the company is now on track to break a three-year stalemate during which it struggled to ship more than 50,000 vehicles annually. The company launched the R2 just in June, but it has made a major bet on scaling production of the lower-cost SUV at a historically quick rate.

The early success of the R2 is also a sign that Rivian is finding new customers despite last year’s loss of the $7,500 federal EV tax credit. The death of that tax credit, along with changes made by the Trump administration to U.S. regulations that supported the growth of EVs, has caused many major automakers to shift their attention back to internal combustion and hybrid vehicles — leaving room in the market for pure-EV companies like Tesla and Rivian.

“In a U.S. automotive marketplace starved for high-quality EV choice, R2 is an attractively priced option for everyday adventures that is resonating with a broad set of consumers and we expect will be a game changer for Rivian’s long-term growth and profitability,” the company said in a letter to shareholders released Thursday.

Rivian revealed earlier this month that it built 19,751 vehicles in the third quarter and delivered 19,248. That was roughly 45% more than it delivered in the same period last year and a big jump from the 12,613 vehicles built and 12,194 delivered in the second quarter of 2026. The increase was driven in large part by the company launching a second production shift at its factory in Normal, Illinois.

Rivian said Thursday that the R2 was responsible for “much of the gain quarter-over-quarter,” but didn’t go into specifics. The company generated $2.2 billion in revenue in the third quarter and suffered a net loss of $913 million — down from $1.2 billion in the same period last year.

Rivian launched the R2 at a starting price of just under $60,000. A more affordable “premium” trim is supposed to be available later this year that starts at $53,990. Even cheaper versions are slated for 2027, which will be available for as low as $44,990.

Rivian reported its third quarter earnings of 2026, providing us a closer look at the company’s financial health as it ramps up production of its all-important R2 electric vehicle — which is now, for the first time, outselling all of Rivian’s other EVs.

Rivian said it brought in $2.155 billion in revenue, a 38 percent increase compared to the $1.558 billion in Q3 2025. Of that amount, $1.55 billion came from automotive revenue, which is up 36 percent year over year. Comparatively, the company reported $605 million from software and subscription services, a 45 percent increase year over year. As EV sales slow down, the company is seeking to make more money from its digital products, including the Autonomy Plus package.

Automotive gross profits were on an upward trajectory, a sign that the company’s finances were improving as it delivered more vehicles. Rivian made $176 million in gross profit this quarter, a $152 million improvement compared to the same time last year. The company still lost $80 million in Q3 automotive gross profits, which is much better than the $130 million loss a year ago.

In other words, Rivian is losing less than it did a year ago because they sold more cars and made more money per vehicle. (Also, the company continued to sell pollution credits to other carmakers, and also received a tariff refund from the government — all of which was recorded as revenue.)

Rivian brought in $256 million in profit in software and services, up $102 million from last year, with a strong 42 percent profit margin. Most of this cash came from licensing their tech to Volkswagen, alongside customer subscriptions for Rivian’s Autonomy Plus driving features. In many ways, Rivian’s tech partnership with Volkswagen and software sales are currently paying the bills, keeping the company profitable overall while it absorbs a lot of heavy upfront costs from launching the R2.

Earlier this month, Rivian reported a record sales quarter, with 19,751 vehicles produced and 19,248 delivered. That represents an 85 percent year-over-year increase in production and a 45 percent jump in deliveries. (For a direct-to-consumer automaker like Rivian, deliveries serve as a proxy for sales.)

Rivian doesn’t break down sales by model, so we don’t exactly how many R2s have reached customers so far. But the company said that the R2 is now the “best-selling Rivian vehicle by volume,” outselling the R1T and R1S, as well as the Electric Delivery Van (EDV).

“We had great results with R2 becoming our best selling vehicle by volume in the third quarter,” Rivian CEO RJ Scaringe said in a statement, ahead of the company’s earnings call this afternoon. “Customer and media feedback on R2 has been exceptional.”

The R2 was designed from the ground up to be Rivian’s higher-volume, mass-market offering — and by overtaking the company’s more expensive, lower-volume legacy vehicles, it seems to be doing just that. Still, the R2 isn’t bringing in the bulk of the revenue yet, as indicated by Rivian’s use of the phrase “by volume.” Most of the money is still coming from the VW partnership and its software division.

But that could change once Rivian starts shipping the more affordable versions of the R2, like the $55,485 Premium trim (coming in late 2026), the $49,985 Standard Long-Range (early 2027), and the $46,495 Standard (late 2027).

Polymarket Halted International Trading After Hackers Drained Accounts

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Trading has resumed on Polymarket’s flagship international platform after a hack that drained money from the wallets of some traders.

A third-party vendor based in the United Kingdom was breached, not the prediction market’s website itself. Law enforcement in the UK have been notified of the incident, the company said. Trading was initially paused while the company investigated for several hours.

On X, prominent traders complained that they had lost money; blockchain analytics firm Bubblemaps reported that around 700 users appeared to have been affected, with approximately $2.7 million stolen. Polymarket declined to comment when WIRED asked how many traders were impacted by the breach and how much money was lost, but the company said it will refund impacted traders.

The hack did not impact users on Polymarket US, the company’s newer platform for people within the United States. Polymarket runs two separate prediction markets; Polymarket US operates as a federally regulated financial exchange within the country, while the flagship Polymarket is a cryptocurrency-based platform that is banned in the United States and a number of other countries.

This is not the first time that Polymarket traders have experienced security woes. In June 2026, Polymarket experienced another breach, which resulted in over $2 million in losses for impacted traders.

The company has been a lightning rod for controversy since its 2020 launch. In the past few years, it has been the center of several high-profile alleged insider trading incidents resulting in arrests. One case involved a Special Forces soldier accused of wagering on the capture of Venezuela’s former leader; another involved a Google employee who allegedly used confidential information to make millions. In both cases, the suspects have pleaded not guilty.

Polymarket recently raised $1 billion in August 2026, with a fundraising round led by 1789 Capital, the venture capital firm where Donald Trump Jr. is a partner. It is now valued at $21 billion.

General Motors has agreed to pay $220 million to settle claims that it illegally sold driver and location information to data brokers, as reported earlier by Reuters. The agreement, which resolves a complaint from a coalition of 36 US attorneys general, requires GM to limit the driving data it collects and obtain “express consent” from drivers before collecting their information.

The allegations surround GM’s now-discontinued Smart Driver program, which collected data about driving habits, including acceleration, braking, and trip length. GM allegedly passed this information to data brokers, enabling insurance companies to purchase the data and raise premiums. The states also accused GM of failing to notify drivers that they were enrolled in its Smart Driver program, and that the carmaker was collecting their data.

“This agreement addresses Smart Driver, a product we ended in 2024, and reinforces steps we’ve taken to strengthen our privacy practices,” GM spokesperson Charlotte McCoy said in an emailed statement to The Verge. “Safer, more helpful and personalized experiences are enabled by connected vehicle data, and GM is committed to being clear about what information is used, how it’s protected, and the choices customers have about what they share.”

This settlement comes just months after GM agreed to pay $12.75 million to settle similar claims in California. Oregon AG Dan Rayfield and Washington AG Nick Brown co-led the states’ case against GM, which also included New York, Florida, Michigan, Nevada, Pennsylvania, and many others.

“People bought a car, not a tracking device,” Rayfield says in a press release. “GM told drivers this program would help them drive more safely and promised it wouldn’t share their data with insurers without permission. Our investigation found GM sold that data anyway, and some drivers paid for it with higher premiums.”

A Defense Contractor Gave Their Personal AI Agent Access to a Secure Pentagon System

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Earlier this year, sources tell WIRED, a contractor caused a security incident at the Department of Defense by connecting a secure centralized system used by multiple intelligence agencies to their personal account for Claude Code, Anthropic’s AI coding agent. It appears that the agent used by the contractor, who is employed by the global consulting firm Accenture, then sent information about the DOD system, including login credentials, to a high-risk, third-party website.

The centralized system contains controlled unclassified information (CUI), which is considered sensitive and not to be released to the public without review. CUI can include information about critical infrastructure, nuclear submarine propulsion, and vulnerabilities to chemical and biological terrorism. Additionally, security experts say, access to the system could allow for the exploitation of even more sensitive ones.

“It puts all the data that’s in there at risk,” says a source aware of the incident, who requested anonymity as they were not authorized to speak to the press. If the third-party software was compromised, “there could be somebody out there with the ability to log into a secure environment without anyone knowing. It’s 100 percent a national security threat and could cause irreparable harm.”

A DOD spokesperson tells WIRED that “the Department does not comment on the status of its networks or systems.”

“We take all concerns seriously. These allegations were thoroughly and independently investigated and found to be without merit. We fully complied with our legal and contractual obligations,” an Accenture spokesperson tells WIRED. When pressed, Accenture didn’t offer clarification on who conducted the investigation, or dispute that there had been what could be characterized as an incident.

The Accenture employee is alleged to have connected a secure production environment to their personal Claude account so as to allow an AI agent to write and deploy code. Claude is currently barred from being used by DOD staff. (Anthropic directed WIRED to the company’s usage policy, which instructs users not to use its products to “interfere with the operation of military bases and related infrastructure” or to “bypass security controls such as authentication systems, end point protection, or monitoring tools without authorization.”)

The Accenture employee’s AI agent ran on the DOD network for two months before it was identified and the vulnerability could be patched. Sources indicate that despite the security risks posed by the AI coding agent, Accenture employees did not treat the incident as severe or urgent. DOD employees and federal contractors are required to report any cybersecurity incident within 72 hours of its discovery, but sources indicate Accenture did not initially report the breach to the government at all.

“We are in a conflict with Iran right now,” says the employee, noting that Iran has attempted to hack several federal agencies and parts of US infrastructure. “It’s the worst time to be having this kind of cavalier attitude.”

Accenture currently has more than $3.5 billion in contracts with the federal government, nearly $605 million of which are with the DOD. The company also contracts with other federal agencies, such as the Department of Education, the General Services Administration, and the Department of Energy, among others. Earlier this month, the Federal Bureau of Investigation found that an Accenture contractor failed to patch a vulnerability that led to the hacking group ShinyHunters getting access to the details of thousands of the Bureau’s employees. The FBI removed that contractor in early October.

Matt Triner, founder of Hunter Strategy, a Washington, DC-based IT consulting company, says that the issue exemplifies the conflicting demands government contractors face more broadly. “Organizations are often punished for reporting incidents like this, even though it’s the right and legal thing to do,” says Triner. In many cases, he says, a company could lose government contracts by admitting they’ve had a security breach, though he says that an incident like the one described is “absolutely one that would need to be reported.”

Triner also notes that the increasing pressure on contractors to deliver projects faster and at a lower price point is at odds with prioritizing safety and security. “Unfortunately, everyone is operating in a system where there are competing rules and incentives, and that existed before AI, but now it’s the same problem on speed,” says Triner.

The coding environment accessed by the Accenture contractor is run on the Non-classified Internet Protocol Router Network (NIPRNet), a DOD network used for unclassified information. While it does connect to the internet—contractors and employees can access their emails and web browsers through the network—it is heavily secured.

According to the source, in order to automate the process of writing code and spinning up cloud servers, the contractor gave their login credentials to the Claude agent. This gave the agent administrative access and allowed it to bypass the security controls enforced through the normal login process. The AI agent—which was not only writing code but also committing it or saving the code into the system on behalf of the Accenture employee—needed to connect out to the internet to perform its tasks. In doing so, it appears to have sent the contractor’s login credentials, passwords, and a map of NIPRNet out to a third-party website.

WIRED is declining to name the third-party website so as to protect the system from further vulnerability, but the National Institute of Standards and Technology has previously identified it as high risk because it has a vulnerability that could let hackers inject instructions into an AI coding agent to steal credentials.

The system the AI agent accessed contained CUI. “Information from a CUI environment is not supposed to leave that infrastructure,” says the source. “There is no insight about what happened to the information, including the login credentials, after it was sent to [that service], whether it’s been retained”

Depending on the agency and its work, CUI can include anything from personally identifiable information to proprietary business or contracting information. According to the DOD’s website, CUI can include information on “the securing and safeguarding of explosives, hazardous chemicals, or pipelines,” documentation related to the designs of “military or space equipment or technology,” and “the exact location and quantities of radioactive material.”

Distinct from the actual data, a map of it could be incredibly valuable to a hacker. “Once an attacker understands how those things fit together, even if those things are public information, what is sensitive is how we are putting those things together and turning that into actionable intelligence,” says Triner.

While a map might not get a hacker into a system, he says, it could help an adversary move faster once they do have access and “help an attacker understand where the crown jewels are.”

Jake Williams, a former NSA hacker and faculty at IANS, an intelligence and security consultancy, says that an AI agent running code in NIPRNet presents a “huge problem, and there are huge national security impacts.”

Williams notes that while individual pieces of data or information might be considered CUI, “when that unclassified information is aggregated, that can become classified itself.” For instance, the location of certain hazardous materials might be CUI, as might be information about the vulnerability of the facilities that house them. Combined, however, they could reveal information that would be kept classified.

But this is not the only way classified information may have been put at risk. Frequently, tools and code are tested in NIPRNet before being deployed into more sensitive systems like the Secret Internet Protocol Router Network (SIPRNet), the military’s secured network for classified information, and Joint Worldwide Intelligence Communications System (JWICS), a secure intranet for top secret information. Both SIPRNet and JWICS are air gapped, a security measure that means they are not connected to any outside networks. On a practical level, that means SIPRNet and JWICS would be extremely difficult for a hacker to access remotely.

But with the login credentials exfiltrated by an AI agent, a hacker could access NIPRNet and insert a backdoor or other malicious code into a tool or system. Without proper review, this code could end up deployed in one of the air-gapped systems.

“The code could operate completely normally in NIPR and do something malicious and undisclosed in a SIPR network, that is 100 percent possible,” says Williams. Exfiltrating data out of these networks would be difficult, he adds, but malicious code could be used to attack the systems from the inside. “I think the most likely thing would be some kind of distributed denial of service attack,” he says.

Triner says that even something more subtle, such as slowing down intelligence operations and activities, could cause damage and make users lose trust in a tool, even once the problem is fixed.

As vibe coding, or prompting an AI assistant to write the code for a product described by an engineer, is becoming more common, vetting code is becoming more of a challenge. AI is capable of generating code at a volume that far surpasses human productivity, making it time-consuming and difficult to vet.

“When you’re using an agent, you have no idea of the supply chain or even the inference chain of what has ultimately generated the code,” says Williams. “A lot of the code that these LLMs were trained on is itself vulnerable, and they can regurgitate the same kinds of architecture and architectural issues that we see in other code.”

The government does have a way to identify flawed code in NIPRNet. Static Application Security Testing (SAST) scans code for bugs and flaws before allowing it to run. But Williams notes that a hacker-inserted “backdoor is not a vulnerability, even if we may not want it there,” and therefore would likely fly under the radar of SAST’s detection. SAST also does not usually identify structural issues in the logic chain of how code is created.

There’s no indication that hackers were able to get into a classified or top-secret system. Still, Williams says, the incident should raise significant alarms. “Architecturally, any information about NIPR that you can’t see through open-source intelligence, DOD considers a close hold regardless of whether something touches a classified network,” he says.

This is not the first time the DOD has been made vulnerable because of AI-related issues. Last year, ProPublica revealed that Microsoft was using “digital escorts” on its projects with the federal government, wherein American employees with security clearances would be overseeing code written by outsourced workers in China. Experts who spoke to ProPublica said that the program presented a particular security risk because the American employees were often not able to vet the code themselves, leaving openings for malicious code to be inserted unknowingly into government systems.

The new MacBook Pro brings with it a bunch of firsts for Apple laptops, the two flashiest being an OLED display and touchscreen functionality. But as an image-accuracy enthusiast, trained calibrator, and resident display expert, what excites me most about the MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR Display is the technology it uses to display an accurate picture. It’s the first laptop, and only the second product overall, to support Apple CMF 2026 color modes. It could be the future for displays.

CMFs, or color-matching functions, mathematically represent how we perceive color, and there are a few different variations. The specification that’s been the standard for years is CIE 1931 (CIE stands for Commission Internationale de l’Éclairage, or International Commission on Illumination). For nearly a century, CIE 1931 has been a reliable calibration target, and most of the reference modes on the new MacBook use it.

But with the development of new display technologies over the past 20 years that have ultra-narrow band spectra — such as OLED, LEDs with KSF phosphors, and quantum dots — CIE 1931’s shortcomings are increasingly apparent. Even when calibrated to the same D65 white point, the same image on an OLED and quantum dot LED can look identical to one person, but different to another because of biological variations in our eyes. We all have small discrepancies in our eyes — such as the sensitivity of the cones in our eyes, which allow us to see color — and those can change as we age. CIE 1931 doesn’t properly handle that and was created based on only 17 test subjects. It’s not ideal in a professional setting where content needs to look the same regardless of the screen technology it’s on or who’s viewing it. Apple CMF 2026 addresses that, so that displays look the same regardless of the technology they use.

Apple first included its new CMF on the Studio Display XDR, which I reviewed earlier this year and found to be an excellent, albeit expensive, monitor for color professionals because of Apple CMF 2026 and the reference color modes. But the fact that this technology is now on a laptop that’s portable and not just a standalone monitor living on a desk is a big help for professionals on the move.

As on the Studio Display XDR, the MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR Display has two Apple CMF 2026 reference modes — one for P3 color primaries and one that includes both P3 and Adobe RGB color primaries for more flexibility. Peak brightness with both HDR and SDR content is 2,000 nits (on the XDR monitor peak SDR luminance is up to 1,000 nits), and the True Tone setting automatically adjusts HDR tone mapping, brightness, and white point based on ambient lighting. (An option to disable True Tone is in Displays preferences.)

The MacBook doesn’t require using Apple CMF 2026, as there are 14 more reference color modes that use the CIE 1931 color space — it is still the standard, after all — including DICOM for medical imaging (which is cool, but only useful to those in the medical field). The MacBook also supports Apple Pro Display Calibrator, which includes three calibration methods to adjust the reference color modes, or a custom mode, if necessary. There are options for a full calibration or to fine-tune a calibration with the proper measurement gear, or to visually fine-tune the display.

Color science and display technologies have come a long way since 1931, when the CIE 1931 color space was established. Apple’s development of the Apple CMF 2026 and its standardization efforts — the company is currently working with a CIE technical committee on a proposed standard — is an important step to make sure current display technologies look the same to as many people as possible.

Extending support to the new MacBook expands the user base considerably (let’s face it, more people are buying a touchscreen MacBook, even at $2,900, than a $3,300 professional monitor). And more importantly, it gives professionals that can get the most out of it easier, portable access.
