Modern phishing campaigns can compromise American businesses without relying on traditional credential theft. Attackers can abuse legitimate cloud services, authentication mechanisms, and trusted business workflows to gain access to corporate accounts while leaving fewer obvious signs behind.

ANY.RUN's analysis of Skyw4lker, a device code phishing campaign primarily targeting U.S. organizations, shows how this works in practice. The campaign has focused on manufacturing, technology, and healthcare, using business-themed documents, Google-hosted infrastructure, and Microsoft authentication flows to facilitate account compromise.

For security teams, the investigation goes beyond identifying the initial lure. It requires tracing the authentication flow and supporting infrastructure, extracting reliable indicators, and turning those findings into detections that can protect the wider organization.

The scale of phishing in the United States provides an important backdrop. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center recorded 193,407 phishing and spoofing complaints in 2024, making it one of the most frequently reported categories of internet crime. In 2025, the FBI recorded more than 1 million internet crime complaints and $20.9 billion in reported losses, with reported losses increasing by 26% compared with the previous year.

Attackers are also adapting to organizations that have invested heavily in identity security and MFA.

Device code phishing is one example. Rather than collecting credentials through a fake login page, an attacker can initiate a legitimate device authorization flow and convince the victim to complete it. The resulting authentication tokens can then provide access to resources available to the compromised account.

The attacker does not need to reproduce Microsoft's login experience perfectly. The authentication itself can be legitimate; the deception is in convincing the victim to complete an authentication request initiated by the attacker.

Once the process is completed, the attacker can obtain access to the resulting Microsoft 365 tokens.

That makes the attack difficult to evaluate through identity telemetry alone. An organization may see a valid authentication event without immediately seeing the malicious context that caused it. Macmillan Staff Say the Publisher Threw a Party for a Workplace AI Assistant That Was Later Killed

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As staff at the Lower Manhattan headquarters of Macmillan Publishers headed into work one morning in January 2024, they filed past something strange on the walls—flyers welcoming a new member of the team named “NOA.” The cafeteria was festooned with purple balloons and banners, according to several workers present, and with NOA’s name in light-up marquee letters. A plate full of doughnuts spelled out “NOA IS HERE.”

NOA was an acronym for No Ordinary Assistant, which many of Macmillan’s human assistants found “offensive,” multiple employees tell WIRED. In an email to staff, NOA was introduced as an AI chatbot developed by the German software company Chaptr, which is “backed” by Holtzbrinck, Macmillan’s parent company.

The email also assured employees NOA was “a safe environment where you can input proprietary information without the risk of sharing that data with any third party.” Ostensibly, this means NOA was a closed-loop enterprise tool that did not retain or share data for model training.

Macmillan staffers say they were warned during training sessions that NOA could hallucinate and would always need to be fact-checked by humans. Still, employees say, they were encouraged to use NOA for composing emails, drafting copy, doing marketing research, and generating ideas. Multiple staffers say they were told to “only” paste a “percentage” of authors’ manuscripts into NOA. Employees could also adjust NOA’s speaking tone, with options including “lighthearted,” “spartan,” pessimistic,” “sarcastic,” and “regretful.”

One Macmillan employee remembers a Chaptr representative reassuring workers during an early in-person training session that Chaptr understood publishers were dealing with something very important—content. “And here I thought we were publishing literature,” the employee says.

Later in 2024, Macmillan employees were encouraged to use NOA to generate their goals for performance reviews. “We built a specialized prompt to have NOA write goals for you!” one email read.

Several Macmillan employees say NOA was unhelpful and unreliable. “It was terrible, and no one used it,” one says. WIRED spoke with multiple publishing employees for this article, including some who have since left their employers. WIRED is not naming these employees, nor the authors we interviewed, because they are concerned about professional repercussions.

In October 2025, Macmillan staff learned via a company-wide email that Chaptr would be sunsetting NOA. Since then, workers say, leadership has been attempting to integrate Google Gemini into the publishing process. Some staffers subsequently experimented with using Gemini to evaluate submission letters from literary agents, but the Gemini-generated evaluations were “useless,” according to one editor.

In response to WIRED’s request for comment, Kelly Collins, vice president of communications and community at Macmillan, says the publishing house uses Gemini for its “business” but “there has not been any directive from Macmillan leadership to use Gemini for any part of the publishing process, including to evaluate submission letters from agents.” She also says “the points shared do not accurately reflect how NOA was rolled out and communicated” but did not provide further elaboration when WIRED asked which aspects were inaccurate.

Collins notes that the company “takes a thoughtful approach to AI, focused on employee awareness and education while always keeping people front and center.”

Macmillan is usually cited as the smallest of the Big Five US book publishers based on the number of books sold each year. Its imprint Farrar, Straus and Giroux publishes literary fiction heavyweights like Sally Rooney, Yiyun Li, and Ben Lerner while Tor Books is a home for science fiction and fantasy authors John Scalzi, Martha Wells, and Nnedi Okorafor.

Since I first reported on the creep of AI in the publishing world, I’ve heard from staff at all of the Big Five who claim that executives are pushing them to use AI tools, even as Hachette, Macmillan, and Penguin Random House Verlagsgruppe (its German-language division) are suing some of the leading AI companies for copyright infringement.

At the same time, multiple bestselling Big Five authors say they’ve gone to “war” with their publishers over AI and are considering abandoning traditional publishing altogether.

At Penguin Random House (PRH), the largest of the Big Five after the 2013 merger between Penguin Books and Random House, several workers say they’ve been encouraged to use ChatGPT Enterprise in AI learning sessions, even though PRH’s German division is suing OpenAI for copyright infringement.

In February, representatives from Hay House—a new-age self-help publisher acquired by Penguin Random House in 2023—presented a slide deck to some PRH employees about how ChatGPT Enterprise had become a “core productivity, creativity, and collaboration tool” since they adopted it a year prior. One of the four return-on-investment pillars in the deck was “Creativity,” including “idea generation, content development, and storytelling innovations.”

Despite encouragement from the C-suite, all of the PRH workers WIRED spoke with say they’re avoiding ChatGPT whenever possible. “It makes it harder for us to do our jobs,” one employee says. Other staff say their managers have explicitly asked them not to use AI for creative tasks like publicity copy and cover design, which has caused some confusion.

“Penguin Random House approaches AI as we would any significant new technology,” says Claire von Schilling, executive vice president and director of corporate communications and social responsibility for the publisher, via email. “We are exploring ways it may appropriately support our work, guided by clear principles and safeguards, including protecting the intellectual property of our authors and the company and preserving the essential role of human creativity and judgment in publishing.”

Von Schilling also says that PRH’s “publishing groups and imprints have discretion to determine whether and how AI tools may be appropriate for particular workflows, including areas such as marketing, publicity, and design.”

Meanwhile, several bestselling Big Five authors say their relationships with publishers have suffered over the past year due to AI use. Combined, the Big Five authors WIRED spoke with have sold more than 30 million copies. One bestselling author says they received marketing copy that included AI-hallucinated quotes from their book, but the publisher insisted they were not AI-generated. “I was gaslit on all of it,” the author says. During a recent round of contract negotiations, another bestselling author says they could not get a single Big Five publisher to promise no AI would be used in any way on their books.

“It’s undermining the necessary sense of trust that has to exist between writers and publishers,” one author says.

“It’s also frustrating to watch publishers get these massive settlements [from] AI companies and then embrace AI to do their work,” another author says. “It feels like they’re feeding at both sides of the trough.”

Authors say cost-cutting layoffs at the Big Five have contributed to deteriorating conditions for their editors and forced some to embrace AI for survival. “We’re seeing editors not reading the material at all and using Claude to create marketing language that barely has any relation to the book,” one author says. Other authors and agents say they’ve been asked by editors to write their own plans for revisions “because the editors don’t have time to do it themselves.”

All of the authors I spoke with say they want publishers to communicate when and how they’re using AI. “The bare minimum we're asking for is disclosure,” one author says. Another wishes the Big Five “would get together and come up with a set of principles around AI that they intend to adhere to.” But some bestselling authors say their publishers’ use of AI is forcing them to consider publishing future books on their own.

“What are authors even doing with traditional publishers anymore if they’re going to outsource everything to a machine?” one bestselling author says. “Why can't an author do that themselves?”

Following WIRED's previous reporting, several high-profile authors condemned the adoption of AI tools in publishing. “I have never looked down on other publishing models (zine girly from way back) & every day, self pub looking ever more possible for future work,” Mo Ryan wrote on Bluesky. Her most recent book, Burn It Down: Power, Complicity, and a Call for Change in Hollywood, was published by HarperCollins.

John Scalzi, the sci-fi novelist who signed a 10-book deal with Macmillan’s Tor Publishing Group in 2024, wrote, “I would be EXTREMELY annoyed to discover my PR is ‘AI’-generated, and as someone bombarded with dozens of ‘AI’-generated emails daily, I assure you the stink of ‘AI’ is immediately noticable [sic] and immediately dismissible. Publishers are not doing their new books any favors in using it.”

Bestselling authors who have already accumulated huge readerships say they don’t need the institutional support of a Big Five publisher to sell copies. Brandon Sanderson, for instance, publishes some of his fantasy novels through Tor Books, an imprint of Macmillan, but raised more than $15 million in 24 hours from his readers on Kickstarter to publish four books on his own in 2022.

“Publishers have always seen authors as a bit of a nuisance, and now there’s a desire to make us obsolete,” says one bestselling Big Five author. “What’s fascinating to me is that in the process of trying to make authors obsolete by using AI, the publishers are rendering themselves obsolete.”

Please Let the Internet Die

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The internet must be put out of its misery.

Any rational observer can admit that the current iteration of the web—and the social fabric woven there—is not what they signed up for. Symptoms of advanced cultural decay can no longer be ignored. This thing is eating itself.

Giants including Meta and Google have been forced to settle massive consumer lawsuits brought over privacy violations and mental health harms allegedly caused by the addictive mechanisms of their platforms. Schools are banning smartphones as multiple countries institute social media restrictions for under-16s. Parents are in a panic about their kids aspiring to careers as social media celebrities, losing themselves in a vast ecosystem of online casinos, or joining extremist movements.

Generative AI, briefly considered an amusing novelty, is now a turbocharged slop engine for the web, filling feeds and comment sections with useless, grotesque content. As humans scroll past headlines about AI taking their jobs, confusing fantasy for reality, and contributing to cognitive decline, any consumer appeal the technology might have is turning to anguish. The data centers that power it have become increasingly unpopular, and politically radioactive.

To be online, many now admit, is to be endlessly manipulated and exploited. In December, Oxford University Press declared rage bait to be the word of the year, noting that use of the term had tripled over the previous 12 months. The lexical spike represented a heightened awareness that monetized social accounts churn out bad-faith takes and extremist content, automating this abusive behavior with generative AI, simply to profit off hostile engagement. Billionaire oligarchs have opened the floodgates to such inauthentic activity, conspiracy-mongering, and hate speech by rolling back moderation.

In short, when you hang around online in 2026, you see little worth saving. Perhaps a decade ago, you felt that the glimmers of community and joy, of artistic and intellectual exchange, were enough to balance out the toxic junk. No longer.

It’s not necessarily that people have gotten worse. “Dead Internet Theory”—the proposition that a vast majority of the interactions on the web are fake, bots talking to other bots—has accrued more supporting evidence recently. One cybersecurity firm concluded this spring that agents already outnumber human users, their traffic surging by 8,000 percent through 2025. No wonder seemingly everyone has at some point caught themselves scrolling through a wasteland of brainrot and asked, “What the hell am I even looking at?” Underlying that question is another: “Is it always going to be like this?”

With this anxiety hammering at my prefrontal cortex day in and out, I was more than intrigued by the absolutist title of technologist and Pulitzer-winning journalist John West’s new book, The Internet Will Die, and So Will You: On Learning to Live in Our Digital Age. In his discerning treatise, West confirms what many have suspected for a good while now: The platforms once seen as enduring archives of knowledge and communication are being cannibalized by ideological warfare and basic greed.

But that’s not the whole story. For one, West argues, people misperceive the “vastness” of the internet, which really amounts to “a vanishing fraction, a rounding error, of the human experience.” Moreover, it is remarkably fragile—West opens with the story of how a 2001 train derailment in a Baltimore tunnel took out a major bundle of fiber-optic cables, leading to internet slowdowns nationwide—and continually undergoes cycles of death and rebirth as a result of this natural instability.

If the internet is much smaller and less solid than imagined, West’s book suggests, then it’s crucial to recognize the forces that determine how those deaths unfold, or which pieces are preserved while others crumble. Because the market has falsely sold us the web as a comprehensive library of accumulated wisdom, each transient phase of this global network comes to define the present, for better and worse. Today we witness a dismal paralysis brought on by algorithmic incentives to churn out inflammatory garbage: Full-time influencers are in a losing arms race for attention with low-effort, high-volume channels that streamline production by delegating the creative process to bleeding-edge software.

West draws a sharp contrast between this holographic environment of monetization and a passionate, personalized internet that spanned from the primordial GeoCities to Tumblr, describing how these innovative communities have been gradually erased. While some endure in half-dead and highly corrupted form, others are lost. In middle school, West maintained a web page devoted to his love of anime; later, he kept a blog documenting his college years. Looking back as an adult, he’s vexed by the fates that have befallen each.

“I really wanted to delete my anime site,” West tells WIRED. “I don't have access to the AOL account anymore, and I couldn't do it.” Instead, it lives on as a zombie page, cluttered with spam ads and dead-end links. As for the college blog, he had neglected to pay domain fees toward the end of his time writing it, and so the final year of posts disappeared. “I kind of got obsessed with this idea that the things we want to last really don't, and then oftentimes the things that we kind of wish would go away hang on for way too long,” West says.

These shifting sands are a far cry from the flawless, collective, permanent memory that internet users originally believed they were building. “‘Forever’ is such a strong word, and it's the wrong word,” West says. Online spaces are memento mori, the same as individuals who self-promote across them (and that includes certain longevity-obsessed biohackers). Sure, there was a time when a celebrity could be “canceled” for tasteless posts unearthed years later, but in 2026, it’s not so easy to surface specific messages when you go hunting for them in your AI-powered Gmail account. Zeitgeist-defining platforms like Vine can be vaporized in an instant, a fact reinforced in January 2025, when Americans posted farewell TikTok videos ahead of an expected US ban on the app.

This random ephemerality, West points out, has eroded any confidence in claims previously backed up by hypertext cross-references, and thus any kind of consensus truth. An analysis of hyperlinks in articles on The New York Times website between 1996 and mid-2019 found that 25 percent of all links were inaccessible. According to a 2016 study, “three out of four citations in scholarly articles have drifted—the websites they purport to cite no longer say what they once did,” he writes. “Over half of Wikipedia’s entries contain at least one link in its references section that goes nowhere, a Pew report found.”

The impulse to drive engagement spreads to users themselves: West noticed, for instance, that when he recently reread a favorite book, Marilynne Robinson’s Gilead, he had the nagging thought that while he loved returning to the novel for the fourth or fifth time, he wouldn’t be able to count it toward his annual Goodreads stats. West says this fixation on the internet’s hollow validations creates “the exact conditions for a space to be filled by something that isn't human.”

Wouldn’t it be liberating, then, to actually deny our attention to astroturfed outrage, engineered virality, and influencer sponcon? To let the mainstream digital economies propped up by AI-enabled surveillance finally fall to ruin? If what the titans of the industry have to offer us these days are “pervert glasses” and the suppression of ads for a probably unflattering documentary, why are we helping them stay in business? Leaving their platforms for dead might be the last lever of power available to the masses.

Doing so could free people to explore dormant corners. West notes that some of the most interesting parts of the internet are the ones that nobody sees, like web portals to streams of YouTube videos that have almost no views. “Some of it’s fucking weird and creepy, but a lot of it’s just people singing a song and having a blast,” he says. West also cites the bustling, zero-revenue, circulation of poetry in web journals as proof that “you can find meaning and make meaning online all the time,” provided there is no financial motivation.

When you eliminate what the platforms want you to fixate on, it’s clear what you’ve been missing, and how you were steered away from it. Your opinions are shaped in a bubble by avatars you’ve never met, weaponized against equally hermetic rival camps. The internet doesn’t really have winners, unless you count the few without the sense to stop chasing relevance at any cost. That’s why the main characters are so malleable in their beliefs; never afraid to pivot, betray, and discard what they once held to be the core of their identity. And that’s when they stood for anything to begin with.

Absent a sudden, lasting outage due to a fresh and severe catastrophe, the internet as it exists won’t perish anytime soon. There are, however, splintering cracks in its facade, which may create room for emergent and strangely evolved life-forms. There’s no reason to assume that the present tech empire will rule the century to come; the organic and anarchic web stands to mount a triumphant renaissance if (or when) it falls.

“Look, the internet is rotting,” West says. “The question is, where are we going to put our energy?” A metaphor he often turns to in his book is compost. “Compost is just rot harnessed for a purpose,” he says. Can a decaying internet be fertilizer for something better? It’s a rosy notion, but speaking as a perennially failed composter—it’s just too gross, and I’m too lazy—I have my doubts about whether enough hardy, patient gardeners can be mobilized to make our digital landscape bloom once more.

Then again, as West’s book sought to remind me, I am destined to die as well. Maybe I’ll be mixed in with the soil.

The Men Spending $50,000 Just to Reserve a Tesla Roadster

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In mid-October, CEO Elon Musk sort of made good on a long-standing promise: Tesla’s new second-gen Roadster flew through the air. Technically, the vehicle used compressed air to “thrust” itself—the “flying” lasted mere seconds, and the system takes six to seven hours to refill.

Never mind, though—some buyers were eager to unsheath their credit cards to reserve one of the cars. “I was freaking out,” says Alex Lawrence, the owner of used-electric-vehicle dealership EVAuto, who already had one reservation for a second-gen Roadster and watched the demonstration live online. “I placed another one.”

It’s not a decision most would make lightly. Tesla took years to get the sports car even close to production. The automaker first introduced the vehicle, a successor to its first car ever, as a surprise at a 2017 event, and immediately began taking $50,000 reservations. At the time, the plan was to deliver the cars in 2020; Musk and company now say the vehicle will enter production in 2028, with delivery dates unclear. (If reservation holders had put that money into Tesla stock, they would be up nearly $1 million today.)

The Roadster is also expensive to reserve—and will likely get pricier to buy. The vehicle’s base model, which doesn’t include acceleration thrusters or the flight system, still costs $50,000 to reserve. The second-tier Thrust variant, which comes with the thrusters, requires a $250,000 deposit. The actual flying car, called Rocket, is listed as “invite-only,” and even Musk has acknowledged the vehicle won’t be street legal. Meanwhile, the automaker’s reservation terms note that purchase prices are “only an estimate”—and those estimates don’t even exist yet.

Whatever! “I’m going to do everything in my power to get the flying one,” Lawrence says. When he sees the Roadster, after all, he sees money signs. His EV dealership has a large and growing social media following, and the business, not the man, technically put down the money for the Roadster reservations. “We’re going to put it on the track and use it for marketing content,” he says.

Utah-based tech founder Travis Cook upped his nearly 10-year-old reservation for the base Roadster variant to the Thrust because he actually wants to live with the thing. He calls himself a “car aficionado” and has a dealer’s license so he can buy and sell fun cars. He loves little more than driving an unusual vehicle, he told WIRED from behind the wheel of a Cybertruck as he used the company’s Full Self-Driving (Supervised) feature.

“When I roll up somewhere, all the other car aficionados come out, and they're so excited,” he says. “I just get a buzz from that.”

Cook says he has no regrets about his years-old reservation, though he may get frustrated if the price shoots up closer to half a million. “I’m not sure I’m willing to drop too much money on the car—but I probably would,” he says. Close family tolerates his obsession, though his wife doesn’t enjoy going 0 to 60 in his other cars and probably won’t in the Roadster, either. “She’s not going to want to go in it,” he says.

The tech founder has faith in Musk and Tesla. The October demonstration was even crazier than he had expected. “When it launched off the top of the ramp and went and landed, my jaw was on the floor,” Cook says.

Despite the Roadster’s memeable debut, the vehicle doesn’t seem to be a top priority for Tesla. Musk has said for years now that the automaker isn’t really in the business of making cars anymore and instead is focused on robotics, artificial intelligence, and autonomy. He has touted the new Roadster as the company’s last human-driven car ever. The supercar garnered just two mentions in Tesla’s hourlong earnings call with investors this month, with Musk calling it “arguably the coolest demo of any product ever.” Musk, meanwhile, has other preoccupations—he has donated at least $445 million to Republican candidates ahead of the US midterms.

But Lawrence believes Musk will pull off what he says he will and that his own business’s investment in the Roadster will be worth it in the end. The looping, wedge-shaped car demo left him a little nostalgic, a little existential.

“We don’t do crazy stuff like this anymore, like going to the moon. There’s not a lot today that will stop you in your tracks,” he says. “I felt like I was 15, watching that.”

Law Firm Linked to Elon Musk Started an ‘Election Integrity’ Organization

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The law firm that helped set up Elon Musk’s America PAC has created a new, nationwide “election integrity” organization aligned with Donald Trump’s false claims of election fraud, WIRED has learned.

The organization, a nonprofit called the Association of State and Local Election Officials, was established by a lawyer then at Lex Politica, the Musk-linked law firm founded by Chris Gober, who was chief counsel for America PAC in 2024 and is now running in Texas for a spot in the House of Representatives.

AI Emergency Alerts Are Coming. Humans Might Not Be Ready

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This story was produced by Grist and copublished with WIRED. It is part of Distorted, a Grist series exploring the spread of misinformation in the wake of extreme weather, and is published with support from the CO2 Foundation.

Earlier this month, after Hurricane Isaias made landfall on the Gulf Coast as a Category 3 hurricane, the emergency management expert Laura Myers got a call from a meteorologist in Alabama. Myers is director of the University of Alabama’s Center for Advanced Public Safety, and she talks to state and local emergency managers around her region all the time. The meteorologist told Myers that a local emergency manager had published a graphic showing where Isaias might threaten the Alabama coast with a “sting jet” of high winds.

The problem? The emergency manager had generated the graphic using an artificial intelligence chatbot, and it wasn’t accurate.

“That county emergency manager was trying to get his stakeholders to focus on the risk in that county, and so he had asked AI to help him,” she said. “It did what he wanted it to do, but it was not meteorologically accurate.”

Most emergency management agencies don’t permit employees to use generative AI, but Myers and most other emergency management experts agree that its usage is likely already pervasive at state and local disaster departments. There are hundreds of companies that are marketing AI tools to emergency managers for tasks like flood damage assessment and resource coordination, but public communication is one of the areas where government employees and startups have the most interest.

Emergency department personnel with the city of New York monitor the watch-command room in Brooklyn on February 23, 2026, ahead of a winter storm.

The reason? Most emergency managers aren’t very good at sending out alerts and quickly communicating about disasters with the public. That’s not because they’re emergency managers, but because they are human beings. They tend to write in jargon that non-experts might not understand, leave out information on the assumption that the public already knows it, and include irrelevant details that could distract people from what they actually do need to know.

“A lot of agencies are really limited in resources and the number of people that they have,” said Jeannette Sutton, a disaster researcher at the University of Albany who has created a comprehensive dashboard that provides templates for disaster alerts. “I think they recognize it as a priority, but they don't prioritize it until they realize they should have. They're just trying to get their job done without screwing something up.”

Emergency managers are the government officials who are in charge of disaster response and recovery. At the state and local level, It is these officials and their agencies, not the Federal Emergency Management Agency, that make up the first line of defense after an extreme weather event. As such, emergency managers often like to say that “trust is our currency,” and it’s in their communication with the public that they build and lose this trust.

As climate disasters become more frequent and widespread, there have been numerous examples of flawed human communication scrambling an emergency response. In 2016, for instance, adjacent counties in California issued a “voluntary evacuation” and a “mandatory evacuation” for the same wildfire. In Australia in 2019, officials warned about wildfires by using the phrase “Watch and Act,” which the government later said caused “significant confusion” because no one knew what it meant.

In some cases, inadequate communication can mean the difference between life and death. This happened in Montecito, California, after the 2017 Thomas Fire. A few weeks after the fire, a heavy rainstorm produced a deadly mudslide. The government of Santa Barbara County struggled to communicate the risk to residents still traumatized from the blaze and experiencing a phenomenon that Sutton calls “warning fatigue.” The county sent alerts to everyone in the “voluntary evacuation zone” for wildfires, telling residents to “stay alert to changing conditions and be prepared to leave immediately.” Most of those residents should have left immediately, but to many the evacuation notice was optional; others just didn’t want to evacuate for a second time in a month. The mudslide killed 23 people and injured more than 160 others.

Governments that put out bad warnings are apt to lose the public’s trust, said Sutton. “They're losing the opportunity to be that voice to help people to be safe.”

In theory, alerts are a perfect use case for artificial intelligence, and in particular for the generative AI systems that have developed at a dramatic pace over the past few years. FEMA provides training sessions for emergency managers to learn best practices for synthesizing and sending alerts, but many officials don’t remember all their training when an emergency arrives. A large language model is different. It can read hundreds of thousands of alerts in mere minutes, digest all the research on what works in similar scenarios, and then apply that to edit a human alert in seconds.

“If you’ve only sent one [alert] in your life, and that was 15 years ago, and you need to remember all your training, and you’re thinking ‘Hey, there's a chemical plume coming my way, the phone's ringing, the radio's blaring, I need help,’ the AI is probably gonna do a better job than you are,” said Rob Dale, a former meteorologist who now runs the emergency management department in Ingham County, Michigan.

Dale has already used AI to draft a few practice alerts behind the scenes, but he’s never had to send one out. Ingham County is a small county in central Michigan, and it’s immune from major weather events like hurricanes and fires. If such an event did happen, Dale said he would have no issue allowing a chatbot to draft his incident communication, but he’d still want control over sending the alert out.

Some experts believe the technology is already good enough to deploy. EM1, a startup that produces AI software for emergency managers, has used Sutton’s research to train a model that its executives say is already better than almost any human emergency manager. The company has tested this in-house model on a number of scenarios including wildfires and tsunamis. It can already match or exceed human capabilities on most benchmark tests.

Sutton gave Grist an example of how the software works: Grist provided her with a recent alert sent by a New Mexico county that was trying to warn residents about flooding on the San Juan River.

After a series of erroneous evacuation alerts to L.A. residents sparked panic during an active wildfire season in January 2025, the director of the county’s emergency management department apologized.

The original alert read, “Breeched banks. The water is rising. EVACUATE NOW. McGee Park open for people and pets, RV.” Sutton asked the EM1 model to evaluate and revise the alert. In a few seconds, it produced what she said is a far better alert, one that checks all the main boxes of disaster communication.

The new alert read: “San Juan County: Floodwater is rising near [location]. People in [evacuation area]: LEAVE NOW for higher ground. Do not walk or drive through floodwater. McGee Park is open for people and pets. Check [official update source] for updates.” The model identified and fixed several problems with the original text, which does not say who is sending the alert, name the specific location where flooding is happening, or clarify who needs to evacuate and give advice on how to do so safely. It misspells the word “breached,” and it doesn’t provide any guidance on where residents can seek further information.

EM1 has contracts with around a hundred government agencies and school districts, but none are using it to automate their extreme weather alerts just yet. For the moment, they’re only using it to workshop plans and internal preparations. Even so, all the experts interviewed for this story said they are certain that many emergency managers are already using chatbots to write messages, even if they won’t admit it. Sutton said that the emergency managers who take her alert training sessions often present her with high-quality alerts they have drafted in seconds using ChatGPT or Gemini.

“Official adoption is pretty low, but people are unofficially using it, absolutely,” said Sarah Miller, a professor of emergency management at Pierce College in Florida and a longtime instructor at the National Disaster Preparedness Training Center. “They're using their own personal accounts, or they're using free ones.”

It sounds like an easy trade-off: if AI is better and faster than humans, and the difference between a good alert and a bad alert can be life and death, we should be using AI for every heat wave and wildfire. Yet even researchers who are bullish on chatbot technology say that making the switch could backfire. Members of the public who are skeptical of AI or who don’t trust the government in the first place may spurn agencies that publish AI-generated graphics or social media posts. In Syracuse, New York, for instance, a union of public employees sued the local government for using an AI service to answer non-emergency 911 calls.

“We trust things that we perceive [are] authentic and care about us,” said Ginny Katz, the founder of HazAdapt, a consultancy that helps disaster officials figure out how to use technology. “AI-generated content does not feel authentic.” There’s also a risk that chatbots could allow emergency managers to send out more alerts, and faster, which could increase the risk of warning fatigue.

As is the case in other areas where AI is being deployed, disaster experts say that for the moment the software is only as capable as the person using it. A seasoned emergency manager might be able to speed up their communication with the public by having a chatbot draft a series of alerts, but an inexperienced one rushing to create an urgent alert won’t know how to evaluate the output of a model like ChatGPT or Claude.

Even though the technology is a ways off from being able to perform without a human expert in the loop, that hasn’t stopped many understaffed emergency management departments from using it. As for how the proliferation of AI-generated content is affecting public trust in government, experts say it’s too early to tell.

“If you had a human sending you a text message versus a computer sending you a text message, which one would you believe is being more accurate?” said Justin Snair, the founder of Preppr, another company that offers AI software to emergency managers.

The answer, he said, depends on the person receiving it.

Anthropic's Unreleased AI Model Chains Flaws Into Working Chrome, Safari, Windows and Linux Exploits

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An unreleased Anthropic AI model found security flaws in Chrome, Safari, Android, Windows, and Linux and chained them into working proof-of-concept exploits, the company said in its latest report.

The exploits were built in the lab after the model was prompted, not seen in any real attack, and Anthropic reported every flaw to the vendor first, so all of them are already patched. One proof of concept opened the Calculator app on a stock Mac running Chrome within seconds of a page loading, with no further clicks.

Anthropic's Frontier Red Team described five chains in a research post. The strongest claim is not any single bug, but that one model found several separate flaws and combined them into a working exploit, something the company says has until now been the work of a few specialist teams. That capability claim rests on Anthropic's own account.

The patches and the fact that Apple and Google credit Anthropic and its Claude model for specific fixes can be verified with the vendors.

What a reader cannot check is how much of each chain the model built on its own. Anthropic says the work needed only "initial prompting" and "little human involvement," but it does not break down what a person did at each step, and it does not name the model beyond calling it an unreleased, internal research model.

For the one flaw with its own public record, the Windows bug, the record names no finder at all. Here are the five chains, what each would do, the condition each needs, and where the fix landed.

In the Chrome chain, the model used four separate flaws in turn. A malicious page first corrupted memory in V8, Chrome's JavaScript engine.

A second flaw escaped V8's memory sandbox and took control of the part of Chrome that renders the page. A race condition in a feature called CacheStorage then let that code read memory it should not reach and learn the layout of the more privileged browser process.

A final use-after-free bug in FedCM, Chrome's sign-in feature, let the exploit run code outside the sandbox entirely. Anthropic says it reported 62 flaws to Google that were assigned CVEs and fixed as of Chrome 156, all rated critical or high, including 13 sandbox escapes.

Google's own release notes credit "Xinyang Ge of Anthropic, assisted by Claude" for specific Chrome fixes, though not for all 62.

The Safari chain is different because it never runs code at all. It chains flaws to obtain full read and write access to the browser's memory, then changes a single value to turn off the same-origin policy, the rule that prevents one website from reading another's data.

The Windows chain is the one with an independent paper trail, and it is also the narrowest. It is a denial-of-service attack: a few network packets crash the machine, and repeating them keeps it down, but it does not run code or steal data. Anthropic describes the flaw as an out-of-bounds read in the Windows kernel's QUIC handling.

The public record for the bug it points to, CVE-2026-33096, instead names HTTP.sys, Windows' built-in web request handler, and rates it 7.5 out of 10. No open source ties those two descriptions together as the same component. The CVE record credits no finder, so Anthropic's authorship of this one rests on its own post.

As of late October, the U.S. government's vulnerability enrichment marked the flaw as not exploited, and it was not in the CISA catalog of known exploited bugs.

The Linux chain is the most serious in terms of impact: it runs commands as root over the network without a login. It needs the target server to be running a multipath TCP listener. The model first leaks a kernel memory address to bypass a defense called KASLR, then reuses the same race-condition bug to redirect the program to code the attacker has already placed, resulting in a root shell.

Anthropic says its proof of concept took 48 minutes to leak the address and five more to reach the shell, and that a failed attempt does not crash the system, so an attacker can keep retrying.

The company ties this chain to CVE-2026-98432, a kernel use-after-free rated 9.8 and fixed upstream on October 15; the public record for that CVE describes an IPv4 TCP fix rather than a multipath TCP one, and the two are linked only by Anthropic's post.

Anthropic frames the work as help for defenders. It says it reported each flaw under a coordinated disclosure policy that gives developers 90 days to patch and 45 more before it discusses details beyond a public advisory, and it points security teams to its expanded Cyber Verification Program, which gives vetted professionals access to Claude Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Mythos 5.1.

OrioleDB public beta: a Postgres storage engine for update-heavy tables

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

OrioleDB, an open-source storage engine for Postgres, is now in public beta on every Supabase plan. We announced the public beta at Supabase Select, alongside Multigres and DBArena. Pick OrioleDB when you create a project. It's billed the same as standard Postgres. Compute and disk resizing, read replicas, Postgres upgrades, and scheduled backups work. It's not ready for production yet.

Postgres stores table rows in a format called the heap. An update doesn't change a row in place. Postgres writes a new version and leaves the old one behind as a dead tuple. Because every index points to a row’s physical location, the new version usually needs a new entry in every index on the table, even indexes on columns that didn’t change. Deletes leave dead tuples too.

Postgres cleans up the old row versions with a process called VACUUM, and a background process called autovacuum runs it automatically once enough dead tuples build up in a table. On tables with a lot of updates or deletes, autovacuum can fall behind. This creates bloat: tables and indexes grow larger than the data they hold, and queries get slower because they read more pages. Teams end up tuning autovacuum settings per table, scheduling manual VACUUM runs, or using tools like pg_repack to rebuild tables.

Heap storage also has limits at high concurrency. A lookup by primary key has to read both the index and the table, every read goes through a shared buffer pool that many connections compete for, and write-heavy workloads generate a lot of disk I/O at each checkpoint. On larger machines, these costs can keep Postgres from using all the CPU and I/O you’re paying for.

OrioleDB replaces the heap through the Table Access Method, Postgres's plug-in interface for storage engines. Your connection strings, Auth, Row Level Security policies, and client libraries stay the same.

OrioleDB has been in development since 2021, and its creators are part of the Supabase team. This beta ships with OrioleDB beta19 on Postgres 17.11.

We benchmark OrioleDB on DBArena, our open benchmarking site for hosted Postgres. It runs a workload derived from TPC-C against Supabase Postgres, Supabase with OrioleDB, Amazon RDS, and Google Cloud SQL across seven compute sizes. It tests two scenarios: one where the data fits in memory and one where it is four times larger than memory. The benchmark code, methodology, and raw results for every run are public, so you can check our numbers or rerun them yourself. This workload is derived from the TPC-C Benchmark and is not comparable to published TPC-C Benchmark results, as this implementation does not comply with all requirements of the TPC-C Benchmark.

When the data doesn’t fit in memory, OrioleDB had the highest throughput per dollar of any product at every compute size we tested. On an 8XL instance (32 vCPU, 128 GiB of RAM), it completed about 1.8x more transactions per minute than Supabase Postgres on heap storage, reaching 113,850 transactions per minute. At the Small size, at the same price, it delivered about 46% more throughput per dollar than heap. When the data fits in memory, the gap is smaller. OrioleDB was about 13% ahead of heap at Large and about 29% ahead at 8XL. At Small, heap came out ahead. You can compare the results for any size and scenario.

The performance benefits of OrioleDB depend on your workload. OrioleDB helps most with tables that are updated or deleted often, such as order status, inventory counts, job queues, and session data, since those are the tables where heap storage builds up the most bloat. It also helps when your data set is larger than memory, when many connections are reading and writing at once on a large instance, and when most queries look up rows by primary key. Read-mostly workloads that fit comfortably in memory will see a smaller difference. Workloads that depend on non-B-tree indexes, like pgvector HNSW, may not see gains yet, since those indexes run through an experimental bridge. We recommend benchmarking your own workload before drawing conclusions.

Tailscale Patches SSH Flaws That Could Let Hacked Devices Gain Root Access on Other Servers

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tailscale has released version 1.104.4 to fix flaws in its built-in SSH server that could let an attacker reach services and files on other servers that only the root user should be able to touch.

The flaws are in how Tailscale SSH forwards network connections, a job the Tailscale background process carries out with root privileges. To use them, an attacker must already control a device that the network's access rules permit to open an SSH session to the target, with forwarding allowed in that rule.

The flaws come from a report called Tailworm, published days before the fix by Erica Windisch of Cyberdione Labs, who tested released versions of the Tailscale client and the self-hosted headscale control server in a lab.

Tailscale SSH decides who can connect using a device's identity and the network's policy file, not a password or key, so an attacker does not need stolen credentials, only a foothold on a device the policy already trusts.

The flaws "don't allow an unauthenticated attacker to connect to arbitrary Tailscale SSH servers," Tailscale said, and an attacker can still reach only the machines the policy file already allows.

Tailscale SSH's server runs only on Linux and the open-source macOS build; the attacker's own device can be any platform.

The problem is in socket forwarding. When a permitted SSH session forwarded a connection to a Unix socket on the server, the Tailscale process checked the path but then operated as root. A timing trick let an attacker swap in a different path after that check, defeating the safeguard meant to limit which sockets forwarding could reach.

In Windisch's own tests, forwarding to the Docker control socket produced a root shell and read the system password file, /etc/shadow, with no administrator rights. Tailscale's bulletin describes the reach more narrowly, saying an attacker could get to root-only sockets such as the Docker socket or the tailscaled local API.

In 1.104.4, Tailscale changed the server to carry out forwarding as the logged-in user rather than as root, and to rely on the operating system's own file permissions in place of its earlier path check. The release covers 4 security bulletins:

Users of Tailscale SSH should update to version 1.104.4 or later. If you cannot update a server yet, you can turn off SSH forwarding on it by setting TS_SSH_DISABLE_FORWARDING=1 in the tailscaled service environment and restarting the service, which Tailscale says turns off forwarding without disabling Tailscale SSH and blocks several of the more serious findings.

Tailscale also recommends reviewing SSH access rules so that only the users and devices that need access have it.

Tailscale suggests using SSH check mode, which requires a user to sign in again before a session starts, for sensitive systems. Check mode does not stop these forwarding flaws: it guards the start of a session, whereas the forwarding occurs within a session that has already been approved.

If a server may already have been compromised, Windisch's report says the fix alone is not enough. A stolen device key stays valid until the device is deleted at the control plane, because reimaging the machine does not revoke it. Tailscale's notices do not cover this case.

The picture is worse on headscale, the self-hosted control server. On headscale, SSH forwarding is granted on every accepted session and cannot be turned off in the policy, so Windisch's report says every SSH grant there should be treated as giving root. The newest headscale release, version 0.29.5 from October 13, does not fix the reported issues.

Windisch called the chain "wormable." In her tests, one compromised device could spread to every device the policy allowed, with no passwords, no stolen keys, and no user action, in under a second per step.

Two controls stopped the spread in those tests. Writing access rules as single device-to-device pairs held it back, and binding device keys to hardware such as a TPM kept a stolen identity from being reused elsewhere.

Tailscale rejected the worm description. The findings do not describe self-replicating malware that spreads between machines on its own, the company said, but lateral movement limited to the connections a policy file already allows.

It called the privilege-escalation problems "serious security issues," said it had reproduced the main ones, and added that it is still working out which configurations are affected.

As of publication, the 4 bulletins carry no CVE identifiers or severity scores, and none state the oldest affected version. The report also covered Tailscale's app connectors and its serve and funnel features.

Tailscale said the app-connector finding is in conn25, an experimental feature it has not released, and that the connectors customers use today are not affected. Of 5 serve and funnel findings, it fixed 3 and said the other 2 work as designed.

Windisch published the report without coordinating the release with Tailscale first, which she has described as "reported but not coordinated." Tailscale said it received the report on the morning of Saturday, October 24, about 2 hours before Windisch published it, and that its engineers were already looking at the same parts of the code.

Neither Tailscale nor the report says the flaws have been used in real attacks. The proof-of-concept code is not public, and Windisch said she gave it only to Tailscale's security team.

The report is the latest in a run of fixes in the same part of Tailscale this year. In June, Tailscale fixed a separate flaw in Tailscale SSH's Unix-socket forwarding that let an unprivileged user reach a privileged socket, in version 1.98.9.

ShinyHunters-Exploited Oracle PeopleSoft Zero-Day Turns Out to Be Three Chained Flaws

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The critical Oracle PeopleSoft bug that ShinyHunters has exploited since May is not one flaw but three chained together, the researchers who reported it said this week.

Oracle patched all three under a single identifier, CVE-2026-35273, in June. Its root is a server-side request forgery (SSRF) that reaches an internal management interface because PeopleSoft trusts its own loopback address by default, opening the door to remote code execution without a login.

The breakdown comes from Bobby Gould of the Zero Day Initiative (ZDI), whose team reported the flaw to Oracle.

In its analysis, ZDI said it reported three distinct vulnerabilities across two PeopleSoft components, and that it is working with Oracle to assign a separate CVE to each. As of this week, the one June identifier is still the only one on the record.

CVE-2026-35273 affects PeopleSoft Enterprise PeopleTools versions 8.61 and 8.62, carries a CVSS score of 9.8, and lets an unauthenticated attacker take over the server over the network. Oracle released its fix in an out-of-band security alert on June 10. The flaw is on CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog.

Attackers were already using it before that fix was in place. Mandiant and Google Threat Intelligence Group (GTIG) attribute a campaign to the group it tracks as UNC6240, better known as ShinyHunters, that exploited the flaw between May 27 and June 9, mostly against universities. Because that activity came before Oracle's June 10 advisory, it was a zero-day the whole time.

In a September follow-up, Mandiant reported a renewed, larger wave that spread beyond education to technology, IT services, healthcare, agriculture, transport, and government, affecting dozens of systems worldwide.

ZDI's analysis explains what that single patch actually closed. Two PeopleSoft components are involved, both run as separate web applications. The Integration Gateway (PSIGW) exchanges messages with outside systems. The Environment Management Hub (PSEMHUB) is an internal admin interface that is supposed to answer only to trusted hosts.

The first flaw is the SSRF in PSIGW. An attacker sends a request to its listening connector and controls both the address it forwards to and the full payload it sends, ZDI said; the handler does no check that the destination is a legitimate target. That alone would be limited, except for what it can reach.

PSEMHUB decides who to trust by checking the requester against a list in a file named allowedhosts.properties. That file ships with the local loopback address, 127.0.0.1, already on the list. So an attacker points the SSRF at 127.0.0.1, the request arrives looking like it came from the machine itself, and the trust check passes. The internal interface Oracle meant to wall off is now reachable from outside.

From there, ZDI described two ways to turn that access into code execution. One abuses a PSEMHUB command meant to write log entries: the caller controls both the file contents and the exact path on disk, so an attacker writes a web shell into the application directory and runs commands as the PeopleSoft service account.

The other writes a malicious XML file that PSEMHUB reads using Java's XMLDecoder when the service next starts, thereby running the attacker's code without planting a web shell at all. The second path leaves less for standard detection to catch.

The design decision at the center, ZDI said, is that allowing loopback access by default makes the trust check ineffective once the SSRF exists. That matters for defenders who thought a firewall rule had covered them.

In the September wave, Mandiant found ShinyHunters slipping past web application firewall (WAF) rules that blocked the /PSEMHUB/ path by url-encoding a single character, requesting /%50SEMHUB/ instead. Many WAF rules match the path's plain text, while the PeopleSoft server decodes it and serves the page.

The fix and the guidance here come from Oracle and Mandiant, not from ZDI's analysis, which covers the flaw rather than the response.

One thing the sources do not settle is whether applying the patch removes access an attacker already gained. Oracle's alert does not address it, and Mandiant's guidance to rotate credentials and hunt for web shells assumes it does not. Patching closes the door; it does not by itself evict someone already inside.

In October, WIRED met with Vos at his office at the Royal Courts of Justice, London. We discussed the ways that AI is spilling into the courts, the practical and ethical issues it raises, and how the justice system might adapt to serve a new generation raised alongside the technology.

This conversation has been edited for length and clarity.

WIRED: You’re just a few days away from retirement. How do you feel about leaving the post at this particular juncture, as AI begins to reshape how law is practiced and justice administered?

GEOFFREY VOS: When I started as a lawyer, we had foolscap paper, typists, and Tipp-Ex. We moved rapidly into word processors, faxes, and eventually computers.

Lawyers are conservative with a lowercase c. But that doesn’t mean that they should be backward-looking or retrogressive. It means they should be adopting change cautiously, with their eyes open.

The legal system is for the people of this country. You can’t be effective in providing access to justice if you’re not on the same technological wavelength. But it’s time to leave it to a new generation of leadership judges.

How have you built AI into your practice to date?

I don’t use it for writing judgments, but I use it for finding things out, navigating large quantities of material, and checking things. It saves me time. I have 11 meetings a day. I know there’s a document created years ago that’s relevant to my meeting—and Copilot finds it in seconds. I do that every day.

In law, isn’t the devil always in the details? Doesn’t confidence in the legal system depend on the understanding that a judge will review and consider every inch of every court submission?

I’m a little more cautious about using it to read papers for my cases, but that’s because I do the most important cases in the system.

People have confidence in our justice system because they believe that our judges have absolute integrity. In order not to lose that integrity, you have to explain how you’re adopting technology and achieve a broad consensus that it is appropriate.

How is AI spilling into the courts right now? Is it changing how litigants and legal professionals behave? What issues is it throwing up?

It’s changing everything remarkably quickly. We see a 10 percent rise in the number of county court cases started every year—and it looks as if this may increase. That’s because everybody who has a legal problem now puts it into Gemini, ChatGPT, or Claude. Ask it to create a particulars of claim, a case, a claim form—it can.

What proportion of these claims are based on sound legal principles, though? How often are people led by a chatbot to mistakenly believe they have a claim?

We don’t have the data. But I think it’s a mistake to assume that just because a claim is created by AI, it’s totally frivolous.

In a way it’s a good problem, because it shows there’s greater access to justice because of technology. One of the problems with justice in the past is that it’s only been available to rich people.

If people have a problem sufficient to bother ChatGPT, they probably genuinely think they have a claim. When you explore their problem in detail, you might find it is actually a bit different, but it may still be a legal problem and may still be reasonable for them to have wanted to bring it to attention.

People are not generally time wasters. They’re not generally trying to make trouble.

What about the opportunity for AI to fabricate evidence on which judges have previously been able to rely—images, video, voice recordings? Where does that leave the courts?

Often, judges have to decide whether a person is telling the truth. That is the most difficult thing to do, because humans are not bad at lying. But judges make these decisions every day. There’s absolutely no difference—in scale or qualitatively—in somebody producing a false image created by AI.

The only reason we’re all getting so excited about deepfakes is because they are so new. But actually, that is the stuff of judging.

But we’re talking about a whole category of evidence that’s now somewhat compromised. Doesn’t that affect a judge’s ability to decide whether somebody is lying?

It was always thus. We always had to decide whether wills were forged, because people used to do that. It’s exactly the same with voice recordings, and video. It’s just an extension of the type of false information that is produced to the court.

People will find out when they produce fake images to the court that they are committing perjury and they will go to prison, just as if they’d forged a will.

I think we need to be cautious about predicting disaster. I’m very optimistic.

You’ve previously described an “inevitable” future where simpler civil cases are adjudicated entirely by AI. Why are you confident that’s where we’re headed?

You need to find a way to deliver justice that is within the cultural grain. That doesn’t mean you just conduct all your cases on TikTok. But if you don’t understand that people are asking LLMs for the answer to almost every question under the sun, including legal questions, you’re likely to get it wrong when you try to devise a process fit for the 21st century.

It’s all about consensus. It will only be possible to ask people to accept algorithmic decision-making if they consent to the process in a particular case, or if society decides through legislation.

When people go to court and get an adverse outcome, even if they regard the outcome as idiosyncratic or even wrong, when it’s delivered in a human way, it can be accepted over time. With AI, we have to be careful; it may be less easy for people to accept an adverse machine-driven decision.

I very much want to see a very open, international discussion about how we should resolve disputes.

I suspect part of the reason people object to trial by AI is because we can’t know precisely how a model has arrived at a decision. What’s to be done about this problem of explainability?

I don’t think it’s about reasoning; it’s about outcomes. The AI can produce very plausible reasoning. You can’t see how it produced that reasoning, but you can see the reasoning it produced, and you can judge the plausibility.

I don’t think you’ll ever do away with human judges. There’s something almost biblical about the human judge; it’s in the human psyche to want to be judged by their fellow man. But that does not mean that in some situations people wouldn’t rather have a quick outcome—even if it’s not a human outcome.

I don’t think judges are yet in the position to say. It’s a question for society, politicians, and legislation to resolve. That debate is really important.

I think that custody and family cases are one of the areas where essentially human judgment is expected, even by the younger generation. In complex commercial cases, I think that’s also the case. But I think we will whittle away at how commercial cases are decided by using AI to collate information to make the decision-making process better and easier.

We will allow more people access to justice more cheaply and more quickly using technology. That will be an overall good for the economy and for the people we serve.

GoAhead TLS Flaws Allow Revoked Client Certificates and Enable Session Decryption

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The GoAhead embedded web server loaded a certificate revocation list into its default TLS backend but never turned revocation checking on, so a revoked client certificate could still pass login on affected setups.

The flaw is in builds that use the OpenSSL backend with mutual-TLS, where the server checks a client certificate and vice versa, and a revocation list is configured to block revoked certificates.

That is a setup an operator turns on deliberately; a default build does not. GoAhead 6.1.1, released to device makers on August 2, 2026, and publicly published on October 12, fixes it.

GoAhead is a small web server that device makers build into firmware to run management pages on routers, printers, gateways and industrial controllers. Its maker, EmbedThis, says it runs in hundreds of millions of devices.

The flaw was found by Sébastien Féry of FoxIO, who described it and two other TLS problems in a write-up on October 26.

On an affected build, the server loads the revocation list file at startup and reports no error, so an operator who configured it has every reason to think revocation is working. It is not. A client whose certificate was revoked, for example a stolen or retired device, completes the handshake as if the certificate were still valid and gets whatever access that identity allows.

In FoxIO's test against GoAhead 6.1.0 with OpenSSL 3.6.2, a revoked certificate was accepted with issuer checking switched on.

The OpenSSL advisory describes revoked client certificates as silently accepted. To be exploited, the build must use the OpenSSL backend with client certificates required, a trusted certificate authority bundle and issuer checking enabled, and a revocation list configured; the attacker must hold a certificate signed by a trusted authority and listed as revoked. Builds that use the Mbed TLS backend are unaffected; that backend checks revocation via a separate code path.

The identifier on the advisory is CVE-2026-115881, rated 9.1 by EmbedThis. As of October 27, that identifier and the others below were not yet live in the public CVE and NVD databases; EmbedThis says it has requested them. The severity ratings are the vendor's own.

The Mbed TLS problem only reaches a session if the client accepts the broken setup. OpenSSL, current Go, current Java, and mainstream browsers refuse a value that small and fall back to a stronger key exchange, so the session never completes.

Clients without a minimum-size check, such as some embedded or custom ones, accept it, and then anyone who recorded the traffic can work out the key and read the session. TLS 1.3 and ECDHE sessions are unaffected, and Mbed TLS 4.x removed the key exchange they rely on.

Which backend and settings a build uses are chosen when the firmware is compiled, so a device owner cannot tell from the outside which apply. That is also why the fix is slow to arrive: GoAhead 6.1.1 is a drop-in replacement that device makers rebuild and ship as a firmware update, and an owner gets it only when their manufacturer does so. Whether any manufacturer has shipped it is not known.

As of October 27, no source reported any of these problems being used in attacks. EmbedThis's policy is to end a disclosure embargo and publish early if a flaw is being exploited or disclosed by someone else, so the coordinated October 12 release is consistent with no known attacks.

However, it does not prove there are none. EmbedThis notifies customers of security updates through its Builder portal. FoxIO has published proof-of-concept code and offers fingerprints through its JA4Scan tool to find GoAhead servers by backend. The tool is free, but its source is available only on request, and a fingerprint identifies a build, not a confirmed-vulnerable server.

None of the device makers EmbedThis lists as GoAhead users, which include HP, Oracle, Siemens, Canon, Rockwell, D-Link and Comcast, has been shown to run an affected backend and setting. The affected population is not established.

The same 6.1.1 release closes 11 advisories in all, three of them rated critical. One is a login bypass: on a build using HTTP Basic or form login, an attacker who knows a valid username can sign in with any password if that user's stored password is in the newer Blowfish format, which GoAhead added in version 6.1.0. EmbedThis says form login is the default for its built-in administration pages.

After upgrading, EmbedThis recommends ending active sessions, restarting the server, and changing Blowfish-stored passwords.
